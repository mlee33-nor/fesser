# The Nature of God, Part I: Background Principles and the First Divine Attributes

*A study guide to Edward Feser, "Five Proofs of the Existence of God" (Ignatius Press, 2017), chapter 6, "The Nature of God and of His Relationship to the World," first half (printed pages 169–205).*

## 1. Orientation

Feser's book defends five classical arguments for the existence of God. Chapter 1 argues from change to a purely actual "Unmoved Mover." Chapter 2 argues from composite things to an absolutely simple "One." Chapter 3 argues from abstract objects such as numbers, propositions, universals, and possible worlds to a divine intellect in which they exist as ideas. Chapter 4 argues from the distinction between what a thing is (its essence) and the fact that it is (its existence) to a cause that is "subsistent existence itself." Chapter 5 argues from the principle of sufficient reason to an absolutely necessary being.

Chapter 6 turns from *whether* God exists to *what God is* and *how God relates to the world*. This study guide covers the first half of that chapter. It has two movements. First, Feser sets out three general principles of reasoning that let us move from the world, considered as an effect, to the nature of its cause: the principle of proportionate causality, the principle that "action follows being," and the analogy of being. Second, he uses those principles, together with the conclusions of chapters 1–5, to derive the first group of divine attributes: unity, simplicity, immutability, immateriality and incorporeality, eternity, and necessity. Along the way he answers critics from Cottingham and Plantinga to Swinburne and Schrader. The second half of the chapter (omnipotence, omniscience, goodness, will, love, incomprehensibility, and God's ongoing causal relation to the world) is covered in a separate guide.

## 2. Why this chapter matters

Suppose someone accepts that the world depends on some first cause. Two questions remain, and a skeptic is right to ask them. First, why think the five arguments reach one being rather than five? Second, why call that cause "God" at all, rather than an impersonal force, a committee of deities, or a powerful alien?

This chapter answers both. Its central idea is that the proofs do not conclude to just any cause. They conclude to a cause that is *purely actual*, with no unrealized potential of any kind. From that single feature, and from the related claim that the cause has no parts, Feser derives the classical attributes in a tightly linked chain. Once the chain is visible, the attributes stop looking like a pious wish list and look instead like one reality described from several angles.

Many popular objections are really objections to the *nature* of the God concluded to: "Why only one God?" "How can a changeless God do anything?" "Isn't your God just a bigger version of us?" "Can't I imagine a world with nothing in it?" Each gets a direct answer here. The chapter also defines *classical theism*, whose heart is divine simplicity, the doctrine that God has no parts of any kind, and argues that the modern alternative quietly turns God into a creature.

## 3. Key concepts and vocabulary

**Actuality and potentiality.** Actuality is the way a thing actually is. Potentiality is a real capacity to be otherwise. A cold cup of coffee is actually cool and potentially hot. Change is the actualization of a potential. (This comes from chapter 1.)

**Principle of causality (PC).** A potential can be actualized only by something already actual. Nothing actualizes itself out of nothing.

**Principle of sufficient reason (PSR).** Every being and every attribute has an explanation, either in itself or in something else.

**Principle of proportionate causality (PPC).** Whatever is in an effect must be present, in some way, in its *total* cause. A cause cannot give what it does not in some way have.

**Formally, virtually, eminently.** These are the three ways an effect's features can be present in a cause. *Formally* means the cause has the very same feature (a hot stove heating a pan). *Virtually* means the cause has the feature's content in a different mode, as a resource or power (money in a bank account that can be withdrawn as cash). *Eminently* means the cause has something more fundamental than the feature, such as the power to produce things of that kind (control over a mint).

**Agere sequitur esse.** Latin for "action follows being." What a thing does flows from, and cannot exceed, what it is. A stone cannot grow or reproduce on its own; anything that could would not be a stone.

**Efficient cause and formal cause.** An efficient cause brings about the existence of something or a change in it (the sculptor). A formal cause is a thing's nature, what makes it the kind of thing it is (for a human being, rational animality). PPC is about efficient causes; *agere sequitur esse* is its counterpart for formal causes.

**Univocal, equivocal, analogical.** A term is used *univocally* when it has exactly the same sense in two uses ("Fido is a dog," "Rover is a dog"). It is used *equivocally* when the senses are completely unrelated (a "bat" in the attic versus a baseball "bat"). It is used *analogically* when the senses are related without being identical ("good wine," "a good man").

**Analogy of attribution.** A term applies intrinsically to one "primary analogate" and to "secondary analogates" only because of their relation to it. George is healthy. His food is "healthy" because it causes health. His complexion is "healthy" because it shows health.

**Analogy of proper proportionality.** A term applies intrinsically and literally to each analogate, but in proportionally different ways. Plants, animals, humans, and angels are all really alive, each in its own way.

**Analogy of improper (metaphorical) proportionality.** A term applies literally to one analogate and only figuratively to another. The animal at the zoo is a lion. Brave George "is a lion" only in a figurative sense, because of his courage. Metaphor is one kind of analogy, but most analogy is not metaphor.

**Genus and specific difference.** A genus is a general kind (animal). A specific difference is the added feature that marks off a species within it (rational, which marks off humans). The difference lies outside the genus: you can understand "rational" without understanding "animal."

**Divine simplicity.** God has no parts of any kind: no matter and form, no genus and difference, no essence distinct from existence, no mix of act and potency, no really distinct attributes.

**Theistic personalism, or "neo-theism."** The modern view that treats God as the unique member of the kind "person," without our limitations, and not simple.

**Essence, property, accident, relation.** Aquinas distinguishes these. A thing's essence is what it is (rational animality). A "property" in the strict sense is a feature that flows from the essence (the capacity to find things funny). A contingent accident is an intrinsic feature that does not flow from the essence (skin color). A relation is a matter of how a thing stands to something else (being Plato's teacher).

**Constituent ontology.** The view, named by the philosopher Nicholas Wolterstorff, that a thing's essence and attributes are concrete ingredients of the thing itself, not abstract Platonic entities it "participates in."

**Cambridge change and Cambridge property.** A change in what is true of something that involves no real change in the thing itself, only in something related to it. The term comes from Peter Geach. When Plato grows taller, Socrates becomes "shorter than Plato" without changing at all.

**Fallacy of accident.** Treating a feature that merely happens to go along with things of a certain kind as if it belonged to the essence of that kind.

## 4. The argument as a story

### Setting the stage

Picture the five proofs as five climbers reaching a summit by different routes. Each describes the summit a little differently: purely actual, absolutely simple, the home of necessary truths, existence itself, necessary. Are they standing in the same place, and what is it like? Before answering, Feser lays out his tools.

### Tool one: a cause cannot give what it lacks

The principle of proportionate causality says that whatever is in an effect must be in its total cause somehow, and the word "somehow" does a lot of work. Feser explains it with money. Suppose you end up with twenty dollars from me. If I hand you a bill from my wallet, the effect was in me *formally*: I had exactly what you now have. If I have no cash but wire twenty dollars from my bank account for you to withdraw, the effect was in the total cause (me plus my account) *virtually*. If I have neither cash nor savings but can get a genuine bill printed for you on the government's press, the effect was in the total cause *eminently*. I had something more basic than a bill, namely the power to make bills.

Why believe the principle? Suppose some feature of an effect came from nothing in its total cause. Then a potential would have been actualized by nothing, which violates the principle of causality. And the feature would have no explanation, which violates the principle of sufficient reason.

John Cottingham objects that the principle assumes an "heirloom" picture of causation, with properties handed down like family jewelry, and that nature refutes it. Helium has properties the hydrogen it came from lacked, and a sponge cake is spongy though eggs, flour, and butter are not. Feser answers in three steps. First, Cottingham looks only at the ingredients, while the principle concerns the *total* cause, which includes the baker, the mixing, and the oven's heat. Shrinking the principle down to ingredients and then refuting it is attacking a straw man. Second, the heirloom picture assumes that the cause must have the feature formally, which defenders of the principle never claimed. As one Thomist writer quipped, a boy doesn't need a black eye to give one. Aquinas makes the same point about heat: a hot thing doesn't hand over its own heat but, by its power, brings about a new heat that the heated thing had only potentially. Third, Cottingham says that calling the sponginess "potentially present" makes the principle trivial. Feser accepts the word "potentially," since the bank account's potential to be drawn on is exactly what explains your twenty dollars. Explanations in terms of potentials may tell us only a little, but they are not empty.

In fact, naturalists rely on the principle. Paul Churchland argues that since humans arise from purely material beginnings by purely material processes, humans are purely material. That inference works only if a wholly material total cause can produce only a wholly material effect. A dualist accepts the same principle and runs it in reverse, starting from the immaterial intellect.

Evolution is not a counterexample either. Even on the standard naturalistic account, a new variation arises when existing genetic material, a mutation (from a DNA copying error, radiation, or chemical damage), and environmental circumstances combine. None of these alone would be enough, and together they are the total cause. Feser also cites the physicist Paul Davies, who points out that denying that new biological information comes from some combination of earlier factors, including the environment, would clash with the second law of thermodynamics. According to that law, order and information tend to decrease in a closed system.

### Tool two: action follows being

The second principle, *agere sequitur esse*, says that what a thing does reflects what it is. Eyes and ears work differently because they are built differently. Plants feed and grow while stones do not, because plants are alive. This is the counterpart of proportionate causality for natures: an effect cannot outrun its efficient cause, and a thing's activities cannot outrun its nature. It too follows from the principle of sufficient reason, since a stone that began using language on its own would be doing something with no explanation. There is one qualification. A thing can exceed its nature when something else makes it do so, as a puppet's limbs dance when the puppeteer moves them. What the principle rules out is a thing doing so by itself.

This principle is not a medieval relic either. Aquinas used it to argue that the soul survives death. Thinking is immaterial, so its subject must be immaterial, and immaterial things have no natural tendency to decay. The naturalist John Searle uses the same principle against "property dualism," the view that immaterial mental properties belong to a material brain. If such a property is really over and above the brain, it is a separate thing rather than a property of the brain; if it is a property of the brain, it isn't over and above it.

### Tool three: the analogy of being

The third tool concerns language. When we say that God "exists," "causes," "knows," or has "power," what do our words mean?

"Dog" is used univocally of Fido and Rover. "Bat" is used equivocally of the flying mammal and the baseball club. "Good" is used analogically of wine and of a man. Even *being* is analogical. A stone and its color are both real, but not in the same way, since the color exists only in the stone and depends on it. Yet the color is not simply unreal.

There are several kinds of analogy. In the *analogy of attribution*, health is really in George, and his food and complexion are called healthy only because of how they relate to his health: the food causes it and the complexion shows it. In *proper proportionality*, life is literally present in plants, animals, humans, and angels, each in its own way. In *metaphorical proportionality*, lionhood is literally in the zoo animal and only figuratively in brave George. "Being" is said of substances, attributes, creatures, and God by proper proportionality.

Why isn't "being" just a very broad kind, like "animal"? "Animal" applies in exactly the same way to fish, birds, and reptiles, and what distinguishes them is a specific difference that lies outside the genus. You can understand "rational" without understanding "animal." But you cannot understand "substance" or "attribute" without already understanding them as ways of being. And the only thing outside being is nothing, which cannot distinguish anything. So "being" is applied by *proportional similarity*. Compare "I see the tree" and "I see that the Pythagorean theorem is true." The eyes are to the tree as the intellect is to the theorem. In the same way, a man's existence is to his essence as an angel's existence is to its essence, and as God's existence is to his. The relation is different in each case. In God, existence and essence are identical. In an angel, existence actualizes the essence of a wholly immaterial being. In a man, it actualizes the essence of a being with both bodily and intellectual operations. (Feser adds that "being" is also said by the analogy of attribution, with God as the primary case and creatures counting as beings because of their relation to him as their cause.)

Feser then argues that "being" must be analogical, using only the distinction between actuality and potentiality. Both are real, and they are really distinct. If potentiality were real in exactly the same sense as actuality, they would not be distinct. If it were real in a totally unrelated sense, it would not be real at all. So only analogy remains. Denying that potentiality is real is Parmenides' denial of change. Denying that actuality is real is the Heraclitean view, on a traditional reading, that only flux exists. That view undermines itself in several ways. There would be no lasting self to reason from premises to conclusion, and no stable argument to repeat. Change is always change *toward* something: a melting ice cube becomes liquid and flat. And a thing changes the way it does because of what it actually is: ice melts because it is water, not wood or glass. Denying that actuality and potentiality are really distinct amounts to saying that only one of them is real. And chapter 4's real distinction between essence and existence is already a case of real potency and act.

This matters because the proofs lead to something radically unlike anything we experience. Words shaped by changeable, composite, material things cannot apply to it in exactly the same sense. With only univocal language, we could say only what God is *not*, and then we could not even say that God exists or causes the world. Negative theology is part of the truth, not all of it. Equivocal language fails too. Try completing the sentence "God has 'power' in the sense that he ___." If the word in the blank is used univocally, the first problem returns. If it is used equivocally, things get worse. We would end in agnosticism, or even atheism. Analogy is the way out, and it was not invented for theology. We already needed it to describe potentiality, and physics needs it to describe things that behave as both waves and particles, or space that is itself curved. We should expect at least as much stretching when we describe the cause of there being any physical world at all.

### Unity: why there is only one God

Now the attributes, beginning with unity: there is, and in principle could be, only one God.

A critic might try a loophole. Grant that the First Cause's *existence* involves no actualization of potential, but suppose its *activity* does. Feser gives two replies. First, action follows being. If a thing exists in a purely actual way, there is nothing in its nature from which merely potential activity could come. Second, such a being would be composite, with an actual part and a potential part. Chapter 2 showed that any composite needs an outside cause to explain why its parts are combined. That holds even if the composite always existed, because the question is why the parts are combined *at all*, not how long they have been combined. A first cause with a cause is not first. So God is purely actual in his activity as well as his existence.

Unity follows. How can there be more than one thing of a kind? Only if there is a distinction between the individual and its kind, or between a species and its genus. Human beings are rational animals. Rationality must be added to animality to make a human, and each individual also has matter that distinguishes one human from another. Each of these parts brings potentiality with it. The matter could have been part of something else, and "animal" is, of itself, open to being rational or not. In general, parts only potentially make up a whole until their union is actualized. So being one of many requires parts, parts require potentiality, and God has none. There couldn't even possibly be a second God. It doesn't help to appeal to some other sort of part, such as an accident one god has and the other lacks, because any composition brings potentiality.

### Five routes, one summit

Feser shows that the proofs' key descriptions entail each other.

- Pure actuality and simplicity go together. Whatever has parts mixes actuality with potentiality, so the purely actual is simple. And something simple must be actual (or it wouldn't exist) and cannot have any potential (or it would have an actual part and a potential part).
- Pure actuality and subsistent existence go together. Where essence and existence differ, they are related as potency to act, so what is purely actual cannot have an essence distinct from its existence. And what just *is* existence cannot depend on some potential being actualized, or its existence would be borrowed.
- Simplicity and subsistent existence go together directly. A simple thing cannot have essence and existence as distinct parts, and existence itself can have no other parts either.
- All of these go together with necessity. A contingent being needs some potential actualized and some parts combined in order to exist. A necessary being can have neither.

So the five proofs are five paths to one God, not arguments for five gods.

### Simplicity: the keystone

Feser insists that unity and simplicity cannot be separated. If God had a genus and a specific difference, or an essence distinct from his existence, there could in principle be more than one God. And if God had any parts at all, he would need a cause like everything else.

So denying simplicity implicitly denies God's uniqueness and his ultimacy. Feser's image: Zeus and Odin would have exactly the same natures even if each became the only resident of Olympus or Asgard. A God who belongs to a kind is at most the sole surviving member of a pantheon. A God with parts that need combining is a creature, even if the one creature that happens to lack a cause. Insisting that such a God is immaterial doesn't fix this. It just gives us a kind of superangel, and worshiping it would be idolatry. Denying that anything at all is simple amounts to atheism.

That is why the mainstream of Western philosophical theology held simplicity to be nonnegotiable: pagans like Aristotle and Plotinus, the Jewish thinker Maimonides, Muslims like Avicenna and Averroes, and Christians such as Athanasius, Augustine, Anselm, and Aquinas. The Catholic Church taught it at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 and the First Vatican Council in 1869–1870, and Luther and Calvin affirmed it. Simplicity is the core of classical theism.

Some modern theistic philosophers reject it. Norman Geisler calls them "neo-theists," and Brian Davies calls them "theistic personalists," a group that includes Alvin Plantinga, Richard Swinburne, and Charles Hartshorne. They treat God as the unique member of the kind "person," without the limits of humans and angels. The dispute is not about whether God is personal, since classical theists attribute intellect and will to God. It is about whether being personal places God within a kind, making him composite. Feser's replies to Plantinga and Morris appear in section 6.

### Immutability, immateriality, eternity, necessity

**Immutability.** Change is the actualization of potential, and God has none, so God cannot change. Doesn't creating mean going from not creating to creating? No: becoming Creator is a Cambridge change. When Plato grows, Socrates becomes shorter than Plato without anything happening to Socrates; when the world begins, God becomes Creator while the real change is in the world. Immutability also follows from simplicity, since real change means changing in one respect while staying the same in others, which requires parts.

**Immateriality and incorporeality.** On the Aristotelian view, every material thing is composed of substantial form and prime matter, and matter is pure potential for form, so a simple, purely actual being cannot be material. But you don't need Aristotle. On anyone's view, material things have parts that can be rearranged, from a chair's pieces down to atoms and subatomic particles, and that means potentiality. Even fundamental particles (fermions and bosons) have distinct attributes and come into being and pass away.

**Eternity.** Whatever begins to exist has parts that need to be combined, above all essence and existence, and so is contingent and needs a cause. God has no parts, is necessary, and just is existence, so he not only needs no cause but couldn't have one. Whatever ceases to exist has parts that can come apart and is potentially nonexistent, and existence itself can hardly lose existence. What neither begins nor ends is eternal.

But God's eternity is timelessness, not a very long life. A God in time would keep adding days to his life, act differently at different moments, and keep learning new facts like "it is now noon." All of that is change, and a life in time has distinct stages, which are parts. Feser notes that William Lane Craig, who rejects both immutability and timelessness, agrees that an unchanging God would have to be timeless.

**Necessity.** The rationalist proof argued directly for a necessary being, and the other proofs imply one. If God were contingent, some potential would need to be actualized for him to exist, and he would have parts, at least an essence distinct from his existence, that needed combining. God has neither. So he exists necessarily. Feser defends this against two objections, covered in section 6.

## 5. The formal line of reasoning, step by step

This chapter has no single formal proof. The steps below restate its main line of reasoning in order. Steps 6 through 12 paraphrase Feser's one explicitly numbered argument, which shows that "being" is analogical.

**Stage 1: Starting points and tools**

1. The five proofs have established a cause that is purely actual, absolutely simple, a necessarily existing intellect, subsistent existence itself, and absolutely necessary. *These are the conclusions of chapters 1–5.*
2. Whatever is in an effect is in its total cause formally, virtually, or eminently. *Illustrated by the three ways of giving someone twenty dollars.*
3. This principle follows from the principles of causality and sufficient reason. *Otherwise some feature would come from nothing and have no explanation.*
4. A thing's activities cannot exceed its nature on its own (action follows being). *Also required by the principle of sufficient reason.*
5. Terms can be used univocally, equivocally, or analogically, and the analogies of attribution and proper proportionality are literal. *As with "dog," "bat," and "good."*

**Stage 2: Being is analogical**

6. Actuality is real. *Denying it (Heraclitus) undermines reasoning itself.*
7. Potentiality is real. *Denying it (Parmenides) means denying change.*
8. Potentiality is really distinct from actuality. *Essence and existence are one example, and a merely verbal distinction would collapse into step 6 or 7 being false.*
9. If potentiality had being in the same sense as actuality, they would not be distinct. *One would just be a kind of the other.*
10. If potentiality had being only in an unrelated sense, it would not have being at all. *Being real in a completely different sense just means being unreal.*
11. The three uses exhaust the literal options, so only analogy remains. *Analogy is the middle ground.*
12. So potentiality has being in a sense analogous to that of actuality, and "being" is analogical. *This follows from steps 6 through 11, with no appeal to God.*
13. So positive, literal talk about a God radically unlike creatures must be analogical. *Univocal language would leave only negations, and equivocal language would leave empty words.*

**Stage 3: Pure actuality without qualification**

14. The First Cause's existence involves no actualization of potential. *That is how it ends the regress.*
15. By the principle that action follows being, its activity cannot involve potential either. *There is nothing in its nature for such potential to come from.*
16. A being with actual existence and potential activity would be composite and would need an outside cause, even if it had always existed. *Composites need something to unite their parts.*
17. So God is purely actual without qualification. *This follows from steps 14 through 16.*

**Stage 4: Unity and convergence**

18. More than one thing of a kind requires a distinction between individual and species, or between species and genus. *Two humans differ by their matter; humans differ from other animals by rationality.*
19. Such parts, and parts of any kind, bring potentiality. *Matter could be otherwise, and a genus is open to different species.*
20. A purely actual being has no such parts. *This follows from steps 17 and 19.*
21. So God is unique, and it is not even possible for there to be a second God. *This follows from steps 18 and 20.*
22. Pure actuality and simplicity entail each other. *So the Unmoved Mover is the One.*
23. Pure actuality and subsistent existence entail each other. *So the Unmoved Mover is existence itself.*
24. Simplicity and subsistent existence entail each other. *So the One is existence itself.*
25. All of these entail, and are entailed by, absolute necessity. *Contingency requires potential and parts.*
26. So all five proofs reach one and the same God. *This follows from steps 21 through 25.*

**Stage 5: Simplicity**

27. Any parts in God would make a second God possible and would mean God needs a cause. *See steps 16 and 18–19.*
28. So denying simplicity turns God into a member of a pantheon or a creature. *Zeus alone on Olympus is still just a god.*
29. Simplicity identifies God's real attributes, not his mere Cambridge properties such as being Creator. *Becoming Creator is a change in the world, not in God.*
30. So God is absolutely simple, and the standard objections rest on assumptions the classical view rejects. *These are univocal predication, Platonic properties, and a flattened idea of "property."*

**Stage 6: Immutability**

31. Change is the actualization of potential. *This is from chapter 1.*
32. So a purely actual being cannot really change. *It has no potential to actualize.*
33. A simple being cannot change either. *Real change requires parts, some that stay and some that go.*
34. God's becoming Creator is a Cambridge change. *The real change is in the world.*
35. Acting and living do not essentially require changing. *Assuming they do commits the fallacy of accident.*
36. So God is unchanging, yet he truly acts and truly lives. *This follows from steps 32 through 35.*

**Stage 7: Immateriality**

37. On the Aristotelian view, material things are composites of form and matter, and matter is potentiality. *This is hylomorphism.*
38. On any view, material things have rearrangeable parts, and even fundamental particles have distinct attributes and come and go. *This is the picture from physics.*
39. So anything material is composite and potential. *This follows from step 37 or step 38.*
40. So God is immaterial and incorporeal. *This follows from pure actuality and simplicity.*

**Stage 8: Eternity**

41. Whatever begins has parts to combine, is potential, is contingent, and needs a cause. *From earlier chapters.*
42. So God never began, and he could not have. *He has no parts or potential and is existence itself.*
43. Whatever ends has separable parts, is potentially nonexistent, and is contingent, so God cannot end. *This is the mirror image of step 42.*
44. So God is eternal in the sense of having no beginning or end. *This follows from steps 42 and 43.*
45. A God in time would change: new moments, different acts, new knowledge. *That contradicts immutability.*
46. A God in time would have distinct stages, which are parts. *That contradicts simplicity.*
47. So God is strictly timeless and causes the whole temporal world in a single act. *"God causes X at time 1" does not mean "at time 1, God causes X."*

**Stage 9: Necessity**

48. A contingent God would need potential actualized and parts combined. *That is what contingency involves.*
49. God has neither, so he is not contingent. *He is purely actual, simple, and existence itself.*
50. What is possible or necessary for a thing is known from its actual nature, not from imagined worlds. *This is the Aristotelian-Thomistic method.*
51. So God exists necessarily, in every possible world, and this necessity is real, not a feature of language. *This follows from steps 49 and 50.*

## 6. Every objection and the reply

**1. The "heirloom" objection (Cottingham).** Helium and sponge cake show that effects contain what their causes lack. *Reply:* The examples look only at the materials, not the total cause, so they attack a straw man. They also assume that the feature must be in the cause formally, but a boy can give a black eye without having one.

**2. "Potentially present" makes the principle trivial (Cottingham).** *Reply:* An explanation in terms of potentials may be minimally informative, but it is not empty. Churchland and his dualist opponents both use the principle to reach substantive conclusions.

**3. Evolution gets the complex from the simple.** *Reply:* Genes, mutation, and environment together are the total cause, even on the naturalistic account. Denying this would also clash with the second law of thermodynamics, as Paul Davies notes.

**4. These principles are medieval relics.** *Reply:* Both follow from the principles of causality and sufficient reason. Churchland relies on proportionate causality, and Searle relies on "action follows being."

**5. Deny actuality (Heraclitus) or potentiality (Parmenides).** *Reply:* Denying potentiality means denying change. Pure flux leaves no lasting reasoner and no stable argument, cannot explain why change heads toward new states, and ignores that things change according to what they are.

**6. The act/potency distinction is only verbal.** *Reply:* Essence and existence give one real case. And a merely verbal distinction would leave only one of the two real.

**7. God-talk is either anthropomorphic or empty.** *Reply:* Univocal talk would be anthropomorphic, pure negation cannot even say that God exists, and equivocal talk would be empty. Analogy is a third, literal option.

**8. Analogy is special pleading.** *Reply:* Analogy is independently required by the act/potency distinction, by ordinary speech ("seeing" a proof), and by physics (curved space, wave-particle duality).

**9. The First Cause could have potential in its activity.** *Reply:* Action follows being. And a mix of actual and potential would be composite, needing a cause that is prior in being, even if not in time.

**10. Why not two gods?** *Reply:* Being one of many requires parts, whether matter, a genus and a difference, or substance and accidents. Parts bring potential, and God has none.

**11. Theistic personalism.** A personal God need not be simple. *Reply:* Classical theists also hold that God is personal. But a God within a kind would be unique only by chance, like Zeus alone on Olympus, and a God with parts would need a cause. None of the proofs supports such a superangel, so denying simplicity amounts to atheism.

**12. Plantinga.** Simplicity makes God a single property, and so an abstract object. *Reply:* Plantinga assumes univocal predication, Platonic properties, and that anything we can say of a thing names a property. Classical theists reject all three. God's power and knowledge are analogous to ours, so their distinctness in us does not transfer to God. A thing's attributes are concrete constituents of it. Essence, properties, accidents, and relations differ. And no classical theist says God is a property. When Plantinga replies that these are all "characteristics" anyway, he misses that the distinctions are metaphysical, not verbal.

**13. Morris (see also Hughes).** Necessary and contingent properties can't be identical. *Reply:* Following Geach and Miller, simplicity identifies only God's real properties. Being Creator is a Cambridge property, like Socrates becoming shorter than Plato, so it need not be identical with omnipotence.

**14. To act is to change.** *Reply:* This is the fallacy of accident, like concluding that no professor could be over nine feet tall. As Davies notes, acting essentially means producing an effect in another, and teaching essentially means causing someone to learn.

**15. An unchanging God can't be alive.** *Reply:* This is the same fallacy. Life is activity from an internal principle, and God, who is acted on by nothing, is alive in the fullest sense.

**16. Causing at different times means acting in time (Swinburne, Pike).** *Reply:* "God causes X at time 1" does not mean "at time 1, God causes X." One timeless act produces the whole of time, as an author might conceive a whole story in a single flash.

**17. Simultaneity (Swinburne, Kenny).** If Socrates and Obama are each simultaneous with eternity, they are simultaneous with each other. *Reply:* The objection treats eternity as a point in time.

**18. Necessity is linguistic convention (Findlay, Russell).** *Reply:* Sentences are not propositions. "Snow is white" and "Schnee ist weiss" express one truth, which would hold even without language. Mathematical necessity resists conventionalism. And the objection begs the question against the proofs' account of God's necessity.

**19. Schrader's empty world.** Imagine a marble, then nothing, not even God. *Reply:* God, like numbers, can't be pictured; erasing "2 + 2 = 4" from an imagined chalkboard proves nothing. And what is necessary is learned from the essences of actual things, not from imagined worlds. Schrader simply assumes that the proofs fail.

## 7. Common misunderstandings

- **"A cause must resemble its effect."** No. The effect's features can be in the cause virtually or eminently. God has creaturely perfections eminently, the way someone who controls the mint "has" money.
- **"Analogical means metaphorical."** No. Attribution and proper proportionality are literal. God really exists and really knows.
- **"Simplicity means God has one attribute, and all our words for him mean the same thing."** No. We have many true concepts of God, drawn from many kinds of effects in the world, and all of them apply to one undivided reality.
- **"Simplicity makes God an abstract object."** That follows only if one assumes Platonic properties. For Aquinas, God is not a property at all. He is subsistent existence itself.
- **"Classical theists think God is impersonal."** No. They attribute intellect and will to God. The dispute is about whether God is composite.
- **"An unchanging God is inert and lifeless."** No. God is fully actual, which is the opposite of inert, and he acts without depending on anything.
- **"Eternal means very, very old."** No. God is timeless, not merely everlasting.
- **"Becoming Creator changed God."** No. It is a Cambridge change. The real change is in the world.
- **"The five proofs might reach five different ultimate beings."** No. Their conclusions entail each other, and pure actuality is necessarily unique.
- **"Divine necessity is an ontological-argument trick."** No. It is derived from what the proofs establish about the world's cause, not from a definition.

## 8. How to explain it to others

**The simple version.** "The proofs show that the ultimate cause of things has no unrealized potential. Anything with parts has potential, because the parts could come apart or be arranged differently, so this cause has no parts. Without parts there is nothing that could make two of it, so there's only one. Changing means realizing a potential, so it can't change. Matter is full of parts, so it isn't material. Being in time means changing, so it's outside time. And something whose very nature is to exist can't fail to exist. When we say it 'knows' or is 'powerful,' we mean something real, just as we really 'see' the answer to a math problem, though not with our eyes."

**The best analogy: the author and the story.** An author who conceives a whole novel in one flash of insight stands outside the story's timeline. He isn't changed when a character is born or dies. He is the source of everything in the book without being one of its characters. He has every character's traits in his imagination "eminently," without himself being a pirate or a queen. This illustrates timeless causation and Cambridge change (a character's birth changes the story, not the author). Theistic personalism, in effect, makes God the novel's hero instead of its author. Be honest about the limits: real authors are composite and think in time.
## 9. Key takeaways and review questions

**Key takeaways**

- Three tools make the move from world to God possible: proportionate causality (about the total cause; formally, virtually, or eminently), "action follows being," and the analogy of being.
- "Being" is analogical, as the act/potency distinction alone shows, and analogy makes literal, positive talk about God possible.
- God is purely actual even in his activity, so he is one. Pure actuality, simplicity, subsistent existence, and necessity entail each other, so the five proofs reach one God.
- Simplicity is the keystone of classical theism. Deny it and God becomes a member of a kind or a creature. Plantinga's and Morris's objections rest on univocity, Platonic properties, and ignoring Cambridge properties.
- God is unchanging yet acts and lives, he is immaterial on any theory of matter, and he is timeless rather than merely everlasting.
- God exists necessarily, and neither linguistic conventionalism nor imagined empty worlds show otherwise.

**Ten review questions with brief answers**

1. *What are the three ways an effect can be in its cause?* Formally (a bill handed over), virtually (money wired from an account), and eminently (the power to print bills).
2. *What is wrong with Cottingham's sponge-cake example?* It looks only at the ingredients, not the total cause, and it assumes the feature must be present formally.
3. *What does "action follows being" mean, and how does it apply to God?* A thing's activity cannot exceed its nature. A purely actual being cannot act in a merely potential way.
4. *Why is "being" not a genus?* A genus is divided by differences that lie outside it, but nothing lies outside being except nothing, and nothing cannot divide anything.
5. *Why can't God-talk be merely univocal or merely equivocal?* Univocal talk would make God a creature or leave only negations. Equivocal talk would be empty and lead to agnosticism.
6. *Why can there be only one purely actual being?* Being one of many requires parts, parts require potential, and pure actuality has no potential.
7. *How does denying simplicity reduce God to a creature?* A God with parts would need a cause to combine them, and a God within a kind would be unique only by chance, like Zeus alone on Olympus.
8. *How does the Cambridge-change distinction answer Morris?* Simplicity identifies only God's real properties. Being Creator is a Cambridge property, so it doesn't need to be identical with omnipotence.
9. *How can God cause things at different times without being in time?* "God causes X at time 1" does not mean "at time 1, God causes X." One timeless act produces the whole temporal world, as an author conceives a whole story at once.
10. *What are Feser's two replies to Schrader's empty world?* God, like numbers, cannot be pictured, so imagining absence proves nothing. And what is necessary is learned from the natures of actual things, so Schrader simply assumes the conclusion he needs.
