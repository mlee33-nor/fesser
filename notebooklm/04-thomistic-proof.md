# The Thomistic Proof: From What Things Are to the One Who Simply Is

*A study guide to Chapter 4 of Edward Feser's* Five Proofs of the Existence of God *(Ignatius Press, 2017), pages 117–146*

## Orientation

Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* defends five classical arguments for God, each starting from a different evident feature of the world: **change** (Chapter 1, the Aristotelian proof, ending in a purely actual "unmoved mover"), **composition** (Chapter 2, the Neoplatonic proof, ending in an absolutely simple "One"), **abstract objects** (Chapter 3, the Augustinian proof), and the **principle of sufficient reason** (Chapter 5, the rationalist proof). Chapter 4, the subject of this guide, is the **Thomistic proof**, named for Saint Thomas Aquinas. It starts from the most basic fact of all: that things **exist**. Its key idea is the distinction between a thing's **essence** — what it is — and its **existence** — the fact that it is. Feser draws the argument from Aquinas's early treatise *On Being and Essence* (*De Ente et Essentia*), and it ends with what Aquinas calls **subsistent existence itself** (*ipsum esse subsistens*): something that does not merely *have* existence, as everything else does, but *is* existence.

## Why This Chapter Matters

Most people who hear "first cause argument" picture dominoes stretching back to the Big Bang, and reply: "Maybe the universe is eternal," or "What caused God?" The Thomistic proof is immune to both replies.

First, it has nothing to do with a beginning in time. It asks what is keeping things in existence **right now**, and would work just as well if the universe had always existed.

Second, it never says "everything has a cause." It says that anything whose essence is distinct from its existence needs a cause of its existence. God is not an arbitrary exception; the rule simply doesn't apply to him, because his essence just *is* existence.

Third, the Thomistic proof goes deeper than any of the other proofs. The Aristotelian proof asks what keeps things *changing*; the Neoplatonic proof asks what keeps their parts *together*; the Thomistic proof asks what keeps them *in being at all*. It also shows why the answer must be unique: there cannot even in principle be two things whose essence is existence. And it connects directly with the earlier chapters, because the relationship between essence and existence turns out to be a case of the relationship between potentiality and actuality — so the God of the Thomistic proof is the same purely actual God reached in Chapter 1.

## Key Concepts and Vocabulary

**Essence.** A thing's nature: what it is; that which makes it the kind of thing it is. The traditional definition of a human being as a "rational animal" states the human essence. The essence of a triangle is being a closed plane figure with three straight sides. For the Thomist, we grasp an essence when we identify a thing's genus and specific difference.

**Existence.** The fact or act of a thing's being real: not *what* it is, but *that* it is. A unicorn has an essence (we can say what a unicorn would be), but it lacks existence. For the Thomist, existence is not a property or feature a thing has alongside its others, because a thing must first exist before it can have any properties at all.

**Real distinction versus merely conceptual (or verbal) distinction.** Some distinctions exist only in our words or thoughts. "Bachelor" and "unmarried man" are two phrases for one and the same reality. A *real* distinction, by contrast, reflects a difference in things themselves, independent of how we think or talk. Crucially, a real distinction does not always mean the two items can be separated. Two dogs are really distinct and separable. But a circle's radius and its circumference are really distinct (one is 2π times the other) and yet can never exist apart.

**Contingent and necessary.** A contingent thing exists but could have failed to exist. A necessary thing could not possibly fail to exist; it would exist by its very nature.

**Genus, species, specific difference, and matter.** In the traditional logical sense, a genus is a general class (animal), a species a narrower class within it (human being), and the "specific difference" what marks the species off (rationality). Members of one species, like Socrates and Plato, differ instead by being made of different parcels of matter.

**Subsistent existence itself (*ipsum esse subsistens*).** That whose essence is identical with its existence. It is not one existing thing among others but existence itself, subsisting in its own right. It neither needs nor could receive existence from anything else.

**Conservation versus generation.** Generation brings a new thing into being out of pre-existing materials (parents produce offspring). Conservation is the ongoing causal activity by which a thing is kept in being from moment to moment — "imparting existence" to its essence.

**Hierarchical versus linear causal series.** A linear series stretches out through time, and each member, once it exists, can act independently of earlier ones (grandparents, parents, child); it could in principle go back infinitely. In a hierarchical series each member causes only insofar as it is being caused, right now, by the one before it. All members except the first are merely **instrumental**. Chapter 1 argued that such a series must have a first member — "first" meaning *primary*, not *earliest*.

**Potentiality and actuality.** From Chapter 1: actuality is the way a thing actually is; potentiality is a capacity to be some way it is not yet. Aquinas held that essence relates to existence as potentiality to actuality: a dog's essence by itself is only a potential dog. What has no potentiality at all is **purely actual** — the God of Chapter 1.

## The Argument Told as a Story

### Stage 1, Part One: Two things we know about everything

Look around: stones, trees, dogs, people. When we know these things, we know two different things about them. We know **what** they are — a human being is a rational animal, say. (Feser adds that you don't have to accept this particular definition; substitute your own.) And we know **that** they are — there really are human beings.

So we can distinguish a thing's essence from its existence. The real question is whether this distinction is only in our heads, like the difference between "bachelor" and "unmarried man," or whether it reflects something in things themselves. Feser gives three separate reasons to think it is real.

### The first reason: knowing what without knowing whether

Imagine a person who has never heard of lions, pterodactyls, or unicorns. You describe each one's nature to him in thorough detail. Then you tell him that one of them is alive today, one used to exist but went extinct, and one never existed at all. Can he tell, just from the descriptions, which is which?

Of course not. Yet what a thing is belongs to its objective reality — the biology of lions would be the same whether or not anyone studied them. And if a thing exists, its existence is obviously part of its reality too. So if a thing's essence and its existence were not distinct aspects of reality, knowing the one would be enough to know the other. Since it isn't, they must be really distinct.

A critic might object: we rarely know a thing's complete essence, so maybe existence is lurking in the part we don't know. Feser answers with a subtle but powerful contrast. Suppose you know a lion is an animal but haven't realized it is a cat. Your idea of a lion is *incomplete*, but not *wrong*. If you went further and judged that a lion is *not* a cat, you would have *misconceived* what a lion is. Now suppose you judge that lions don't exist — you think they are extinct like pterodactyls or mythical like unicorns. You are mistaken about the world, but you have not misconceived what a lion is. If existence were part of a lion's essence, denying it would be just as much a misconception as denying that lions are cats. It isn't, so existence is not part of what a lion is.

### The second reason: contingency

The things of our experience are contingent. Lions exist, but if the history of life had gone differently there would be no lions, and someday lions may go extinct. Now suppose a lion's existence were not really distinct from its essence. Then it would have existence simply by virtue of being what it is — it would exist by its nature. But then it could not fail to exist; it would be necessary. Since lions are not necessary, their essence and existence must be distinct.

A critic might say: we can explain contingency without any metaphysical distinction. A lion is contingent because it needs a cause (earlier lions) and has potentialities for going out of existence (being killed, starving, catching a disease). Feser replies that this misses the point. The question is *why* a lion needs a cause of its existence — or could even have one — and *why* it has any potential for nonexistence, if existence is simply part of what it is. If existence were built into its nature, nothing else would need to supply it, and nothing in it could make it liable to cease. Having causes and being perishable are signs of the real distinction, not replacements for it.

### The third reason: only one thing could have existence as its essence

This argument is ingenious. Suppose there were something whose essence and existence were *not* distinct. Then they would be identical: that thing's essence would just *be* existence itself. Feser argues that there could be only one such thing.

For there to be two such things — call them A and B — something would have to make them two rather than one. Something would have to differentiate them. There are two standard ways things are differentiated.

One is the way two **species within a genus** differ. Humans differ from other animals by a specific difference, rationality: a human being is animality *plus* rationality, which is why a human is not simply "animality itself." So if A and B were two species of the genus "that which is existence itself," each would need its own specific difference. But then A would be existence itself *plus* its difference, and B existence itself *plus* its difference. Neither would be simply existence itself.

The other way is how **members of the same species** differ. Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle are all human; Fido, Rover, and Spot are all dogs. What makes them different individuals is that each is associated with different matter. Socrates is humanity plus *this* bit of matter; Plato is humanity plus *that* bit. That is exactly why Socrates is not "humanity itself." So if A and B were differentiated this way, A would be existence itself plus this matter, and B existence itself plus that matter — and once again neither would be existence itself.

The general point: any feature that distinguished one "existence itself" from another would make each of them "existence itself plus something," and so not existence itself after all. So there is simply no sense in the idea of two things whose essence is existence. If there is such a thing, it is unique.

But stones, trees, dogs, and people are not unique in this way. There are many of each, and even if a kind were reduced to one last member, there *could* be more. So none of them is something whose essence is existence. In each of them, essence and existence are really distinct.

### No middle ground

Someone might try to wriggle out: perhaps some thing's essence and existence are not distinct, not because its essence *is* existence, but because existence is one *part* of its essence, alongside other parts. (Feser mentions this suggestion as discussed by the scholar Peter Weigel.)

Feser argues the suggestion collapses. Take the human essence, rational animality, with its two parts, rationality and animality. Suppose existence were a third part. Then the existence of the whole human being would depend on that one part. But that is no more plausible than saying the whole human essence depends on animality alone. And if someone really insisted that the whole essence depended on animality alone, he would effectively be treating animality as the true essence. In the same way, if the whole thing depends on its "existence part," then existence is its true essence. We are right back to something whose essence just is existence. So there are only two possibilities: essence really distinct from existence, or essence identical with existence.

### Distinct, but not separable

It is important not to misunderstand "real distinction." Feser is not saying that a stone's essence is one object and its existence another, each of which could exist without the other. Some real distinctions involve separability — two dogs, or a dog and its leg. Others don't. Drawing on the philosopher David Oderberg, Feser points to the circle. Having a radius and having a circumference are really different properties, since the circumference is 2π times the radius. Yet in a circle you never have one without the other. The same is true of triangularity (having three angles) and trilaterality (having three sides). In just this way, there is no such thing as a stone's essence existing without its existence, or a free-floating existence with no essence. Essence and existence are inseparable in any real thing — and still really distinct.

### How are essence and existence joined?

If essence and existence are really distinct, what unites them in an actual lion or dog? Someone might say: they are inseparable, and that's that. But inseparability isn't an explanation; it is exactly what needs explaining. Why are the radius and circumference of a circle inseparable? Because both *follow from* the essence of a circle — anything with that essence will have both.

Could a lion's existence follow from its essence in the same way? No. If it did, knowing what a lion is would tell you that lions exist, which it doesn't. And lions would then exist necessarily, not contingently. The only thing whose existence would follow from its essence is something whose essence *is* existence — and there can be only one of those, while there are many lions, dogs, and stones.

Could a thing give existence to itself, adding it to its own essence? That idea is incoherent. To do anything at all — including imparting existence — a thing must already exist. But a thing whose essence and existence are distinct doesn't exist until existence is conferred on it. So to give itself existence it would have to exist before it exists. Nothing can cause its own existence. Its existence must come from a cause outside it.

### Fido, right now

Ordinary experience agrees that dogs and trees have causes: parents, seeds, and so on. But the dependence Feser means is far more radical. Everything said so far is true of a thing not only before and as it comes into being, but at **every moment** it exists.

Consider a dog named Fido. Right now, Fido's existence is still distinct from his essence, still doesn't follow from his essence, and still can't be supplied by Fido himself. So right now, something must be imparting existence to his essence. Otherwise he would no more exist now than he did before he was conceived; he would simply "blink out." And Fido can't be doing this for himself even for an instant, since to do anything at an instant he must already exist at that instant. Causing one's own existence is as incoherent at a single moment as over a stretch of time.

So anything whose essence is distinct from its existence needs a cause of its existence **here and now**, not merely a cause back in the past.

### The chain of causes and where it must end

Call Fido's present cause of existence C. If C is also something whose essence is distinct from its existence, then C too needs something imparting existence to it right now — call it B. If B is the same way, it needs A. And so on.

This, Feser points out, is exactly the kind of **hierarchical causal series** discussed in Chapter 1. It is hierarchical not merely because the causes act at the same time, but because none of them can act independently. C can give existence to Fido only insofar as B is giving existence to C, and B only insofar as A is giving existence to B. Each merely passes on existence it is itself receiving.

As Chapter 1 argued, a series like this cannot go back infinitely. There would be no series at all unless there were a cause that is first in the sense of *primary*: one that causes without, in the very act of causing, needing to be caused. Here, that means a cause that can *give* existence without having to *receive* it.

Nothing whose essence is distinct from its existence could be that cause, since anything like that must receive existence. So the first cause can only be something whose very essence is existence — something that just is existence itself. Such a thing doesn't need, and indeed couldn't have, its existence from anything else; its existence is, so to speak, built in. It isn't one more existing thing alongside the rest. It is what Aquinas calls **subsistent existence itself**.

So for Fido to exist at any moment, he must be caused to exist at that moment — directly or through intermediaries — by subsistent existence itself. And that, Feser says, is to be caused by God.

### Stage 2: Why this is God

Why identify subsistent existence itself with God? Feser gives a chain of reasons.

**It is unique and the cause of everything else.** What holds for Fido holds for every thing whose essence is distinct from its existence — stones, trees, lions, people, and even immaterial things like angels or souls, if there are any. There can be only one thing whose essence is existence. And these two categories — things whose essence is distinct from their existence, and a thing whose essence is identical with it — exhaust the possibilities. So everything other than subsistent existence itself falls in the first category, and everything in the first category depends on subsistent existence itself. Therefore it is the cause of everything other than itself.

**It is necessary and uncaused.** Since its essence just is existence, it cannot fail to exist. And for the same reason it neither needs nor could receive existence from anything else. It is the uncaused cause of the existence of all other things.

**It is purely actual.** Aquinas emphasized that in a thing whose essence is distinct from its existence, essence and existence relate as potentiality and actuality. Fido's essence by itself is only a potential dog. Only when existence is imparted to it is there an actual dog. So if essence taken by itself is a kind of potentiality and existence a kind of actuality, then what just *is* existence must be purely actual. It cannot have some potential for existence waiting to be actualized — if it did, it would be one more thing needing existence imparted to it.

**It is the same God as the Aristotelian proof's.** Chapter 1 showed that there can be only one purely actual thing. So the purely actual actualizer reached from the fact of change and the subsistent existence itself reached from the distinction between essence and existence are one and the same cause of all things, arrived at from different starting points.

**It has the divine attributes.** Chapter 1 also showed that whatever is purely actual must be immutable (change requires potentiality), eternal, immaterial and incorporeal (matter involves potentiality), perfect, omnipotent, and fully good. And adding the principle of proportionate causality — roughly, that whatever is in an effect must be in some way in its cause — the cause of all things must have intellect and be omniscient.

So subsistent existence itself is one, necessarily existing, the uncaused cause of everything else, purely actual, immutable, eternal, immaterial, incorporeal, perfect, omnipotent, fully good, intelligent, and omniscient. That, Feser concludes, is God.

### A connection to Chapter 2

Chapter 2's Neoplatonic proof had already listed essence and existence among the "metaphysical parts" whose combination needs a cause, and argued that the ultimate cause must be absolutely simple. The Thomistic proof develops that one kind of composition in depth, and its uniqueness argument mirrors Chapter 2's: anything that distinguished two candidates would be an added feature destroying their defining character. Subsistent existence itself, with no distinction of essence and existence, is the utterly simple One.

## The Formal Argument, Step by Step (Paraphrased)

Feser summarizes the proof in 36 steps. Here they are in fresh wording, each with a one-line justification.

**Part A — The real distinction (steps 1–11)**

1. For everything we know from experience, we can distinguish its essence from its existence. *(What it is versus that it is.)*
2. If this weren't a real distinction, knowing a thing's essence would tell us whether it exists. *(Both are part of objective reality; if identical, knowing one gives the other.)*
3. But knowing a thing's essence doesn't tell us whether it exists. *(The lion, pterodactyl, and unicorn test.)*
4. If it weren't a real distinction, the things of experience would exist necessarily, not contingently. *(They would exist by their very nature.)*
5. But they exist contingently. *(They come to be and pass away.)*
6. If there could be more than one thing whose essence is its existence, they would differ as species of a genus, as members of a species, or in some other way. *(Plurality requires a differentiator.)*
7. They couldn't differ in any of these ways. *(Any differentiator makes each "existence itself plus something.")*
8. So there couldn't be more than one thing whose essence is its existence. *(From 6 and 7.)*
9. So if a thing of experience lacked a real distinction, there could be only one of its kind. *(From 8; there's no middle ground.)*
10. But there is, or could be, more than one of each kind of thing we experience. *(Many stones, trees, dogs, people.)*
11. So for each thing of experience, essence and existence are really distinct. *(From 9 and 10, backed by 2–3 and 4–5.)*

**Part B — Dependence here and now (steps 12–18)**

12. If a thing's essence is really distinct from its existence, its existence must come either from itself or from a distinct cause. *(Existence doesn't follow from its essence, so it must be conferred.)*
13. If it gave itself existence, it would be its own cause. *(That's what self-imparting means.)*
14. Nothing can cause itself. *(It would have to exist before it exists.)*
15. So it can't give itself existence. *(From 13 and 14.)*
16. So its existence must come from a distinct cause. *(From 12 and 15.)*
17. Since its essence and existence remain distinct at every moment, including now, it needs that cause at every moment, including now. *(The Fido point.)*
18. So everything we experience has its existence imparted by a distinct cause at every moment, including now. *(Applying 16–17 via 11.)*

**Part C — The first cause (steps 19–23)**

19. That cause either has an essence distinct from its existence, or is something whose essence is existence — subsistent existence itself. *(These are the only options.)*
20. If the former, it too needs a distinct cause imparting existence to it at every moment. *(Steps 16–17 apply to it as well.)*
21. The resulting series is hierarchical, so it can't regress infinitely and must have a first member. *(Each member only passes on received existence; see Chapter 1.)*
22. That first member can only be something whose essence is existence. *(It must give existence without receiving it.)*
23. So, directly or indirectly, everything we experience receives its existence at every moment from subsistent existence itself. *(From 18–22.)*

**Part D — Unique cause of all (steps 24–27)**

24. Since there can be only one thing whose essence is existence, this cause is unique. *(From 8.)*
25. So everything else has an essence distinct from its existence. *(The two categories are exhaustive.)*
26. Everything with an essence distinct from its existence receives existence, directly or indirectly, from subsistent existence itself. *(The reasoning of 16–23 applies universally.)*
27. So subsistent existence itself is the cause of everything other than itself. *(From 25 and 26.)*

**Part E — The attributes (steps 28–33)**

28. Whatever lacks a real distinction between essence and existence exists necessarily, so this cause exists necessarily. *(It exists by its very essence.)*
29. Subsistent existence itself doesn't need, and couldn't have, a cause. *(There's nothing in it that receives existence.)*
30. So it is uncaused. *(From 29.)*
31. If it had a potential for existence needing actualization, existence would have to be imparted to it by a cause. *(Essence is to existence as potency to act.)*
32. So it has no such potential; it is purely actual. *(From 30 and 31.)*
33. Whatever is purely actual is immutable, eternal, immaterial, incorporeal, perfect, omnipotent, fully good, intelligent, and omniscient. *(Established in Chapter 1.)*

**Part F — Conclusion (steps 34–36)**

34. So everything we experience receives existence at every moment from a cause that is subsistent existence itself: one, necessary, the uncaused cause of all else, purely actual, immutable, eternal, immaterial, incorporeal, perfect, omnipotent, fully good, intelligent, and omniscient. *(Gathering 23–33.)*
35. For such a cause to exist is for God to exist. *(This is what classical theism means by God.)*
36. So God exists.

## Every Objection and Its Reply

### 1. "We never fully grasp essences."

*Objection:* The knowability argument assumes we know a thing's complete essence. We don't — so existence might be part of the essence we haven't grasped.

*Reply:* Denying a genuine part of an essence (saying a lion isn't a cat) is a misconception of the thing. Denying a thing's existence (saying lions are extinct) is merely a false judgment about the world; it leaves your idea of what a lion is intact. So existence doesn't behave like a part of any essence.

### 2. "Contingency is just having causes and being perishable."

*Objection:* We can explain why lions are contingent by pointing to their causes and their vulnerability to death, without any real distinction.

*Reply:* Why would a lion need — or even be able to have — a cause of its existence, or any potential to lose its existence, if existence were simply part of what it is? Dependence and fragility presuppose the real distinction.

### 3. "Existence could be one part of an essence."

*Objection (a middle option discussed by Peter Weigel):* A thing could have existence as a component of its essence without its essence simply being existence, escaping the uniqueness argument.

*Reply:* If the whole thing depends for its existence on that component, then that component is the true essence — just as making the whole human essence depend on animality alone would make animality the true essence. So the middle option collapses into "essence is existence."

### 4. "Essence and existence are just inseparable; no cause needed."

*Objection:* If essence and existence are like a circle's radius and circumference, they simply go together.

*Reply:* Inseparability needs explaining. Radius and circumference are inseparable because both follow from the circle's essence. A lion's existence does not follow from its essence (otherwise we'd know lions exist from their definition, and they'd be necessary). And a thing can't give itself existence. So the union requires an external cause.

### 5. "A first cause needn't be God."

*Reply:* Stage 2 shows it is unique, the cause of all else, necessary, uncaused, and purely actual, with all the attributes of pure actuality (including intellect). The attributes are defended at length later in the book.

### 6. "The argument assumes the universe had a beginning."

*Reply:* It doesn't. The chain of causes it traces doesn't run backward into the past; it runs "downward" to what is sustaining things here and now. An eternal universe or eternal multiverse would leave the argument untouched.

### 7. "If everything has a cause, what caused God?"

*Reply:* The argument never says everything has a cause. It says that whatever has an essence really distinct from its existence needs a cause. God is not an arbitrary exception: in him there is no such distinction, so he not only needs no cause but could not have one. Feser stresses that this was always the Thomistic view, not a recent evasion, and calls the retort utterly beside the point.

### 8. Hume and quantum mechanics against causation.

*Reply:* To appeals to Hume or quantum indeterminacy against the causal principle, Feser refers back to Chapter 1: conceiving something without conceiving its cause isn't conceiving it as uncaused, and causation doesn't require determinism. These objections are no stronger here.

### 9. "Why need there be a first cause?" (Hume)

*Objection:* If every member of a causal series is explained by an earlier one, then even an infinite series leaves nothing unexplained.

*Reply:* That's true for linear series, but not for hierarchical ones. In a hierarchical series every member except the first is merely instrumental, so extending it to infinity just multiplies borrowers; something outside the series must supply the power to all of them. Moreover, the series Hume allows is one of generation in time — Fido from his parents, and so on. The Thomistic proof concerns conservation: what keeps Fido, his parents, and even their matter and powers in existence at each moment. Even an infinite line of dogs would need each dog to be conserved in being. An infinite series of conserving causes that merely pass on existence can no more give you a real dog than an infinite series of IOUs can give you real money. Feser adds Barry Miller's observation that an explanation of the form "A is caused to exist by B insofar as B is caused to exist by C insofar as…" can only be completed by naming a first member — and the only non-arbitrary place to stop is with subsistent existence itself.

### 10. The fallacy of composition.

*Objection:* Each brick in a wall weighs a pound, but the wall doesn't; likewise, each thing in the universe may need a cause, but the universe as a whole need not.

*Reply:* First, part-to-whole reasoning isn't always fallacious: a wall made entirely of red Lego bricks is red. Since what makes a material thing need a cause is its real distinction between essence and existence, and the universe is made of such things and has its own distinct essence and existence, it too needs a cause. Second, and more importantly, the argument never reasons from parts to the whole. It can begin with a single dog. The conclusion that God causes the whole universe comes at the end, as a consequence of God's uniqueness.

### 11. Kenny's objection.

*Objection:* The philosopher Anthony Kenny distinguishes two senses of "existence." **Specific existence** is what modern logic expresses with "there is an x such that x is F" — it says a concept has instances (a view associated with the logician Gottlob Frege). **Individual existence** is what the Great Pyramid still has and the Library of Alexandria lost when it was destroyed. On the first sense, God's essence and existence are just as distinct as a unicorn's — we can know what God is without knowing whether there is one — and to say God's essence *is* "there is an x such that…" is nonsense. On the second sense, existing is just continuing to be what you are; that's as true of Fido as of God, so the contrast between God and creatures collapses.

*Reply (drawing on Gyula Klima and John Knasas):* First, Kenny assumes a real distinction requires separability; the circle shows it doesn't, and assuming otherwise begs the question. Second, he assumes his two senses are the only respectable ones, usually on the ground that if existence were predicated of things, "Martians do not exist" would contradict itself (some existing Martians lack existence). Knasas replies that we grasp the subjects of such statements in an existence-neutral way: the statement says Martians, of themselves neither existent nor nonexistent, are not in fact real. Third, the quantifier leaves something unexplained: if "cats exist" means something falls under the concept *cat*, what makes that true? The Thomist's answer is existence — the act that makes an otherwise merely potential essence actual (and which is not a property, since a thing must exist to have properties).

### 12. "It's only a conceptual distinction" (the aspirin objection).

*Objection:* Knowing X without knowing Y doesn't prove X and Y are distinct. Aspirin is acetylsalicylic acid, yet I might know aspirin relieves pain without knowing that acetylsalicylic acid does. Likewise, knowing a lion's essence without knowing its existence shows only that the concepts differ.

*Reply (Klima):* It depends on whether the concepts are logically independent. With a vague notion of aspirin ("some chemical sold for pain"), I might not know it's acetylsalicylic acid. But with complete knowledge of aspirin's chemical essence, I couldn't fail to know it. If a lion's essence were identical to its existence, complete knowledge of the essence should likewise reveal its existence. It never does, for lions, stones, trees, or anything we experience.

*"But that begs the question!"* No: there's independent reason. When I know the essence of a kind, I know in advance what any member that does, did, will, or could exist would be like; but whether any member actually exists I can only learn from experience. And while judging that aspirin is not acetylsalicylic acid misconceives aspirin, judging that aspirin doesn't exist doesn't.

### 13. "Things don't really have essences."

*Objection:* Essences are human conventions, like the definition of "carburetor" or "can opener." Without real essences, there's no real distinction between essence and existence.

*Reply:* Feser grants that artifacts have conventional essences but argues that natural things have real ones:

- As Aristotle held, it's absurd to try to prove things have natures; it is more obvious than any argument about it.
- Oaks, polar bears, and copper samples share causal powers just as real natures would predict — a miracle if the groupings were mere convention. And each thing shows internal unity: characteristic behavior, persistence through change, integrated parts. Natures can be hard to discover, but that doesn't mean they're absent.
- Science's practice (Nancy Cartwright): physics studies idealized, contrived conditions and often settles a law with a few experiments — just how one looks for natures. Science's results (Brian Ellis): chemistry reveals discrete natural kinds, discovered rather than invented.
- Conventionalism is incoherent (Crawford Elder): if every essence depended on the mind, so would the mind's own, making the mind prior and posterior to itself.
- The proof needs only *some* real essences. Even if "dog" were a mere label for arranged particles, the particles and the labeling mind would have real essences distinct from their existence.

Feser's verdict: all the objections fail, leaving a fourth proof of the existence of God.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **"It's about the Big Bang."** No. It's about what keeps things in existence right now, and works even if the universe is eternal.
- **"It says everything has a cause."** No. Only things whose essence is distinct from their existence need a cause of existence.
- **"Real distinction means essences and existences float around separately."** No. They are inseparable, like a circle's radius and circumference, yet really distinct.
- **"Essence and existence are just two words."** Feser gives three arguments that they differ in reality, and Klima shows why the aspirin comparison fails.
- **"First cause means the earliest cause."** No. It means the primary cause on which every other depends at this moment.
- **"The argument needs to treat the universe as a whole."** No. One dog is enough; the claim about the universe is a consequence.
- **"Subsistent existence itself is just the biggest thing there is."** No. It isn't one being among others at all.
- **"This gives a different God than the argument from change."** No. It is purely actual, and there can be only one purely actual being.

## How to Explain It to Others

**The simple version.** Everything around us has a *what* and a *that*. You can know what a unicorn is without there being any unicorns, and you can know what a lion is without knowing whether lions exist. So being real isn't part of being a lion. That means a lion has to get its existence from something else — and not just when it's born, but at every moment, because at every moment its existence still isn't part of what it is. Whatever gives it existence either merely *has* existence too (and so needs a source of its own) or *is* existence. A chain of things that merely have existence, each borrowing from the next, can't explain anything, no matter how long it is. So it has to end in something whose very nature is to exist. There can only be one such thing, since anything that made two of them different would make each "existence plus something." That one source of the existence of everything is what we call God.

**The best analogy: IOUs and real money.** An IOU is only worth something if somewhere it is backed by actual money. A pile of IOUs, each promising payment from another IOU, is worthless however high you stack it — even infinitely high. Things whose essence is distinct from their existence are like IOUs: their existence is "on loan." A chain of causes that merely pass along borrowed existence never produces a real dog unless it is backed by something that has existence not on loan but by nature — subsistent existence itself. (The analogy's limit: the "cash" isn't just the first item in the stack, but something of an entirely different kind.)

**Teaching tips.** Start with the lion, pterodactyl, and unicorn test before any technical terms. Say early that the argument is not about the universe's beginning and doesn't claim everything has a cause. Use the circle to explain "distinct but inseparable." Spend real time on why there can be only one thing whose essence is existence — that step rules out rival necessary beings.

## Key Takeaways

- Every ordinary thing has an essence (what it is) and existence (that it is), and these are **really distinct**.
- Three arguments support this: essence can be known without knowing existence; ordinary things are contingent; and anything whose essence is existence would be unique, while ordinary things come in many.
- Real distinction does **not** mean separability (radius and circumference).
- A thing whose essence is distinct from its existence can't get existence from its essence and can't give it to itself, so it must receive it from a cause — **at every moment**, including now.
- The causes that impart existence form a **hierarchical** series, which must end in something whose essence **is** existence: **subsistent existence itself**.
- There can be only **one** such thing; it causes everything else; it is necessary and uncaused.
- Essence relates to existence as potency to act, so subsistent existence itself is **purely actual** — the same God as the Aristotelian proof, with all the divine attributes.
- It doesn't assume a beginning, claim "everything has a cause," or commit the fallacy of composition.
- Kenny's objection, the aspirin objection, and conventionalism about essences all fail, according to Feser.

## Ten Review Questions with Brief Answers

1. **What is the difference between essence and existence?** Essence is what a thing is; existence is the fact that it is. A unicorn has an essence but no existence.

2. **What does the lion, pterodactyl, and unicorn test show?** That knowing what things are doesn't tell you whether they exist — so existence isn't part of their essence.

3. **How does contingency show the real distinction?** If a thing's existence were part of its essence, it would exist by nature and couldn't fail to exist. Ordinary things can fail to exist.

4. **Why can there be only one thing whose essence is existence?** Anything that distinguished two such things — a specific difference, a parcel of matter, anything — would make each "existence itself plus something," which isn't existence itself.

5. **Does a real distinction require that the two items be separable?** No. A circle's radius and circumference are really distinct but inseparable; so are a thing's essence and existence.

6. **Why can't a thing give itself existence?** It would have to exist already in order to do anything, so it would have to exist before it exists.

7. **Why does Fido need a cause of existence right now, not just in the past?** Because at every moment his existence still isn't part of his essence and still can't be self-supplied; without a present cause he would blink out of existence.

8. **Why must the chain of existence-giving causes end in subsistent existence itself?** Because it is hierarchical: each member only passes on existence it is receiving. It must end in something that gives existence without receiving it, and only something whose essence is existence can do that.

9. **How does the Thomistic proof connect to the Aristotelian proof?** Essence is to existence as potency to act, so subsistent existence itself is purely actual. Since there can be only one purely actual being, both proofs reach the same God, with the same attributes.

10. **Why does "What caused God?" miss the point?** The argument's premise is only that things whose essence is distinct from their existence need a cause. God's essence is existence, so he needs no cause — and could not have one.
