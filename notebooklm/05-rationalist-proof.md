# The Rationalist Proof: Everything Has an Explanation, So Something Must Explain Itself

*A study guide to Chapter 5 of Edward Feser's* Five Proofs of the Existence of God *(Ignatius Press, 2017), pages 147–168.*

## Orientation

Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* defends five classic philosophical arguments for God. Each starts from a different feature of the world, and each is meant as a demonstration, not a best guess. The first four were the **Aristotelian proof** (from change to a purely actual Unmoved Mover), the **Neo-Platonic proof** (from composite things to an absolutely simple One), the **Augustinian proof** (from abstract truths to a divine intellect), and the **Thomistic proof** (from the distinction between what a thing is and the fact that it exists, to a being whose essence *is* existence). Chapter 5 presents the fifth, the **rationalist proof**. Its starting point is the most general of all: the fact that things are *intelligible*, that there is always a reason why they exist and are as they are. This guide covers the whole chapter: the defense of the principle of sufficient reason, the move from that principle to an absolutely necessary being, the identification of that being with God, and Feser's replies to objections. Everything here is paraphrased in plain language for learners.

## Why This Chapter Matters

Most people who hear an argument for God meet the rationalist proof in some form, often under the name "the argument from contingency." It asks the most natural question there is: *why does anything that doesn't have to exist exist at all?* It is also the argument most directly aimed at a popular atheist position, which Bertrand Russell summed up in his famous debate with Frederick Copleston roughly as "the universe is just there, and that's all." On this view, explanation eventually stops at some brute fact, perhaps the fundamental laws of physics, and there is nothing more to say.

Chapter 5 takes on that position at its root. Feser spends most of the first half of the chapter defending the principle that everything has an explanation. He argues that this principle is not a pious assumption smuggled in by theists but something every scientist, philosopher, and ordinary reasoner relies on, so that denying it undermines reasoning itself. Only then does he show how the principle leads, step by step, to a necessary being and on to God.

Three further things make this chapter important.

First, it is where Feser most clearly separates his version of the argument from the version associated with Leibniz, the version most contemporary critics attack. His understanding of the principle of sufficient reason comes from the Thomist tradition and is more modest in specific ways, and that lets it escape the most famous objection to the principle.

Second, it shows how the five proofs fit together. The rationalist proof reaches a "necessary being," and Feser then argues that anything necessary in the strictest sense must be purely actual, simple, and self-subsistent existence, which are the conclusions of the earlier proofs. The proofs, he says, complement one another.

Third, it gives the clearest answer in the book to "What caused God?" The rationalist proof says that *absolutely everything* has an explanation and makes no exception for God. God is not unexplained; he is self-explanatory.

## Key Concepts and Vocabulary

**The principle of sufficient reason (PSR).** The thesis that whatever exists has an explanation of its existence and of the attributes it has, whether or not we ever discover it. Leibniz made it famous, but thinkers from many traditions have held it. Thomist writers put it as "whatever exists has a sufficient reason for existing" or, more simply, "everything is intelligible."

**Sufficient reason, or explanation.** Whatever makes a thing's existence or features intelligible. It can lie outside the thing, when the thing is caused by something else, or inside the thing's own nature, when the thing is self-explanatory.

**The principle of causality.** From the book's first chapter: any potential that is actualized must be actualized by something already actual. Two points separate it from PSR. A cause must be *distinct* from its effect, since nothing causes itself, but a sufficient reason need not be distinct from what it explains. And the principle of causality does *not* say everything has a cause, while PSR *does* say everything has a sufficient reason. Every cause is a reason, because it makes its effect intelligible, but not every reason is a cause.

**Self-explanatory versus self-caused.** A self-explanatory thing is one whose existence is accounted for by what it is. That is different from being self-caused, which is impossible, because to be caused is to have a potential actualized, and nothing can actualize its own potential. God, on Feser's view, is self-explanatory and uncaused.

**Brute fact.** A supposed fact with no explanation at all, neither in itself nor in anything else. PSR says there are none.

**Contingent being.** Something whose explanation lies outside itself. It depends on outside circumstances and would not exist if they didn't obtain. Stones, trees, dogs, people, planets, galaxies, and universes are examples.

**Necessary being.** Something whose explanation lies in its own nature, so that it cannot fail to exist and depends on nothing else.

**Necessity in the strictest sense versus derivative necessity.** Aquinas sometimes calls things "necessary" in a looser sense, meaning they have no natural tendency to decay. Angels, which are naturally immortal, qualify. But angels are still caused, because they mix actuality with potentiality, have parts, and have an essence distinct from their existence, so their necessity is derivative. The rationalist proof is about necessity in the strictest sense: depending on nothing whatsoever.

**Reductio ad absurdum.** Proving a claim by showing that denying it leads to absurdity. Feser, following the Thomist Reginald Garrigou-Lagrange, holds that PSR cannot be proved directly but can be proved this indirect way.

**Explicability argument (EA).** The philosopher Michael Della Rocca's term for arguing that some state of affairs does not obtain because, if it did, it would be inexplicable.

**Explanatory demonstration.** An Aristotelian term for a deductive argument whose premises give the reason why the conclusion is true. For example: all rational animals can use language; all human beings are rational animals; so all human beings can use language.

**Nomological regress.** A chain of explanations by laws of nature, each explained by a deeper law.

**Fallacy of composition.** Wrongly inferring that a whole has a property because its parts do. Whether a part-to-whole inference is fallacious depends on the property involved.

**Simultaneous (sustaining) cause.** A cause that keeps something in existence *right now*, as opposed to one that produced it in the past.

**Pure actuality, absolute simplicity, subsistent existence itself.** The conclusions of the first, second, and fourth proofs. Pure actuality is existence with no unrealized potential. Absolute simplicity is having no parts of any kind. Subsistent existence itself is a being whose essence simply *is* existence, as opposed to an essence that receives existence from something else.

**Explanans and explanandum.** The explanans does the explaining; the explanandum is what gets explained. If the ground is wet because it rained last night, the wet ground is the explanandum and the rain is the explanans.

**Scholastic realism.** The view, defended in the book's third chapter, that universals and propositions are real but exist only in the minds that think them, ultimately in the divine mind, and not in a separate Platonic realm.

## The Argument Told as a Story

### Stage 1, part one: we live by explanation

Feser begins with ordinary detective work. You find a pair of sunglasses you don't recognize on your sofa, ask around, and learn that a visiting friend forgot them. A rash appears on your arm, and you realize you probably brushed against poison oak on a recent hike. You hear scratching on the roof and discover a cat, or a family of raccoons. Science does the same on a grander scale. Biologists explain new forms of life by mutation and natural selection. Physicists explain the temperature of water by the average kinetic energy of its molecules, and the orbits of the planets by Kepler's laws.

Most revealing is what we do when we *fail* to find an explanation. We never conclude that there isn't one. We usually even have an explanation of our failure. A murder goes unsolved because the killer was careful: no fingerprints, no witnesses, the body hidden so it would be found only much later. Nobody concludes that the victim died for no reason.

So we reach the question: does *everything* have an explanation, even those we will never find? The thesis that it does is the principle of sufficient reason.

Feser immediately separates PSR from the principle of causality used in earlier chapters. A cause must be distinct from its effect, but a sufficient reason need not be distinct from what it explains, so something could in principle explain itself. And while the principle of causality never claimed that everything has a cause, PSR does claim that everything has a sufficient reason. If something is caused, its reason lies in something else. If something is uncaused, its reason must lie in itself.

### Stage 1, part two: why believe the principle?

**The inductive case.** Treated as an inductive generalization, PSR is as well supported as any claim could be. We find explanations when we look, and when we don't, we usually have good reason to think one exists but is out of reach, perhaps because evidence is missing. Following the philosopher Alexander Pruss, Feser adds that the world simply doesn't look the way it would if PSR were false. If things could happen for no reason, unexplained events would happen constantly, and the world would lack the intelligibility that makes science and common sense work so well. Given how orderly the world is, it would be a miracle if PSR were false.

**Beyond induction: the reductio.** Feser insists that PSR is far more certain than any empirical hypothesis. If it seems hard to prove, that is not because it is doubtful but because it is more obvious than anything we could say for or against it. It can be defended indirectly, by showing that denying it is absurd.

The first step comes from Pruss, developing an idea of Robert Koons: *denying PSR leads to radical skepticism about perception.* If PSR is false, our perceptual experiences might occur for no reason, with no connection to the objects and events we assume cause them. We couldn't even say that such a disconnect is *unlikely*, because objective probabilities depend on the objective tendencies of things, and without PSR events might occur with no regard to any tendencies. So nobody can deny PSR and still be justified in trusting the senses or the science built on them.

Feser then pushes the argument further. A stubborn critic might accept perceptual skepticism and retreat to a priori knowledge such as logic and mathematics. But this retreat offers no shelter. Whenever we accept a claim as rationally justified, we assume we have a good reason for it *and* that this reason is *why* we accept it; we think good considerations move us to believe. We also assume that our minds track truth and the rules of logic. If PSR is false, we have no basis for any of this. Whatever causes our beliefs might have nothing to do with our reasons. Our faculties might produce conclusions unconnected to truth. And it might merely *seem*, for no reason, that we reason well. This applies to any reasons for doubting PSR as much as to anything else. So denying PSR is self-undermining: it destroys the possibility of rational inquiry, including the inquiry that produced the denial.

**Science presupposes PSR.** Some philosophers, including J. L. Mackie and Bertrand Russell, thought we could have real scientific explanations without PSR. We explain phenomena by laws, those laws by deeper laws, and eventually reach fundamental laws that are just brute facts.

Feser thinks this is incoherent, and he uses a simple image. Suppose I tell you a book stays off the floor because it rests on a shelf, but the shelf stays up for no reason at all. Have I explained the book's position? The shelf, by hypothesis, has no tendency of its own to stay aloft, so it has no such tendency to give the book. The "explanation" is an illusion. Saying the book has its own brute tendency doesn't help either; that just moves the illusion somewhere else.

Laws of nature work the same way. If law C is explained by law B, and B by law A, but A is a brute fact, then nothing about A and nothing beyond A accounts for A's working, so A has nothing to give B or C. A chain of laws ending in a brute fact is as incoherent as a tool that isn't the tool of anyone. Feser notes that a series of ever more fundamental laws is like the hierarchical causal series from earlier chapters, such as a stone pushed by a stick pushed by a hand, where each lower member only passes along power it gets from above.

**Della Rocca's challenge.** Michael Della Rocca points out that even philosophers who reject PSR constantly use *explicability arguments*, arguing that something isn't so because otherwise it would be inexplicable. Materialists argue that consciousness must be physical because otherwise it would be inexplicable. Early modern philosophers rejected the Aristotelian idea of substantial form (or a caricature of it) as insufficiently explanatory. Inductive reasoning refuses to treat the future and the unobserved as unlike the past and the observed, because that would make them inexplicable.

Using an explicability argument doesn't by itself commit you to PSR. But apply one to the question of *why things exist*, and you are committed to PSR, because PSR just is the claim that existence must be explained. So the critic has three options:

1. Some explicability arguments are legitimate, but not those about why things exist.
2. No explicability arguments are legitimate.
3. All explicability arguments are legitimate, including those about existence.

Option 3 amounts to accepting PSR. Option 2 is unavailable to anyone who uses explicability arguments elsewhere, and practically everyone in philosophy and science does. That leaves option 1, which cannot be defended without begging the question. Why allow such arguments everywhere except existence? Saying it's just a brute fact that the line falls there assumes the very thing in dispute, since PSR denies brute facts. Appealing to intuition doesn't work either, because the critic's own reliance on explicability arguments everywhere else casts doubt on this one exempting intuition. The conclusion: there is no good reason to think *anything* is explicable unless *everything* is.

Feser goes a step beyond Della Rocca, who allowed that option 2 might be deeply problematic without being strictly incoherent. Feser thinks it *is* incoherent. Even a critic who rejects Della Rocca's particular examples still relies on logically valid and inductively strong patterns of reasoning and rejects fallacies, because the rules of logic embody standards of intelligibility. Many arguments *are* explanations: explanatory demonstrations, arguments to the best explanation, and inductive reasoning in general. Giving up all explicability arguments means giving up argument itself, which makes no sense for someone who argues against PSR. And accepting argument in general while rejecting the specific examples is really option 1 again, which has already failed.

### Stage 1, part three: from the principle to a necessary being

With PSR secured, Feser draws a basic division. A thing's explanation lies either *in something outside it* or *in its own nature*. If outside, the thing is **contingent**: it depends on outside circumstances and would not exist without them. If inside, the thing is **necessary**: something about its nature makes it impossible for it not to exist, so it depends on nothing. There is no third possibility, because a thing explained neither way would be explained by nothing, and PSR rules that out. And contingent things obviously exist: stones, trees, dogs, human beings.

**Could it be contingent all the way down?** Suppose every contingent thing was caused by an earlier contingent thing, with no beginning. This could take several forms. There might be a beginningless universe. There might be an endless cycle of universes, each starting with a Big Bang produced by the Big Crunch of the one before. There might be a beginningless multiverse, with universes branching off from other universes. The details don't matter, because each is an infinite series of contingent things, and Feser grants for argument's sake that such a series is possible.

In that case, it can seem that everything has been explained, since each member is explained by the one before it, and no necessary being is needed. David Hume drew exactly this conclusion in his *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*. Feser says it is an illusion.

**Why does the series exist at all?** The first reply, from Leibniz and Samuel Clarke: even if each member is caused by an earlier member, we can still ask why the *series itself* exists, because it is just as contingent as its members. It could have failed to exist.

In a footnote, Feser recommends against the famous phrasing "why is there something rather than nothing?" It can suggest that there *could* have been nothing at all, and the book's arguments imply otherwise: something purely actual, simple, and absolutely necessary could not have failed to exist. The better question is *why are there any contingent things at all?*

**The composition charge.** Critics say it is fallacious to infer that the series is contingent because its members are. Each stone in a pile might weigh less than an ounce, but the pile doesn't. Feser answers, as he did in the Thomistic chapter, that reasoning from parts to whole is only sometimes fallacious; it depends on the property. It fails for weight, but it works for color: build anything out of red Lego blocks and the result is red. Contingency is like color. Three stones are no less contingent than one, and three million no less than three. Collections are, if anything, *more* obviously contingent, in fact **doubly contingent**. Each stone depends on things like the laws of physics keeping its atoms together, and the collection depends on all of that *plus* the stones being gathered as they are. It would be silly to think that the collection of *all* contingent things suddenly stops being contingent.

**Leibniz's geometry books.** Feser also denies Hume's assumption that once every member has an immediate cause, everything has been explained. He borrows Leibniz's example: an infinite series of geometry books, each copied from an earlier copy. Each book has an immediate cause, but important questions remain. Why does the series have this content? Why geometry rather than Shakespeare, a coloring book, or an auto-repair manual copied forever? In the same way, an infinitely old series of contingent things leaves us asking why it consists of these kinds of things (stones, trees, dogs, people, planets, stars, galaxies) rather than others, and why the universe, the cycle of universes, or the multiverse is governed by these particular laws.

**Passing the buck.** Someone might agree that the series needs a cause beyond itself but say that cause could be contingent. Then it needs a cause too, and so does any further contingent cause. Positing a second, higher-order infinite series of contingent causes only relocates the problem, because whatever forced us beyond the first series applies equally to the second, then a third, then a fourth, and even to an infinite series of infinite series. Each move just passes the explanatory buck. So, as Leibniz and Clarke concluded, given PSR there must be a cause outside the series that is *necessary*: something that by its nature cannot fail to exist and depends on nothing. Nothing less can end the regress.

### Stage 1, part four: the here-and-now route

Feser then shows that the argument doesn't depend on asking about the collection of contingent things as a whole. Grant the infinite past again. Now pick any single contingent thing, a particular tree, say, and ask: *why does it continue to exist at this moment, rather than vanishing?* Pointing to whatever produced it long ago doesn't answer the question. Because it is contingent, nothing in its nature guarantees its existence, and that is as true now, and at every moment, as it was when it first came to be.

So, given PSR, something must be causing it to exist here and now. If that cause is contingent, the same question arises about it, which produces a regress of *simultaneous* causes. That regress has the same problem as the regress into the past: taken as a whole, the series of present sustainers needs an explanation from outside itself just as much as the tree did. The only way to end the regress is a cause that exists necessarily, by its own nature.

This version of the argument reaches a necessary being without saying anything about the universe as a whole or the multiverse. It needs only the question of what explains one ordinary thing existing right now.

### How this proof fits the others

Feser notes that this second route resembles the Aristotelian, Neo-Platonic, and Thomistic proofs, and he presents the proofs as complementing one another. The *deep reason* things are contingent, needing outside causes both to come into being and to keep existing, is that they mix actuality and potentiality, are made of parts, and have an essence distinct from their existence. The *deep reason* such things need causes at all is that otherwise they would be unintelligible, and PSR says everything is intelligible.

So the rationalist idea can be developed in several ways: arguing that the whole collection of contingent things needs an outside cause; arguing that each individual contingent thing needs a cause at every moment; or, borrowing from the earlier proofs, arguing that anything with potentiality, parts, or a real distinction between essence and existence needs a cause at every moment. Whichever route one takes, the argument begins with PSR and ends at an absolutely necessary being.

### Stage 2: the necessary being is God

Why call the necessary being God? Start with what necessity demands.

- **It must be purely actual.** If it had potentials needing actualization, its existence would depend on whatever actualizes them, so it would not be necessary after all.
- **It must be absolutely simple.** If it had parts, it would depend on something combining them.
- **Its essence must be identical with its existence.** Otherwise it would need a cause to give its essence existence.

So a necessary being in the strictest sense is purely actual, without parts, and subsistent existence itself.

Earlier chapters argued that there cannot, even in principle, be more than one such thing. So there is **only one necessary being**. If we reasoned from the whole collection of contingent things, its necessary cause is unique. If we reasoned from what sustains one contingent thing and noticed that every other contingent thing needs a necessary sustainer for the same reason, it must be the same necessary being sustaining them all. Since everything else is contingent and caused by this being, it is **the cause of everything other than itself**.

The earlier chapters also showed that what is purely actual, what is absolutely simple, what is a necessarily existing intellect, and what is subsistent existence itself are one and the same reality, reached from different starting points. So everything already established about that reality applies to the necessary being: it is immutable, eternal, immaterial, incorporeal, perfect, omnipotent, fully good, intelligent, and omniscient. For such a being to exist is exactly what it means for God to exist.

## The Formal Argument, Step by Step

Feser restates the argument as 27 numbered steps. Here they are, paraphrased, each with a short justification.

1. **PSR says that whatever exists has an explanation of its existence and its attributes.** This is the principle being defended, distinct from the principle of causality.
2. **If PSR were false, things and events with no evident explanation would be extremely common.** Nothing would keep brute events rare.
3. **But common sense and science find the opposite.** We find explanations, or know why we can't.
4. **If PSR were false, we couldn't trust our own cognitive faculties.** Perception might be disconnected from reality, and beliefs from reasons.
5. **But we can trust them.** Every argument, including arguments against PSR, depends on this.
6. **There is no principled way to deny PSR while accepting that science and philosophy give genuine explanations.** This is Della Rocca's dilemma, and brute-fact laws explain nothing.
7. **But science and philosophy do give many genuine explanations.** Critics of PSR accept this.
8. **So PSR is true.** This follows from any of the three pairs of premises: 2–3, 4–5, or 6–7.
9. **A thing's existence is explained either by another thing that causes it (so it is contingent) or by its own nature (so it is necessary). PSR rules out a third option of explanation by nothing.** The explanation is either inside or outside, and "neither" means "none."
10. **Contingent things exist.** Stones, trees, dogs, people.
11. **Even if every contingent thing were explained by an earlier one, forever, the existence of the whole infinite series would still need explaining.** The series is itself contingent (the Leibniz–Clarke point; the geometry books).
12. **Explaining the series by some further contingent cause, and that cause by another, and so on, just creates another series needing explanation, and repeating the move repeats the problem.** This is passing the explanatory buck.
13. **So no contingent thing or series of contingent things can explain why there are any contingent things at all.** From 11 and 12.
14. **But by PSR, there must be an explanation of why there are contingent things, and the only one left is a necessary being as cause.** From 8, 9, and 13, by elimination.
15. **Also, an individual contingent thing's continuing to exist at any moment needs an explanation, and since it is contingent, that explanation must be some simultaneous cause distinct from it.** Past causes don't explain present existence.
16. **If that cause is contingent, then even with an infinite chain of further contingent simultaneous causes, we have an infinite series whose existence is still unexplained.** It has the same problem as the temporal series.
17. **So no contingent thing or series explains why a particular contingent thing continues to exist; the only remaining explanation is a necessary being as its simultaneous cause.** From 9, 15, and 16.
18. **So at least one necessary being exists, explaining both why any contingent things exist and how each continues to exist.** Combines 14 and 17.
19. **A necessary being must be purely actual, absolutely simple, and subsistent existence itself.** Any potential, part, or received existence would make it dependent.
20. **In principle there can be only one thing that is purely actual, absolutely simple, and subsistent existence itself.** Shown in earlier chapters: nothing could distinguish two such beings.
21. **So there is only one necessary being.** From 19 and 20.
22. **So this one necessary being both explains why any contingent things exist and causes each contingent thing's existence at every moment.** From 18 and 21.
23. **So it is the cause of everything other than itself.** Everything else is contingent and depends on it.
24. **What is purely actual, simple, and subsistent existence must also be immutable, eternal, immaterial, incorporeal, perfect, omnipotent, fully good, intelligent, and omniscient.** Argued in earlier chapters.
25. **So there is a necessary being that is one, purely actual, simple, subsistent existence, the cause of all else, and has all these attributes.** Collects 18–24.
26. **For such a being to exist is for God to exist.** This is what classical theism means by God.
27. **So God exists.**

Notice the structure. Steps 1–8 give three independent defenses of PSR, so if one fails the others still stand. Steps 9–18 give two independent routes to a necessary being, one from the whole series and one from present existence. Steps 19–27 connect this chapter to the rest of the book.

## Objections and Replies

### 1. "Explain each member and you've explained the whole" (Hume)

*Objection:* In an infinite series, each thing is explained by its predecessor, so nothing is left to explain.

*Reply:* Three things remain unexplained: why the series exists at all, since it is as contingent as its members; why it has its particular character, its kinds of things and its laws (the geometry books); and why any member exists right now. Supplying immediate causes doesn't answer these.

### 2. "That's the fallacy of composition"

*Objection:* The members being contingent doesn't make the whole contingent, just as bricks weighing a pound each don't make a one-pound wall.

*Reply:* Part-to-whole reasoning fails for weight but succeeds for color. Contingency is like color, and a collection is doubly contingent, depending on its members and on their arrangement. Besides, the here-and-now route doesn't reason about the whole at all.

### 3. "Maybe the series has a contingent cause outside it"

*Reply:* Then that cause needs a cause, and positing higher-order series only relocates the problem, even if there are infinitely many of them. Only a necessary being ends the regress.

### 4. "The fundamental laws of nature are just brute facts" (Mackie, Russell)

*Reply:* A brute base has nothing to pass on, like a shelf floating for no reason, which can't explain why the book on it stays up. A chain of laws is like a hierarchical causal series and must end in something that explains itself, or none of it explains anything. The brute-fact view thus undermines the science it means to protect.

### 5. "I'll accept skepticism about perception"

*Reply:* Retreating to a priori knowledge doesn't help. Without PSR, nothing ensures that our reasons cause our beliefs or that our faculties track truth, and that applies to logic and mathematics too, and to the case against PSR.

### 6. "Explanation is fine within the world, but not for existence itself"

*Reply:* This is Della Rocca's option 1, and it is arbitrary. "It's a brute fact that the line is here" begs the question, and intuition is undermined by the critic's own use of explicability arguments elsewhere.

### 7. "Then I'll reject all explicability arguments"

*Reply:* That means abandoning argument itself, since logic embodies standards of intelligibility and many arguments are explanations. Anyone who argues against PSR can't coherently do this.

### 8. "It's rationalism: innate ideas and armchair knowledge"

*Reply:* The argument assumes none of that. It is "rationalist" only in denying brute facts and holding that reality is intelligible through and through. Nor does it accept every thesis defended in PSR's name. Specifically, it rejects the idea that propositions need explaining like concrete things, and the idea that explanations must logically entail what they explain.

### 9. The big conjunction of contingent truths (Ross, van Inwagen, Rowe)

*Objection:* Take the single proposition made by joining all true contingent propositions. If a necessary truth explained it, it would be entailed by a necessary truth and so would itself be necessary; everything would become necessary, which is often called "modal collapse." If a contingent truth explained it, that truth would already be part of the conjunction, so the conjunction would explain itself, which nothing contingent can do. So something lacks an explanation, and PSR is false.

*Reply, part one:* This treats propositions as Platonic objects existing "out there" like rocks. For the Scholastic realist, abstract objects exist only in minds. What needs explaining are concrete things and their attributes. Once you explain the concrete triangles and the minds (including God's) that grasp triangularity, nothing is left over, and the same goes for propositions.

*Reply, part two:* The objection assumes that explanation means logical entailment. It doesn't. As Pruss notes, scientific explanations generally don't entail what they explain. The sun's gravity explains the planets' elliptical orbits, yet other gravitational influences could have interfered. PSR requires only that an explanation make things intelligible.

### 10. "We can conceive of something happening for no reason" (Hume)

*Reply:* This repeats a Humean objection answered in chapter 1. Imagining something appearing without *also imagining its cause* is not imagining it as *having no cause*, just as thinking of a three-sided figure without thinking "triangle" doesn't show it could fail to be a triangle.

### 11. "Quantum mechanics shows things happen for no reason"

*Reply:* Whether or not quantum events have causes, they plainly have explanations: they presuppose, and are made intelligible by, the laws of quantum mechanics. And the quantum critic still owes Della Rocca an answer about where to draw the line between legitimate and illegitimate explanatory demands.

### 12. "PSR faces puzzles, so the burden is on its defenders"

*Reply:* Puzzles aimed at particular formulations don't touch formulations that lack the problematic assumptions, and they don't answer the positive arguments that PSR is true and that even critics presuppose it. The burden is on the critic to show how PSR can be *coherently* rejected.

### 13. "Why not say the universe itself is the necessary being?"

*Reply:* A strictly necessary being must be purely actual and simple. The universe changes, so it has potentials being actualized, and it has many parts. So it can't be necessary.

### 14. "Numbers and propositions are necessary, and they aren't God"

*Reply (a footnote):* As argued in chapter 3, abstract objects exist only in minds. If they are necessary, their necessity is derivative, flowing from an infinite mind that thinks them.

### 15. "What caused God? If God needs no explanation, neither does the universe"

*Reply:* The argument doesn't say everything has a cause, and it makes no exception to "everything has an explanation." God's existence is explained by his nature as pure act, simplicity, and subsistent existence. The universe's nature can't explain its existence. So the contrast is not explained versus unexplained but *self-explanatory versus explained by another*, and it rests on principled distinctions: act versus potency, simple versus composite, existence itself versus an essence that receives existence. And self-explanatory does not mean self-caused.

### 16. A modern worry: doesn't God's explaining the world make the world necessary?

This is not discussed in chapter 5, but the chapter supplies the answer. Explanation means making something intelligible, not entailing it. A free choice can be fully intelligible in light of the chooser's reasons without being forced. The book's sixth chapter argues that nothing inside or outside God compels him to create one world rather than another, so his will is free. His creative act explains the world without making it necessary.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **"PSR means everything has a cause."** No. It means everything has an explanation, which may be the thing's own nature.
- **"The argument needs the universe to have a beginning."** No. Feser grants an infinite past, whether a beginningless universe, endless cycles, or a multiverse.
- **"It depends on reasoning about the universe as a whole."** The composition charge fails anyway, and the here-and-now route never mentions the whole.
- **"God is self-caused."** No. God is self-explanatory. Being caused means having a potential actualized, which pure act cannot have.
- **"Rationalist means innate ideas."** No. The argument's only rationalism is PSR.
- **"PSR is just a rule of thumb."** It has inductive support, but its main defense is that denying it undermines all reasoning.
- **"A necessary being exists by definition."** No. Its necessity is metaphysical, grounded in pure actuality, simplicity, and the identity of essence and existence. It is not a matter of what a word means, and the argument does not rely on the ontological argument.
- **"A necessary being could be the universe, the laws of physics, or numbers."** Stage 2 rules these out.
- **"This is just Leibniz's argument."** Feser's version uses a Thomist PSR (intelligibility, not entailment), a stronger defense of PSR, an additional here-and-now route, and a Stage 2 grounded in the metaphysics of the earlier proofs.

## How to Explain It to Others

**The simple version.** Everything has an explanation. We assume that in daily life and science, and if we denied it, we couldn't trust our own thinking. Most things are explained by something else; their existence is, so to speak, on loan. But a chain of loans, even an endless one, never explains why there is any existence to lend. So something must exist by its very nature. Such a thing can't change or have parts, because anything that does depends on something else, and that rules out the universe. There can be only one such thing, and it is the source of everything else. That is what classical theists mean by God.

**The best analogy: the endless chain of IOUs.** You need a hundred dollars. You borrow it from A, who borrows it from B, who borrows it from C, forever. Does any money ever arrive? Only if someone in the chain *has* money without borrowing it. Making the chain longer, even infinitely long, only multiplies debts. Adding a second, "higher" chain of lenders changes nothing, because they are borrowers too. Contingent things have their existence on loan, from their causes and from whatever sustains them right now. Only something that *owns* its existence by nature can explain why there is any existence to go around.

Two tips for using the analogy. First, stress that the loans are happening *right now*, not just in the distant past, so "the chain goes back forever" is no answer. Second, use it against "What caused God?": the rich lender doesn't need a loan, and that isn't an exception to how money works but the only way anyone gets paid.

**A second image for PSR: the floating shelf.** If a shelf stays up for no reason at all, it can't explain why the book on it stays up. If the deepest laws of nature are brute facts, they can't really explain anything built on them.

**Traps to avoid.** Don't say "everything has a cause." Don't lean on the Big Bang. Don't skip the defense of PSR. Don't stop at "a necessary being" without showing why it must be God. Don't say God exists "by definition." Don't claim the argument proves any particular religion.

## Key Takeaways

- PSR says everything has an explanation. It differs from the principle of causality because a reason can be a thing's own nature, while a cause must be something else.
- PSR has inductive support, but its main defense is a reductio: denying it undermines trust in perception, then reasoning itself, and makes scientific explanation illusory.
- A chain of laws ending in a brute fact explains nothing, like a book on a floating shelf.
- Della Rocca's dilemma: you can't accept explanations everywhere but exempt existence without arbitrariness. Feser adds that rejecting all explanatory reasoning means rejecting argument.
- Everything is either contingent (explained by another) or necessary (explained by itself). PSR excludes "explained by nothing."
- Even an infinite series of contingent things leaves unexplained why the series exists, why it has these kinds and laws, and why anything exists right now.
- Contingency transfers from parts to whole like color, not weight. Collections are doubly contingent.
- Contingent causes outside the series only pass the explanatory buck.
- The here-and-now route reaches a necessary being from one thing's present existence.
- A strictly necessary being must be purely actual, simple, and existence itself, so it is unique, the cause of all else, and has all the divine attributes.
- Feser's Thomist PSR asks only for intelligibility, not entailment, and doesn't require explaining abstract propositions, which defuses the big-conjunction objection.
- The universe can't be the necessary being because it changes and has parts.
- God isn't an exception to PSR. He is self-explanatory, not self-caused.

## Ten Review Questions with Brief Answers

1. **What is the principle of sufficient reason?** The principle that whatever exists has an explanation of its existence and attributes, whether or not we find it.

2. **Give two ways PSR differs from the principle of causality.** A cause must be distinct from its effect, while a reason can be the thing's own nature. And PSR says everything has a reason, while the causal principle doesn't say everything has a cause.

3. **Why is denying PSR self-undermining?** Without PSR, we have no grounds for thinking our beliefs are caused by our reasons or that our faculties track truth, and that includes whatever reasons we have for denying PSR.

4. **What does the book-and-shelf example show?** That a brute, unexplained foundation can't pass explanatory power up the chain, so laws resting on a brute fact explain nothing.

5. **What are Della Rocca's three options, and which is left for the critic?** Some, none, or all explicability arguments are legitimate. Only "some" is left, and it is arbitrary or question-begging.

6. **Why isn't there a third option besides contingent and necessary?** A thing explained neither by its nature nor by anything else would be explained by nothing, which PSR forbids.

7. **How does Feser answer the fallacy-of-composition charge?** Contingency is like color, not weight: it carries from parts to whole, and collections are doubly contingent. The here-and-now route doesn't reason about wholes at all.

8. **What does the here-and-now route ask?** What keeps a particular contingent thing existing at this moment. Its past cause doesn't answer that, and a regress of contingent sustainers must end in a necessary being.

9. **Why must a necessary being be purely actual, simple, and existence itself?** Potentials, parts, or an essence distinct from existence would each make it depend on something else, and so make it contingent.

10. **How does Feser answer the big-conjunction objection?** Propositions exist only in minds and don't need separate explanation, and explanation requires making something intelligible, not logically entailing it (the sun's gravity and elliptical orbits).
