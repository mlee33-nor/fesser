# The Nature of God, Part II: Power, Knowledge, Goodness, Will, Love — and How God Relates to the World

*A study guide to the second half of Chapter 6 of Edward Feser's* Five Proofs of the Existence of God *(Ignatius Press, 2017), printed pages 205–248.*

## 1. Orientation

Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* defends five classic arguments for God: the Aristotelian proof from change, the Neo-Platonic proof from composition, the Augustinian proof from abstract objects, the Thomistic proof from the distinction between essence and existence, and the rationalist proof from the principle of sufficient reason. Each argument ends at the same destination: a cause of the world that is **purely actual** (it has no unrealized potential), **absolutely simple** (it has no parts of any kind), **subsistent existence itself** (it does not *have* existence; it *is* existence), **absolutely necessary**, and **unique**. Everything else, by contrast, is composite, a mixture of actuality and potentiality, something whose essence is distinct from its existence, and contingent — and it depends on that first cause for its existence *at every moment*, not merely at some starting point long ago.

Chapter 6 asks: what else can we know about this first cause? The first half of the chapter (covered in a separate study guide) introduced three tools and derived God's unity, simplicity, immutability, immateriality, and eternity. This guide covers the **second half**: God's **omnipotence**, **omniscience**, **perfect goodness**, **will** and its **freedom**, **love**, and **incomprehensibility**; then God's **relationship to the world** — **conservation**, **concurrence**, and **miracles** — and finally a short appendix asking **whether God is male**.

In the course this guide belongs to, this is module "Chapter 6 · Part B." It is where the abstract "God of the philosophers" is shown to be a personal, knowing, loving God, and where the most popular objections to classical theism are met head-on.

## 2. Why this chapter matters

There are three reasons this part of the book is so important.

**First, it closes the gap objection.** A skeptic often says: "Even if you prove a first cause, you haven't proved *God* — an all-powerful, all-knowing, good, personal being." This chapter answers that objection directly. It shows, step by step, that the first cause the proofs reach must have power, intellect, knowledge, goodness, will, and love.

**Second, it is where the hardest popular objections live.** Can God make a stone too heavy for him to lift? Doesn't God's knowledge of the future destroy free will? If God causes everything, doesn't he cause evil? Can an unchanging God really love? Must God create the best possible world? Aren't miracles violations of the laws of nature? Feser answers every one of these.

**Third, it introduces the single most useful idea for explaining classical theism: God is not a cause among causes.** He is not the biggest, strongest item in the universe, competing with other things for causal room. He is the source of the existence and causal power of everything. Feser's favorite image is that God relates to the world as an **author relates to a story**, not as one character relates to another. Once this idea sinks in, objection after objection loses its force, because most of them quietly picture God as a very powerful character inside the story.

## 3. Key concepts and vocabulary

**Pure actuality.** Actuality is the way a thing actually is; potentiality is the way it could be but isn't yet. Everything we experience is a mix of both. God, the proofs argue, is pure actuality: nothing in him is waiting to be realized.

**Principle of proportionate causality.** Whatever is in an effect must be in its total cause in some way. The feature can be in the cause *formally* (the cause has the same feature — I hand you a $20 bill from my wallet), *virtually* (the cause has the power to produce it — I transfer $20 from my bank account), or *eminently* (the cause has it in a higher way — I control a printing press that makes $20 bills). A cause cannot give what it doesn't in some way have.

**Agere sequitur esse ("action follows being").** What a thing does reflects, and cannot outrun, what it is. A stone can't digest food, because digesting goes beyond a stone's nature. And since *existing* comes before *acting*, a thing's ability to act can be no more independent than its ability to exist.

**Analogy.** A word can be used *univocally* (in exactly the same sense: "Fido is a dog," "Rover is a dog"), *equivocally* (in unrelated senses: a baseball bat and a flying bat), or *analogically* (in related but not identical senses: "good wine," "a good man"). The **analogy of attribution** applies a term first to one thing (the primary analogate) and to others because of their relation to it — health belongs primarily to an organism, and food is "healthy" because it causes health. The **analogy of proper proportionality** applies a term literally to several things because each stands in a similar relation — the eye is to a tree as the intellect is to a mathematical truth, so we "see" in both cases. Positive words about God are used analogically: literally, but not in exactly the creaturely sense.

**Fallacy of accident.** Mistaking something that *usually accompanies* a thing in our experience for something *essential* to it. Every college professor who has ever lived was under nine feet tall, but being short isn't part of being a professor. Feser repeatedly finds this fallacy in objections that God can't act, choose, or love without changing.

**Privation.** The absence of something a thing *ought* to have, given its nature. Blindness in a dog is a privation; a stone's not seeing is not. A tree with rotten roots has a privation; a tree without wings does not.

**Occasionalism, mere conservationism, concurrentism.** Three views of how God relates to created causes. *Occasionalism*: only God causes anything; creatures are just occasions for his action. *Mere conservationism*: God keeps creatures in existence, but they act on their own. *Concurrentism* (Feser's view): creatures are real causes, but secondary ones — they act only as God cooperates, or "concurs," with their action.

**Conservation.** God keeps everything in existence at every instant. Without his causing, the world would not wind down; it would simply cease.

**Existential inertia.** A rival thesis: some things, once they exist, just keep existing on their own until something destroys them.

**Supernatural, preternatural, subnatural.** A *supernatural* effect is beyond the power of any created nature, so only God can produce it. A *preternatural* effect is extraordinary but within the power of some created being, such as an angel. A *subnatural* event is a defect, something falling short of a nature.

**Limit case.** A term Feser borrows from Barry Miller. A mere *limit* differs only in degree from what it limits (the speed of light is the top speed). A *limit case* differs absolutely, yet the series points to it: a speed of zero is not the slowest speed but no speed at all. God's will and goodness are limit cases of the appetites and goodness we find in creatures.

## 4. The argument as a story

### Chapter one of the story: all power is borrowed power

Start with what the proofs gave us. Nothing other than God can exist even for an instant unless God is causing it to exist. Now apply "action follows being." If a thing cannot even *exist* on its own, where could it get the ability to *act* on its own? Existing is more basic than acting; acting presupposes existing. So everything depends on God at every instant not only for being but for every power it exercises. The sun's power to melt ice, your power to lift a cup, a magnet's power to attract iron — all of it is borrowed. Something that is the source of all power, with nothing outside the reach of its power, is **omnipotent**.

Feser also runs the argument the other way. God just *is* existence, existing in the fullest possible way. To act is to actualize some potential, so power is the ability to actualize potential. Since action follows being, what exists in the fullest way can act in the fullest way. If there were some potential that could be actualized but God couldn't actualize it, what could possibly stop him? Nothing. So God is omnipotent again, by a second road.

Does that mean God can do literally anything? He can cause anything that could possibly exist or happen. He cannot make a round square or make two plus two equal five — but not because his power runs out. There simply is no such thing as a "power to make round squares." The phrase names nothing. The famous **paradox of the stone** falls to the same point. A stone's rising is always something that can happen, so God can always make any stone rise. "A stone too heavy for God to lift" is as incoherent as "a round square." And the fact that God cannot sin is no more a limit than his inability to make round squares, because "perfectly good yet sinning" is also a contradiction.

### Chapter two: there is a mind at the root of things

Now take proportionate causality: a cause can't give what it doesn't in some way have. Every feature of every thing that exists or could exist — colors, shapes, chemical make-ups, the powers of plants and animals, the minds of humans and angels — depends on God, so each must be in God somehow.

It can't be in God the way it is in things. God is immaterial, so he isn't literally red or round. But Feser adds a subtler reason: even if God were material, if he were just one more red thing, his redness would be one more instance of redness that needs explaining. He wouldn't be the *ultimate* explanation of red things.

Here Feser draws on chapter 3. Universals — redness, roundness, catness — are real. If they don't exist only in their concrete instances, there are just two other options: they exist as ideas in a mind, or in a Platonic "third realm" of abstract forms. Chapter 3 argued there is no such realm. So the universal patterns of all things must be in God as **concepts in an intellect**. God has a mind.

Not just concepts but whole thoughts. God causes not only the cat and the mat but the cat's *being on* the mat. A proposition — "the cat is on the mat" — is to a state of affairs what a concept is to a universal: its counterpart in the mind. So every state of affairs that is or could be exists in God's intellect as a proposition, including the thought that it is true that the cat is on the mat and false that unicorns exist.

### Chapter three: the author who cannot be wrong

Picture an author who conceives an entire story in one instantaneous flash. At that instant, he can't be mistaken about whether a character is in the story, because the character is there only because he put her there. God is like that. Everything about the world is as it is because he is causing it, so he can't be mistaken about it. A human author might later forget his story — but only because he lives in time, passes from one thought to another, depends on a brain that can fail, and is subject to outside forces. None of that applies to God, who is eternal, unchanging, bodiless, and all-powerful.

Philosophers often say someone knows a claim when they believe it, it's true, and they believe it through a reliable process. Apply this, analogically, to God. He has the thought that the cat is on the mat. It's true — because he is making it true. And there is no more reliable way of "finding out" whether something is true than being the one who makes it true. So God **knows**. The same goes for every truth about everything that is or could be. And God knows himself perfectly too, since an author can't know less about his own creative act than about his story. (A human author might not know what's happening in his stomach, but that's because he's made of parts; God is simple.) Knowing everything about himself and everything else, God is **omniscient**.

Here is the crucial point. An author doesn't know his characters by *watching* them. He knows them by knowing his own intentions. God knows the world the same way. He doesn't observe history unfolding — he isn't in time, has no senses, and can't learn anything, since learning is a change. In one timeless act he causes everything that ever was or will be, and in knowing himself as doing so, he knows it all. **God's knowledge of the world is a consequence of his self-knowledge.**

### Chapter four: how one simple act can know many things

God is simple, so there can't be a crowd of separate thoughts in him. Even imagining one giant thought containing every truth won't quite work, since a giant list still has parts. Feser offers two images. White light contains all the colors of the rainbow in a unified way; a prism draws them out as separate beams. A lump of dough contains countless possible cookie shapes; cutters draw out a star, a circle, a heart. If you knew the white light perfectly, you'd know every color it could yield; if you knew the dough perfectly, every shape. God is unlimited actuality; every creature is a limited way of being actual — actual *as a stone*, *as a tree*, *as a dog*. By knowing himself perfectly, God knows every limited way of being that could be drawn from him. (The images are imperfect: creatures aren't made *out of* God the way cookies are made of dough.)

### Chapter five: goodness is fullness of being

What does "good" mean? Feser argues it is objective. Picture a triangle hastily drawn on the cracked plastic seat of a moving bus, with gaps and wobbly sides, next to one drawn carefully with a ruler and pen. The second is a better triangle — not because we happen to like straight lines, but because it comes closer to what a triangle is. Or picture a tree whose nature is to sink roots, draw up water, grow leaves, and photosynthesize. A diseased tree that can't do these things is to that extent a bad tree. "Good" here means a good *specimen*: a thing that lives up to the standard built into its kind. Moral goodness is a special case: it appears when free, rational creatures choose to act in ways that help or hinder the flourishing their nature calls for.

Notice the pattern. Goodness is always *being actual* in the ways a nature calls for. Badness is always a *failure* to be actual in those ways — a **privation**. A triangle missing a fourth side isn't defective, because triangles shouldn't have four sides; a triangle with a wavy side is. A tree lacking wings is fine; a tree lacking strong roots is not. So good and bad aren't equal partners. Goodness is primary; badness is a lack of goodness, as blindness is a lack of sight.

Now the conclusion is almost immediate. If goodness is actuality and badness is unrealized potential, then God — **pure actuality** with no unrealized potential — is perfectly good, with no badness at all. And by proportionate causality, all the good in the world must come from God, so he is the source of all goodness.

But doesn't proportionate causality mean evil is in God too? No — because evil isn't a *thing*. If I draw two sides of a triangle and stop halfway through the third, the defect isn't something extra I added; it's what I left out. God doesn't make two kinds of things, good ones and bad ones. He makes good things and refrains from making certain other goods. And refraining can be wise: maybe I left the triangle unfinished to make a point in a lecture, and the understanding my students gained outweighed the trivial flaw in the drawing. Some goods even *require* some badness to be permitted. There can't be courage unless people face real danger and do the right thing anyway. God can't make courage without danger any more than he can make a round square. (Feser notes that the saints in heaven keep the courage they won on earth, as an old soldier keeps the courage he showed in battle long ago.) The full problem of evil comes in chapter 7.

### Chapter six: a free will, and love

Everything strives toward its fulfillment. A tree does so without awareness. A dog does so consciously, seeking food and water — the tradition calls this *sensory appetite*. Human beings do so rationally, pursuing what they understand to be worth pursuing — *rational appetite*, or **will**. Since God has an intellect, he has something analogous to will. He doesn't strive to realize his nature — he is already fully actual — so he is more like something that has completed its striving: the limit case of rational appetite. And since he grasps every possible thing yet causes only some of them to exist, there must be in him something analogous to choosing.

That will is **free**. Nothing outside God can force him, because everything outside God depends on him. And nothing inside him forces him either. Both the concept of a lion and the concept of a unicorn are in his intellect, and neither concept requires that its kind exist or not exist. Before creation, a world with unicorns was just as possible as a world with lions.

Finally, **love**. We tend to think love is a feeling. But what makes love real is that the lover *wills the good* of the beloved. Love that is only warm feelings rising and falling is shallow; love that keeps willing the other's good when the feelings fade is serious. That's why "tough love" is love, and why loving an enemy is possible. Now, creating something is actualizing it — willing that it exist along with everything good that flows from its nature. When God makes trees, he wills that they exist and that they have what is good for trees: roots drinking water, leaves turning sunlight into food. So God's creating is itself his **loving**.

### Chapter seven: knowable, but never fully grasped

Can we comprehend God? In a loose sense, yes — the whole book shows it. Much of what we know is negative: God is not made of parts, not changeable, not material, not in space or time. That is real knowledge. And positive words like "powerful" and "good" apply literally, by analogy. God is the *primary* bearer of power; the sun's power to melt ice is borrowed. A tree is to its goodness as a virtuous person is to his, and as an angel is to his, and as God is to his — each goodness very different, the tree's growing slowly and unconsciously, the person's through choices made sometimes against disordered desires, the angel's through choice but not over time. God's goodness is even more different: a good angel could have chosen evil, but God, being pure actuality, cannot fail to be good. His goodness is the limit case.

But in the *strict* sense, we can't comprehend God, and simplicity guarantees it. We understand things deeply by learning their parts and how they fit together, or by placing them in a category and naming what makes them distinctive. Neither works for something with no parts at all. God is hard to understand *because* he is simple. That sounds paradoxical until you remember that "simple" here means "without parts," not "easy." This isn't a cop-out. Physics tells us of things that behave like both waves and particles and of space itself being curved — strange ideas, because physics has traveled far from everyday experience. Natural theology goes deeper still, to the deepest level of reality, and the arguments themselves tell us our grasp will be thin.

### Chapter eight: the world hangs on God right now

The proofs showed that nothing could stay in existence for an instant unless God sustained it — the doctrine of **conservation**. Creation isn't a one-time event long ago; it is happening now. Some philosophers suggest **existential inertia** instead: maybe things just keep existing on their own. Feser replies that no one ever explains what feature of a contingent thing would give it this remarkable power, and in fact nothing composite could have it, because whatever is a mix of potential and actual, or of essence and existence, needs a cause of its existence here and now. Suggesting inertia doesn't answer the arguments; it ignores them. (And the universe can't be the necessary being, since it is composite and changing.)

Yet created things are not God. They are composite; he is simple. They are part potential; he is pure act. They are contingent; he is necessary. So **pantheism** (God is the world) is false, and so is **panentheism** (God is not the world but is changed by it), since God is unchangeable.

### Chapter nine: real causes that act only with God

How do God and creatures share the work of causing? Consider the iced tea sitting in the sun, its ice cubes melting. The **occasionalist** says the sun does nothing; God melts the ice whenever the sun is out. The same with billiards: God moves the eight ball when the cue ball touches it. The **mere conservationist** says God keeps the sun in existence, but the sun melts the ice on its own.

Feser rejects both. Against occasionalism: action follows being, so something that truly did nothing would truly be nothing. Then only God would exist. But you know you exist, and you know you're not God — you move from thought to thought, so you change; you doubt, so you're not all-knowing; you can't make your experiences stop, so you're not all-powerful. Worse, occasionalism destroys the path that led to God. We reasoned from real things that really change and really are composite. Denying that they are real is like climbing a ladder carefully and then blasting it out from under yourself with a ray gun.

Against mere conservationism: by the same principle, if something could act independently of God, it could exist independently of him. That is really deism — a God whose creatures could run without him. But nothing composite can exist on its own for a single instant. And this view kicks the ladder away too.

The truth is **concurrentism**. Creatures are *real* causes: they make a genuine contribution to what happens. They are *secondary* causes: without God's cooperation, they would do nothing. Alfred Freddoso's example: draw a square on a chalkboard with blue chalk. You are why there's a square at all; the chalk is why it's blue. Use red chalk or a pencil and the result changes, so the chalk really contributes — but without your hand, nothing happens. Or consider the moon: how it looks depends on its own surface, but without the sun it gives no light at all.

What about human freedom? David Oderberg's example: a father teaches his little son to write the letter A by guiding his hand. The boy can resist or cooperate, and his choice makes a real difference. But no A appears without the father's guidance. That is how God's concurrence relates to our free choices.

### Chapter ten: miracles without violations

Since David Hume, people often define a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, and skeptics argue that evidence of a "violation" would really just show the supposed law wasn't a law. Feser rejects the definition. On the Aristotelian view, a law of nature describes what a thing tends to do *because of what it is*. Water's freezing at 32°F is just water's natural tendency to freeze at that temperature — and tendencies can be blocked (water can stay liquid below freezing in unusual conditions) without any law being broken. As Oderberg puts it, "The laws of nature are the laws of natures." So laws are not arbitrary rules that could be swapped out. For Hume, water could have turned into chocolate mousse at 32°F, acorns could grow into dogs, dogs could fly by wagging their tails, if only the laws were different. For the Aristotelian, anything that reliably turned into mousse at 32°F simply wouldn't be water. Given that water exists, its laws are necessary — and so genuine violations are impossible.

So what is a miracle? It is God raising a thing to a power its nature can't account for. Defects fall *below* nature; miracles go *beyond* it. Because the cause lies outside the natural order altogether, only God can work a miracle — not even an angel, whose extraordinary deeds would only be preternatural, since angels are creatures too.

And God doesn't "intervene." The world isn't an airplane on autopilot with God occasionally grabbing the controls. He is the cause of the ordinary course of things just as much as of miracles, and the ordinary course is actually the clearest evidence of him. As the philosopher Herbert McCabe argued, you can only interfere with something you stand alongside, and the cause of everything stands alongside nothing. Better pictures: the world is music and God the musician — the natural order is his playing from the score; a miracle is a jazz improvisation. Every note, written or improvised, was always his. Or the world is a film and miracles are the director's cut; or a novel, and miracles are new material in the second edition. In each case the original was complete and good; the addition raises it higher rather than repairing it.

Feser adds that miracles can also *subtract*. In the story of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego thrown into the fiery furnace, what matters is what does *not* happen: they aren't burned. Isn't that a violation of the law that fire burns? No. Following Oderberg, it's a **suspension**, not a **breach** — like a government temporarily taking a law off the books rather than leaving it on the books and ignoring it. Since nothing can act unless God keeps it in being and concurs with its action, God can simply refrain from concurring with the fire, and the burning doesn't happen. He isn't forcing fire to act against its nature; he's just not bringing about the burning. In the music picture, it's the musician leaving out some notes.

Why would God work miracles? One reason: to confirm a revelation. If a prophet claimed to bring truths no philosophy or science could reach, how would we know he wasn't making them up? If he worked miracles — which only God can do — that would be God's seal of approval. So miracles are the **bridge from natural theology to revealed religion**. Faith, properly understood, isn't believing without evidence; it's believing something because God has revealed it — and that God has revealed it is something that needs evidence. Natural theology can't prove any particular religion, but it can rule some out: any religion that says there are many ultimate gods, that God is the world, that God is impersonal, material, or limited in power or knowledge is false. And a religion claiming revelation without miracles gives us no reason to take that claim seriously.

### Coda: why "he"?

God is immaterial, so he is not an animal, not a human being, and not literally male or female. He is sexless. Why then call him "he"? First, God has intellect and will, which make him personal, so "it" is wrong. Second, God's relationship to the world is more like a father's biological role than a mother's. The father acts; the mother is acted upon. The father's body doesn't change through conception; the mother's changes radically. The father isn't physically dependent on mother or child; the child is physically connected to the mother, not the father. Likewise God is active in creating, the world passive; God is unchanging, the world constantly changing; the world depends on God, not God on the world; God is utterly distinct from the world. Maternal images, Feser argues, would suggest a God changed by or continuous with the world — pantheism or panentheism.

## 5. The line of reasoning, step by step

Feser doesn't give one numbered proof in this part of the book. Here is a paraphrased reconstruction of his main line of reasoning, with a brief justification for each step.

**Stage 1 — Omnipotence**
1. Everything other than God is composite, part potential, has essence distinct from existence, and is contingent. *(From the five proofs.)*
2. Such things exist at any instant only because the one purely actual, simple, necessary being is causing them. *(The proofs' conclusion.)*
3. So everything depends on God at every instant for existence. *(From 1–2.)*
4. Action follows being; existing is presupposed by acting. *(Defended in Part A.)*
5. So everything depends on God at every instant for its power to act. *(From 3–4.)*
6. So all power derives from God and nothing is beyond his power: he is omnipotent. *(Definition of omnipotence.)*
7. God exists in the fullest way, and power is the ability to actualize potential. *(God is subsistent existence.)*
8. So God can actualize any actualizable potential. *(From 4 and 7; nothing could block him.)*
9. Contradictory "tasks" (round squares, the unliftable stone, sinning while perfectly good) are not powers he lacks. *(They name nothing.)*

**Stage 2 — Intellect and omniscience**
10. Every thing and state of affairs depends on God at every moment. *(From 3 and 5.)*
11. Whatever is in an effect is in its total cause somehow. *(Proportionate causality.)*
12. So every feature of every possible thing is in God somehow. *(From 10–11.)*
13. Not as instances. *(God is immaterial; and instances would themselves need explaining.)*
14. Universals are real and, apart from instances, exist only in an intellect or a Platonic realm; there is no Platonic realm. *(Chapter 3.)*
15. So they exist in God as concepts: God has intellect. *(Elimination.)*
16. States of affairs exist in God as propositions, including thoughts of truth and falsity. *(Propositions are the mind's counterparts of states of affairs.)*
17. Knowledge is true belief from a reliable process, applied analogically. *(Standard account.)*
18. God meets each condition, so he knows. *(He thinks p; p is true because he causes it; making p true is maximally reliable.)*
19. He also knows himself fully, so he knows all truths: omniscience. *(Author's self-knowledge; God is simple.)*
20. He knows the world by knowing himself as its cause, timelessly, not by observation. *(He is eternal, bodiless, unchanging.)*
21. His knowledge is one undivided grasp of pure actuality containing all limited ways of being. *(Prism and dough.)*

**Stage 3 — Goodness**
22. A thing is good insofar as it actualizes what its nature calls for; moral goodness is the case of free agents. *(Triangle, tree.)*
23. So goodness is actuality and badness is privation. *(Wavy side vs. missing fourth side.)*
24. The privation account survives its objections. *(See section 6.)*
25. God is pure actuality. *(Proofs.)*
26. So God is purely good. *(From 23 and 25.)*
27. All good in the world comes from him, so he is all-good. *(Proportionate causality.)*
28. Evil is not in God, and permitting it is compatible with his goodness. *(Evil is absence; courage requires danger.)*

**Stage 4 — Will, freedom, love**
29. Natures incline to their ends: unconsciously, sensorily, or rationally. *(Tree, dog, human.)*
30. God has the limit case of rational appetite and wills some possibles, not others. *(He has intellect and chooses among possibles.)*
31. Nothing external compels him. *(All depends on him.)*
32. Nothing internal compels him. *(No concept necessitates its instances.)*
33. So God's will is free. *(From 31–32.)*
34. Simplicity and goodness don't undermine this freedom. *(Fallacy of accident; no obligation; no best world; the diamond.)*
35. Love is essentially willing another's good. *(Tough love, love of enemies.)*
36. Creating is willing things' existence and good. *(Goodness is actuality.)*
37. So God loves creatures, without change. *(From 30, 35, 36.)*

**Stage 5 — Incomprehensibility**
38. Negative knowledge is real, and analogy is literal. *(Attribution; proportionality.)*
39. So we comprehend God loosely. *(Against fideism.)*
40. Strict comprehension analyzes into parts or genus and difference. *(How understanding works.)*
41. God is simple, so we cannot comprehend him strictly — as the arguments predict. *(Physics parallel.)*

**Stage 6 — Conservation and concurrence**
42. God conserves all things at every instant; no existential inertia. *(From 2–3; composites need sustaining causes.)*
43. Creatures are distinct from God: no pantheism or panentheism. *(Composite vs. simple; God immutable.)*
44. Occasionalism is false. *(What does nothing is nothing; you exist and aren't God; ladder.)*
45. Mere conservationism is false. *(Independent action implies independent existence — deism; ladder.)*
46. Concurrentism is true: real, secondary causes. *(Chalk, moon.)*
47. Humans are among these secondary causes. *(They are creatures.)*
48. Concurrence leaves freedom intact. *(Father guiding the hand.)*

**Stage 7 — Miracles**
49. Laws describe dispositions of natures, which can be blocked. *(Water at 32°F.)*
50. Given the things exist, laws are necessary; violations are impossible. *(Different laws, different natures.)*
51. A miracle elevates beyond any created nature: supernatural. *(Not subnatural or preternatural.)*
52. Only God works miracles, and he doesn't intervene. *(He is not a cause alongside others.)*
53. Miracles may add or subtract; subtraction is suspension via withheld concurrence. *(Fiery furnace.)*
54. So miracles are possible. *(From 52–53.)*
55. Miracles could certify a revelation. *(Only God can work them.)*
56. So miracles bridge natural and revealed theology; faith rests on reason. *(Faith is belief on God's word, which needs evidence.)*

**Stage 8 — Coda**
57. God is sexless but personal. *(Immaterial; intellect and will.)*
58. His relation to the world resembles fatherhood. *(Active, unchanged, independent, distinct.)*
59. So paternal language is least misleading. *(Maternal imagery suggests pantheism.)*

## 6. Every objection and the reply

**The paradox of the stone.** Can God make a stone he can't lift? Either way there's something he can't do. *Reply:* A stone's rising is always possible, so God can always cause it. "A stone God can't lift" is a contradiction, like a round square, and there is no power to make contradictions. (A footnote adds: if God *could* do contradictions, he could make the stone and then lift it anyway.)

**God can't sin, so he isn't omnipotent.** *Reply:* "Perfectly good yet sinning" is a contradiction. Being unable to fail is not a lack of power.

**Timeless knowledge implies a block universe.** If God knows all times at once, all times must exist at once. *Reply:* That follows only if God knows by observing. He knows by causing. Knowing timelessly that he causes a temporal world doesn't make the world timeless, just as a motionless hunter who knows he fired doesn't make the bullet motionless.

**Foreknowledge destroys free will** (Nelson Pike). If God knows I'll go to work tomorrow, I can't not go. *Reply:* Knowing doesn't compel. If I know you're sitting in the next room, you still sat freely. If I knew by a promise or a crystal ball that you'd sit there tomorrow, that wouldn't compel you either. So why would God's knowing?

**But God causes everything, so we aren't free.** *Reply:* In a crime novel, a character plots and commits murder. He's guilty even though the author wrote it — but not if another character hypnotized him. The hypnotist is a character interfering with others; the author isn't in the story. God is the author, not the hypnotist. The objection works only if we speak of God as if he were one more agent inside the world.

**Ideas in God contradict simplicity.** *Reply:* Concepts aren't in God as distinct thoughts. Like white light containing all colors in a unified way, God's single self-knowledge contains every limited way of being. The many-ness is on the creatures' side.

**Goodness is subjective.** *Reply:* The good triangle and the healthy tree show that "good" can be read off a thing's nature, not our tastes.

**The privation theory denies evil is real.** *Reply:* Blindness is an absence of sight and is perfectly real. Explaining what evil *is* doesn't deny that it is.

**Pain is a positive bad.** *Reply:* Pain isn't bad without qualification — it warns us of danger, and the ache after a workout can feel good. What's bad is the damage it signals or the peace it disturbs. People with pain asymbolia feel pain without the unpleasantness.

**Murder involves a positive intention** (Todd Calder). *Reply* (following David Alexander): The privation theory never said bad acts have no positive features, only that their *badness* is a privation. The murderer's desire for money may be fine; what's missing is the intention to pursue money only in ways that respect innocent people.

**Sadism is a positive delight in harm.** *Reply:* Sadistic desire is misdirected desire — aimed against the concern for others that social animals need to flourish. It's a psychological deformity, as blindness is a physical one.

**Proportionate causality puts evil in God.** *Reply:* The principle concerns positive features. Evil is an absence — goods God didn't make. The unfinished triangle's flaw isn't something added.

**Not making all possible goods is a defect.** *Reply:* Leaving a triangle unfinished to teach a lesson is no defect. Some goods require permitting some badness: no courage without danger.

**A simple God can't be free** (Norman Kretzmann, Jay Wesley Richards). Freedom means having potentials and actualizing one. *Reply:* That's a fallacy of accident. Our free choices involve actualizing potentials; freedom as such needn't. What matters is being uncompelled. It's hard to picture, as Brian Davies notes, but mystery is expected here — and our own freedom is mysterious too.

**God must create the best possible world, so he isn't free** (Leibniz, William Rowe). *Reply:* Two false assumptions. First, that one must always do the best: yet no one is wronged if God doesn't create — not God, who lacks nothing, and not nonexistent trees, which can't be wronged. Second, that there *is* a best world: for any world with some number of forests, a world with one more forest would be better. There's no top.

**Then there's no most perfect being** (Rowe). Whatever world God makes, a maker of a better one would be more perfect. *Reply:* God's perfection comes from what he is, not what he makes. A perfect diamond is perfect whether it cuts thin glass, thick glass, or none.

**An unchanging God can't love.** Love means being affected. *Reply:* Fallacy of accident. Love's essence is willing the good of the beloved. Feelings come and go; tough love and love of enemies show the will is what counts. God wills the existence and good of every creature.

**Analogy and mystery are obscurantism.** *Reply:* We have real negative knowledge and literal analogical knowledge. The inability to comprehend God fully is *derived* from his simplicity, and physics, too, uses strange concepts at its frontiers.

**Existential inertia** (John Beaudoin, Mortimer Adler, Bede Rundle). *Reply:* It's never explained, and no composite, contingent thing could have it.

**The world is the necessary being.** *Reply:* Nothing composite or changeable can be necessary, and any derived necessity presupposes God.

**Occasionalism.** *Reply:* What does nothing is nothing; you exist and aren't God; and occasionalism knocks out the ladder of the proofs. The Augustinian proof can't rescue it, since it doesn't reach God as the source of causal power.

**Pantheism by illusion.** Maybe change and multiplicity are illusions, so the world is God. *Reply:* Someone must have the illusions — and whoever has changing, differing, mistaken experiences is changeable, composite, contingent, and not all-knowing. So not God.

**Mere conservationism.** *Reply:* Independent action implies independent existence — that's deism — and nothing composite exists independently. It too kicks away the ladder.

**Concurrence destroys freedom.** *Reply:* The father guiding the child's hand: the child's cooperation is real, but the letter needs the father.

**Miracles are violations and so incoherent** (Hume). *Reply:* Laws are the dispositions of natures; they can be blocked without being violated, and they can't be violated at all given the natures exist. A miracle is an elevation beyond nature or a suspension of an effect.

**Miracles mean God tinkering with a defective world.** *Reply:* God is always causing everything; he is alongside nothing, so he can't "interfere." A miracle is like a jazz improvisation or a director's cut, adding to what was already good.

**The fiery furnace would be a violation.** *Reply:* It's a suspension, not a breach: God withholds his concurrence from the fire, so no burning occurs. Nothing is forced against its nature.

**Faith is belief without evidence.** *Reply:* Faith is believing because God revealed something; that he revealed it requires evidence, which miracles could provide.

**Calling God "he" is sexist.** *Reply:* God is sexless, but personal, and his relation to the world resembles fatherhood's active, independent, distinct role.

## 7. Common misunderstandings

- **"Omnipotence means God can do anything you can say."** No — contradictions describe nothing, so there's no power to lack.
- **"God sees the future."** No — God isn't in time. He knows by causing, like an author, not by looking ahead.
- **"If God causes everything, freedom is fake."** No — the author doesn't compete with his characters; only a character like the hypnotist does.
- **"The privation theory says evil isn't real."** No — evil is a real lack of what should be there, like blindness.
- **"An unchanging God is cold."** No — love is willing someone's good, and God wills every good we have.
- **"Classical theists think they've got God figured out" or "they hide behind mystery."** Neither — we know God truly but not exhaustively, and the limit follows from the arguments.
- **"God made the world long ago and now it runs by itself."** No — creation is happening now; nothing has existential inertia.
- **"Miracles are God breaking the laws of nature."** No — they elevate nature or suspend an effect; God never forces anything against its nature and never "intervenes."
- **"Calling God 'he' means God is male."** No — God is sexless; "he" signals personhood and a fatherlike relation to the world.

## 8. How to explain it to others

**The simple version.** "The proofs point to something that is the source of everything's existence right now. If everything gets its existence from it, everything gets its abilities from it too — so it has all power. It can't give what it doesn't have, so it contains, in a higher way, every pattern in the world — it has a mind. It knows everything, not by watching, but the way an author knows his story. Being good means being all you're meant to be, and it lacks nothing — so it's perfectly good, and evil is just a gap where some good is missing. It chooses freely, and creating us is willing our good, which is what love is. And because it's the author and not a character, it never has to 'break into' the world — it's already doing all of it."

**The best analogy: the author and the story.** Ask your listener to imagine a novelist and a crime novel. Then walk through five points:
1. The author knows everything in the story without watching it, because he's writing it. *(Omniscience.)*
2. A character who freely commits a murder is still responsible, even though the author wrote it; a hypnotized character would not be. *(Freedom.)*
3. Characters really do things in the story, but only because the author is writing them. *(Concurrence.)*
4. When the author adds a new chapter in a second edition, he isn't "interfering" with his story. *(Miracles.)*
5. The author can't be found anywhere on the pages, and you can't understand him just by analyzing the plot. *(Incomprehensibility.)*

Then be honest about the limits: we are real, not fictional, and God isn't in time the way an author is. The analogy shows that the claims don't conflict; it doesn't show *how* God does it.

**A second image: the singer and the song.** A painting can outlast its painter, but a song exists only while it's being sung. The world is more like the song. Every note is real, with its own pitch, yet none sounds without the singer.

## 9. Key takeaways and review questions

**Key takeaways**
- Every attribute here is *derived*, usually by two routes: backward from the world as effect, forward from God as pure actuality.
- Omnipotence: all power is borrowed from the source of existence; contradictions are not tasks.
- Omniscience: by proportionate causality and the reality of universals, God has intellect; he knows by causing, as an author does, in one timeless, undivided act.
- Goodness is actuality in line with a thing's nature; badness is privation; so God is perfectly good and evil is not in him.
- God's will is free (nothing compels it), there is no best possible world, and his perfection doesn't depend on what he creates.
- Love is willing another's good; creating is loving.
- God is knowable truly but not comprehensible fully, because he is simple.
- God conserves all things at every instant; creatures are real but secondary causes (concurrentism), not puppets (occasionalism) or independent agents (mere conservationism).
- Laws of nature express natures and can't be violated; miracles elevate nature or suspend effects, and God never "intervenes," since he is not a cause among causes.
- Miracles are the bridge from natural theology to revealed religion.

**Ten review questions (with brief answers)**

1. *Why does dependence for existence imply dependence for action?* Because action follows being: what cannot exist on its own cannot have an independent power to act.
2. *How does Feser dissolve the paradox of the stone?* A stone's rising is always possible, so "a stone God can't lift" is a contradiction; there is no power to lack.
3. *Why must the universal patterns of things exist in God as ideas?* They must be in him somehow (proportionate causality), not as instances, and there is no Platonic realm, so they must be in an intellect.
4. *How does God satisfy the "reliable process" condition for knowledge?* Nothing is more reliable than being the one who makes a truth true.
5. *What does the author-versus-hypnotist contrast show?* A cause outside the whole order (the author) doesn't compete with a character's freedom, unlike a cause inside it (the hypnotist); God is the author.
6. *What is a privation? Give one example and one non-example.* The lack of something a nature calls for: weak roots in a tree (privation), no wings on a tree (not).
7. *Why doesn't proportionate causality put evil in God?* Evil isn't a positive feature but an absence of goods God didn't make.
8. *What two assumptions of the "best possible world" argument does Feser reject?* That one must always do the best, and that a best world exists.
9. *Why do occasionalism and mere conservationism both fail?* By action follows being, the first implies only God exists and the second implies creatures could exist without God; and both undermine the proofs' starting points.
10. *Why is a miracle not a violation of a law of nature?* Laws express the natures of things and can't be violated; a miracle elevates nature beyond its power, or, as with the fiery furnace, suspends an effect by God withholding his concurrence.
