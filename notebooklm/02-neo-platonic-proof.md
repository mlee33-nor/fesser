# The Neo-Platonic Proof: From Things Made of Parts to the Absolutely Simple One

## A study guide to Chapter 2 of Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (pages 69–86)

---

## 1. Orientation

Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (Ignatius Press, 2017) defends five classic philosophical arguments for God. Each one starts from a different, very general feature of reality and argues, step by step, that this feature can only be explained by a being with the traditional divine attributes. Chapter 1 presents the **Aristotelian proof**, which begins with change (the actualizing of potentials) and concludes to a purely actual Unmoved Mover. Chapter 2, the subject of this guide, presents the **Neo-Platonic proof**. It begins with something just as ordinary: the fact that the things around us are **made of parts**. It concludes that there must exist, right now, a cause that has **no parts at all**. Following the ancient philosopher Plotinus, Feser calls this cause **the One**. He then argues that the One must be unique, changeless, eternal, immaterial, a mind, purely actual, all-powerful, all-knowing, and perfectly good — in other words, God — and that it is the very same being chapter 1 arrived at from a different starting point.

This is the shortest of Feser's five proofs, and in many ways the most intuitive. It also explains more clearly than any other why the ultimate cause of things does not itself need a cause.

---

## 2. Why this chapter matters

There are four reasons this chapter deserves careful study.

**First, it shows that the case for God doesn't rest on one line of thought.** Feser's larger aim is to show that very different starting points lead to the same conclusion. Chapter 2 is where that convergence first becomes explicit: the One turns out to be purely actual, and there can be only one purely actual being, so the One and chapter 1's Unmoved Mover are the same reality. Two roads, one summit.

**Second, it makes divine simplicity central.** Classical theism holds that God is **simple** — without parts of any kind. This chapter shows why simplicity is not an optional extra but the very thing that makes the First Cause first, setting up chapter 6's fuller defense.

**Third, it dissolves the most common objection to arguments for God.** "If everything needs a cause, what caused God?" Once you see that the argument's principle is that *things with parts* need a cause of their composition, the answer is obvious: something with no parts has nothing to be put together, so it not only needs no cause but could not have one.

**Fourth, it teaches tools used throughout the book:** past versus present dependence, hierarchical causal series, "metaphysical parts," Frege's sense/reference distinction, and analogical language about God.

---

## 3. Key concepts and vocabulary

**Composite.** Anything made up of parts of any kind, whose existence depends on those parts existing and being joined in the right way. A chair, a tree, a molecule, and a human being are all composites.

**Part (in the broad sense).** In this argument, "part" does not only mean a piece you could snap off. It means any constituent or factor that has to be combined with others for a thing to exist. That includes physical pieces (legs, screws), underlying constituents (molecules, atoms), sustaining conditions (a suitable temperature), and metaphysical principles (form and matter, essence and existence).

**Absolutely simple (noncomposite).** Having no parts of any kind — no physical parts, and also no metaphysical composition. Something absolutely simple has no distinction between what it is and that it is, between what it actually is and what it could be, or between itself and any features it has. Note that "simple" here means *partless*, not "easy to understand" or "primitive."

**The One.** Plotinus's name for the utterly simple first principle of reality, borrowed by Feser for the partless cause his argument reaches. It is "one" in two senses: it has no parts, and there can be only one of it.

**Plotinus and Neo-Platonism.** Plotinus (about 204/5–270 AD) was a Greek-speaking philosopher, probably from Egypt, who studied in Alexandria and taught in Rome. His student Porphyry collected his writings as the *Enneads* ("nines"). Plotinus saw himself as a follower of Plato; "Neo-Platonism" is a modern label. His reality has three levels: the **One** (also called the Good), then **Intellect** (*Nous*, the realm of the Forms), then **Soul**, with everything flowing from the One like light from the sun. His central intuition is that *to be is to be one*: an army, a choir, or a living body exists only while it has unity, and every "many" presupposes a prior "one." Plotinus denied that the One thinks, since thinking seemed to involve a duality of thinker and thought; later thinkers such as Augustine and Aquinas kept the One's simplicity but placed intellect in it, and Feser follows them. (This background comes from the history of philosophy; Feser's chapter mentions Plotinus only briefly.)

**Atemporal dependence.** Dependence that holds at every moment, regardless of history: what a thing needs *right now* in order to exist *right now*. Contrast this with temporal dependence, such as depending on a builder who put you together at some time in the past.

**Concurrent cause.** A cause that operates at the same time as its effect, sustaining it rather than merely starting it. Friction keeping the screws in a chair is a concurrent cause of the chair's holding together.

**Explanatory vicious circle.** A supposed explanation in which A depends on B and B depends on A, so that neither is actually accounted for. If a chair's existence depends on its parts being united, then the chair cannot be what unites them.

**Hierarchical (per se) causal series.** A series in which each member's causal power is derived from, or borrowed from, a prior member, so that the whole series depends here and now on a first member whose power is not borrowed. Feser's classic example from chapter 1: a hand pushes a stick, which pushes a stone. The stick moves the stone only because the hand moves the stick.

**Linear (per accidens) causal series.** A series in which earlier members don't need to still exist or act for later members to act. The worker who assembled your chair years ago belongs to a linear series; he can die and your chair stays put.

**Form and matter.** In Aristotelian philosophy, **form** makes a thing the kind of thing it is and gives it its powers; **matter** ties that universal form down to a particular individual.

**Essence and existence.** In Thomistic philosophy, a thing's **essence** is *what* it is and its **existence** is *that* it is.

**Pure actuality.** Being entirely actual, with no unrealized potential. This is how chapter 1 describes the Unmoved Mover.

**Principle of proportionate causality.** Whatever is in an effect must be present in some way in its total cause; a cause cannot give what it doesn't in some way have. Chapter 1 uses this to show that a purely actual cause must have intellect, power, and goodness.

**Sense and reference.** A distinction from the logician Gottlob Frege. The **sense** of an expression is the way it presents something; its **reference** is the thing it picks out. "The morning star" and "the evening star" have different senses, but both refer to the planet Venus.

**Univocal, equivocal, and analogical.** A word is used **univocally** when it has the same meaning in two uses ("Rover is a dog," "Fido is a dog"). It is used **equivocally** when the meanings are completely unrelated (a baseball bat versus the bat flying around the attic). It is used **analogically** when the meanings are related but not identical ("this wine is good," "George is a good man"). Analogical use is still *literal*, not metaphorical.

**Brute fact.** A fact with no explanation at all.

**Abstract entity and mental content.** An abstract entity is something like a number, a pattern, or a universal considered in itself — neither physical nor mental, and unable to cause anything. A mental content is something that exists only as the product or object of a mind, such as a thought.

## 4. The argument as a story

### Stage 1: From parts to the partless

**Start with the chair you're sitting on.** It has legs, screws, a frame, a seat, a cushion, and fabric. The book in your hands has a cover, pages, glue, and ink. You yourself have arms and legs, eyes and ears, bones and muscles. Everything we encounter in experience is made of parts.

Feser notes something subtle here. In one way, the whole comes before its parts: we understand what a leg is by what it does for the animal (helps it move around), what an eye is by what it does (lets it see), what a book cover is by what it does (protects the pages and tells you the title and author), what a cushion is by what it does (makes the chair comfortable). But in another way, the parts come before the whole: the whole simply cannot exist unless its parts exist *and* are put together in the right way. Take away the seat and there is no chair. Leave the pieces in a heap on the floor, or bolt them together as a little table, and you still have no chair. Scatter a book's pages across a field and the book is gone. Lump a body's organs into a pile and there is no body. It is this second kind of priority — the dependence of the whole's very existence on its parts and their arrangement — that powers the argument.

**This dependence is about now, not about history.** You might think "depending on your parts" just means "having been assembled at some point." That is true of chairs, which are manufactured piece by piece and then put together. But it isn't true of everything. Your body was never assembled from ready-made arms and eyes; all your organs developed together as cells divided in your mother's womb. More importantly, even a chair that had *always* existed — or one that popped into being all at once — would still depend on its parts. At any moment it exists, it exists only because at that moment its parts exist and are joined in the right way. So the dependence is fundamentally *atemporal*: it holds moment by moment, whether or not there was ever a time of assembly. This is why the argument does not care whether the universe had a beginning.

**Nothing can hold itself together.** So what accounts for the parts being united? Not the composite thing itself. In the temporal sense this is obvious: chairs don't build themselves. But it is equally true in the moment-by-moment sense. The chair's existence right now *depends on* its parts being present and arranged right now. If the chair were also what *made* its parts present and arranged, we would have a vicious circle — the chair's existence depending on the arrangement, and the arrangement depending on the chair. Feser's vivid image: the chair would be hauling itself into being by its "metaphysical bootstraps."

In reality, of course, the chair's parts are held together by many things outside the chair. Screws fasten the legs to the frame. Friction keeps the screws from backing out. The legs and screws exist because the molecules that make them up exist and are bonded in particular ways, and those molecules exist because their atoms are bonded in particular ways. Background conditions matter too: the room has to stay within a certain temperature range, because if it got hot enough, the metal screws would melt and the wood would burst into flames. All of these factors, and others, have to be combined correctly *at a given moment* for the chair to exist at that moment. What is true of the chair is true of every composite thing: it has causes not just of its coming into being, but of its continuing to be — causes operating right now.

**The series is hierarchical.** Borrowing chapter 1's vocabulary, Feser points out that the factory worker who once assembled the chair belongs to a *linear* causal series; he could die and the chair would remain. But the factors keeping the chair in existence now form a *hierarchical* series. These factors happen to operate at the same time, but Feser insists that simultaneity isn't what makes a series hierarchical. What makes it hierarchical is that each member's causal contribution is *derived* from something further — as the stick moves the stone only because the hand moves the stick. The chair holds together only because its parts hold together; the parts hold together only because deeper factors hold together; and so on down. If those deeper factors stopped "holding together," the chair would instantly stop holding together too.

**Parts can be stranger than chair legs.** Notice how the discussion has drifted. We started with everyday parts like chair legs and eyeballs, but we are now talking about atoms (which are not everyday objects) and temperature (which is not an object at all). Philosophers identify even more unusual parts.

For **Aristotelian** philosophers, every physical thing is a combination of **form** and **matter**. Copper can be hammered into shape and conducts electricity because of its form; a tree draws water up through its roots, and an animal senses its surroundings, because of their different forms. Each form is universal — the same form of copper is in every piece of copper, which is why they are all copper rather than lead or gold — and matter ties that form down to *this* particular piece, here and now. Feser says it doesn't matter, for this argument, whether you accept this analysis. The point is that *if* it's true, form and matter are parts that need a cause of their combination. Form without matter is only an abstraction; matter without form is only a potential to be something. Each depends on the other, so unless something outside them accounts for their union, we have another vicious circle.

**Thomist** philosophers add another distinction: between a thing's **essence** (what it is) and its **existence** (the fact that it is). Every triangle shares the essence of a triangle — being a closed plane figure with three straight sides — but the existence of this particular triangle is different from the existence of that one. There's no such thing as the existence of a triangle apart from its being a triangle, because whatever exists exists as some kind of thing. But the essence of a triangle all by itself, belonging to no actual triangle, is just an abstraction. So in any real triangle, essence and existence depend on each other, and something outside the triangle has to account for their being joined. Feser notes that this distinction will return later in the book (it becomes the basis of chapter 4's Thomistic proof).

The lesson is that the principle **whatever is composite has a cause** is completely general. It applies whatever the parts turn out to be — physical pieces, chemical constituents, or metaphysical principles.

**The regress must end in something absolutely simple.** Suppose one composite thing is held in existence by another composite, and that one by another, in a hierarchical series. For the reasons given in chapter 1, that series cannot go on forever; it must have a first member — one whose power to hold things in being is not borrowed from anything else. But the first member cannot itself be composite, because anything composite would need a cause of its own composition, and so it wouldn't be first. So the first member must be **absolutely simple**: without material parts, and without metaphysical parts such as form and matter or essence and existence. For any composite thing to exist here and now, there must exist here and now an utterly simple ultimate cause of its existence. Following Plotinus, we can call it **the One**.

### Stage 2: What the One must be like

**There can be only one.** Could there be two or more simple causes? Suppose there were two, A and B. For them to be two, they would have to differ in some way: A would have some feature F that B lacks, and B would have some feature G that A lacks. But then neither would really be simple. A would be "a simple cause plus F" and B would be "a simple cause plus G." F and G would be parts. A truly simple cause has no parts, so there is nothing that could make one simple cause different from another. Therefore there can only be one. The One is "one," then, in two senses: it is **simple** (without parts), and it is **unique**. All the composite things of our experience ultimately trace back to the very same simple cause.

**It cannot change.** To change is to gain or lose some feature. If the One could gain or lose a feature, then it would be the One *plus* that feature — composite again. So the One is **immutable**.

**It is eternal.** Since being in time involves undergoing change, something immutable is **eternal** in the strict sense of being outside time altogether. It is also eternal in the sense of having no beginning and no end. If it came into being, it would have been caused, which means it would have parts that were put together when it was caused. If it could pass away, it would have parts it could be broken down into. It has no parts, so it can neither begin nor end.

**It is a mind, or more like a mind than anything else.** Here Feser draws on the contemporary philosopher William Vallicella, who defends an argument similar to this one. Vallicella proposes that everything is one of four kinds of thing: a **mind**, a **content in a mind** (such as a thought), a **physical entity**, or an **abstract entity**. The One can't be an abstract entity, because abstract things can't cause anything. A stone can break a window, but the abstract pattern of "being a stone" can't break a window or do anything else, while the One is the cause of the existence of composite things. The One can't be a physical thing, because physical things have parts that need to be combined, which is why they can come into existence and pass away — and the One can do neither. And the One can't be a mental content, because a thought depends on the mind whose thought it is, so it cannot be the *ultimate* cause of anything. By elimination, the One is a mind — or, as Vallicella cautiously puts it, it is "more like a mind than anything else."

Feser adds an honest footnote. Plotinus himself put intellect in a second divine reality below the One. But you don't have to accept every detail of the Neo-Platonic system to use its general strategy of reasoning from composition to a simple source.

**It is the cause of everything else.** Since the One is unique, everything other than it is composite. And every composite ultimately depends on the One. So the One is the ultimate cause of everything besides itself. At this point, Feser says, we have a cause that is uncaused, simple, unique, immutable, eternal, immaterial, and intelligent — already enough to call it God.

**It is purely actual — and so it is the Unmoved Mover.** But more can be said. The One must be at least partly actual, because (as chapter 1 showed) what is merely potential can't do anything, and the One is doing something: causing everything else. If the One were only partly actual, it would be partly potential too, and then it would have parts — an actual part and a potential part. Since it has no parts, it must be **purely actual**. And once we know it is purely actual, everything chapter 1 said about the purely actual Unmoved Mover applies to it, using the principle of proportionate causality: the One is **perfect, omnipotent, fully good, and omniscient**. In fact, chapter 1 showed that there cannot be more than one purely actual being. So the One and the Unmoved Mover are the very same thing. Chapter 1 started from change — potentials being actualized — and concluded that there must be something purely actual. Chapter 2 started from composition and concluded that there must be something absolutely simple. These turn out to be two ways of thinking about one reality.

**How can one simple thing have so many attributes?** A thoughtful reader will object: if the One is omnipotent *and* intelligent *and* eternal, doesn't that mean it has several attributes, and therefore several parts? Feser's first answer draws on Frege. The phrases "the morning star" and "the evening star" don't mean the same thing — one presents a light in the dawn sky, the other a light in the evening sky — yet they refer to one and the same object, Venus. In the same way, "the One is omnipotent" doesn't mean the same as "the One is an intellect," but both can be true descriptions of one and the same simple reality. The One's power, intellect, eternity, and immateriality are one thing, conceived and described in different ways.

**Analogical language.** The objector can push harder: in a human being, intelligence and power are genuinely different features — a brilliant person can be physically weak. So why not in the One? Feser answers that they *would* be different if we used "intellect" and "power" in exactly the same sense for the One as for us. But because the One has no parts, we shouldn't. Following Thomas Aquinas, we should use them **analogically**. "Dog" is univocal when said of Rover and Fido; "bat" is equivocal when said of a baseball bat and a flying mammal; but "good" in "the wine is good" and "George is a good man" is analogical — different but related meanings, and both literal, not metaphorical. So to call God powerful or intelligent is to say there is something in God analogous to our power and intellect. And one key difference is that in God these are one reality seen from different angles, while in us they are distinct.

**Why the strangeness is expected.** Feser admits this is odd, but argues it is exactly what we should expect. Physics tells us elementary particles behave partly like particles and partly like waves. That is hard to understand, yet the evidence compels it, and the difficulty is predictable because we are stretching concepts built for ordinary objects to phenomena remote from everyday life. Reasoning to the absolutely simple cause of everything takes us farther still — to the most fundamental level of reality — so our concepts should strain to the limit. Reason itself tells us there is a level of reality that reason can only partly comprehend.

## 5. The formal argument, step by step

Feser summarizes the proof in 38 numbered steps. Here they are, restated in plain language, with a brief justification for each.

**Stage 1: There is an absolutely simple cause.**

1. **Everything we experience is made of parts.** — Chairs, books, bodies, molecules; and if the metaphysicians are right, form/matter and essence/existence too.
2. **At any moment a composite exists, it exists only because its parts are joined at that moment.** — A heap of chair parts is no chair; this holds even for something that always existed.
3. **The joining of the parts needs a cause operating at the same time.** — The composite can't do it itself without a vicious circle; in fact screws, friction, bonds, and temperature do it.
4. **So every composite has a cause of its existence at every moment it exists.** — Follows from steps 2 and 3.
5. **So each thing we experience has a cause at every moment it exists.** — Apply step 4 to the things in step 1.
6. **If that cause is itself composite, it too needs a cause at that moment.** — Step 4 applies to any composite cause.
7. **This regress is hierarchical, and a hierarchical regress must have a first member.** — Each member's power is borrowed, like the stick's; without an unborrowed source, nothing would be held in being (chapter 1).
8. **Only something absolutely simple could be the first member.** — Any composite member would need a cause of its own and so would not be first.
9. **So the existence of everything we experience presupposes an absolutely simple cause.** — Follows from steps 5 through 8. This is the One.

**Stage 2: What the simple cause is like.**

10. **For there to be more than one simple cause, each would need a feature distinguishing it from the others.** — Two things are two only if something is true of one and not the other.
11. **But having such a feature would mean having parts, so it wouldn't really be simple.** — It would be "simple cause plus feature."
12. **So no simple cause can have a distinguishing feature.** — Follows from step 11.
13. **So there cannot be more than one simple cause.** — Follows from steps 10 and 12.
14. **If the simple cause could change, it would have parts to gain or lose — but it has none.** — Change is gaining or losing a feature.
15. **So it is changeless.** — Follows from step 14.
16. **If it had a beginning or an end, it would have parts that could be put together or come apart.** — Beginning requires being caused (parts combined); ending requires dissolution into parts.
17. **So it has neither beginning nor end.** — Follows from step 16.
18. **Whatever is changeless and without beginning or end is eternal.** — Being in time involves change.
19. **So it is eternal.** — Follows from steps 15, 17, and 18.
20. **Whatever is caused has parts that need to be combined.** — To cause a thing's existence is to account for the union of its parts.
21. **So the simple cause, having no parts, is uncaused.** — Follows from step 20; not an arbitrary exception.
22. **Everything is either a mind, a mental content, a material thing, or an abstract thing.** — Vallicella's classification.
23. **Abstract things can't cause anything.** — The pattern of a stone can't break a window.
24. **So the simple cause, which does cause things, is not abstract.** — Follows from step 23.
25. **Material things have parts and can change.** — They are composed and so can come to be and pass away.
26. **So the simple cause, being partless and changeless, is not material.** — Follows from step 25.
27. **A mental content depends on a mind, so it can't be the ultimate cause of anything.** — A thought exists only as someone's thought.
28. **So the simple cause, being ultimate, is not a mental content.** — Follows from step 27.
29. **So the simple cause is a mind.** — By elimination from steps 22, 24, 26, and 28.
30. **Since the simple cause is unique, everything other than it is composite.** — Follows from step 13.
31. **Every composite has the simple cause as its ultimate cause.** — Follows from Stage 1 plus uniqueness.
32. **So the simple cause is the ultimate cause of everything other than itself.** — Follows from steps 30 and 31.
33. **If the simple cause had potentials as well as actualities, it would have parts.** — A mix of act and potency is a kind of composition.
34. **So it has no potentials; it is purely actual.** — Follows from step 33.
35. **A purely actual cause must be perfect, all-powerful, fully good, and all-knowing.** — Carried over from chapter 1 using proportionate causality.
36. **So there is a cause that is simple, unique, changeless, eternal, immaterial, a mind, the uncaused ultimate cause of everything else, purely actual, perfect, all-powerful, fully good, and all-knowing.** — Gathers the earlier results.
37. **For such a cause to exist just is for God to exist.** — This is what classical theism means by "God."
38. **So God exists.** — Follows from steps 36 and 37.

Notice the pattern that drives most of Stage 2: *if the One had X, it would have parts; it has no parts; so it doesn't have X.* Every distinction within a thing — between bearer and feature, actual and potential, before and after — is a kind of composition. Remove all composition, and you remove every limitation.

## 6. The objections and the replies

Feser discusses two groups of objections: those this proof shares with the Aristotelian proof, and those aimed at what is distinctive about it.

### Objections shared with the Aristotelian proof

**"Even if there's a first cause, it needn't be God."** Feser postpones detailed criticisms of the attribute arguments to his chapter on the divine attributes. But he says it is lazy simply to assert that a first cause might not be God, because the chapter has just given specific reasons why it must be: uniqueness, changelessness, eternity, immateriality, intellect, and pure actuality all follow from simplicity. A critic must engage those reasons, not just wave them away.

**"The argument assumes the universe had a beginning."** It doesn't. The argument is not concerned at all with whether the universe began in time. It doesn't trace causes back into the past until it reaches a first moment; it traces them *down*, here and now, to a simple cause that is sustaining things at this very moment. A chair that always existed would still need its parts united right now.

**"If everything has a cause, what caused God?"** The argument doesn't say everything has a cause. It says whatever is *composite* has a cause. And God doesn't lack a cause because he is an arbitrary exception to a general rule. He lacks a cause because only what is in some way composite needs a cause — or even could have one. Something absolutely simple not only needs no cause but could not have had one, because there is nothing in it to put together. This isn't a trick invented to dodge the objection; it was what the Neo-Platonic tradition meant all along. Far from being devastating, Feser says, the objection completely misses the point.

**Hume and quantum mechanics.** Critics may appeal to David Hume or to quantum mechanics to doubt that composites need causes. Feser refers back to chapter 1: imagining something appearing without imagining its cause is not imagining it with no cause, and quantum indeterminism at most shows that some events lack *deterministic* causes, not causes altogether.

### Objections aimed at what is distinctive about this proof

The distinctive objections challenge the assumption that a composite's parts must be unified by something *outside* the composite. Why not say that some part of the thing unifies it, or that its unity is just an irreducible fact?

**"Some further part of the thing does the unifying."** Following Vallicella, Feser argues that this merely relocates the problem. Take a thing with two parts, A and B. Suppose we say a further part, C, unites them. Now we need to know what unites C with A and B. Suppose we posit another part, D. Then we need to know what unites D with A, B, and C. The question comes back for every new part we add. We haven't explained the unity of A and B at all; we've just multiplied the things whose unity needs explaining. Picture two boards glued together: what holds the glue to the boards, and what holds the glue itself together? Adding more glue never answers the question.

**"The unity is just an irreducible fact about the thing."** What would that mean? There are two options. First, it might mean the composite causes its own parts to be united. But then the composite would be both the cause and the effect of that very union — which is incoherent. Nothing can lift itself up by its own bootstraps. Second, it might mean the union has no cause at all — it is a brute fact. But then the critic hasn't offered an alternative explanation, which is what he claimed to be doing. He has offered no explanation.

**"All right, maybe there's simply no explanation."** Suppose the critic admits he has no alternative and just says: maybe there's no explanation for why composite things exist. As the Plotinus scholar Lloyd Gerson has pointed out, this is not a serious response to a Neo-Platonic argument. The defender of the argument can reply: "But I have given you an explanation — the parts are united by an absolutely simple cause — and you haven't given any reason to reject it that doesn't simply assume your conclusion. So it makes no sense to say 'maybe there's no explanation.'" The mere logical possibility of refusing to explain is not an argument against an explanation that's already on the table.

**"Science explains why parts hold together — the laws of nature do it."** A final critic might accept that there is an external cause, but insist it is scientific rather than divine: laws of atomic structure or molecular cohesion explain why A and B are joined. Feser replies that this isn't a genuine alternative. However we understand laws of nature (a topic he returns to later in the book), an explanation in terms of laws leaves us with something else made of parts whose composition needs explaining. If it's a law that things of type A and type B combine under certain conditions, we must ask why A and B are governed by *that* law rather than some other. A, B, and the law together form a new kind of composite — just another instance of exactly what we were trying to explain. The regress continues instead of ending.

Feser adds a subtle point in a footnote. Aristotelians don't think of laws of nature as rulers standing outside things; for them, a law is shorthand for how a thing tends to behave given its nature, or substantial form. But this is no help to the critic, because a thing's substantial form is, together with its matter, one of the two basic principles it is made of. So the operation of a law *presupposes* the combination of form and matter — it can't be what explains it. The only way to end the regress, Feser concludes, is with something absolutely simple, and for the reasons given, that must be God.

### A modern objection the chapter equips you to answer

**"Why not stop at fundamental particles, or the universe itself?"** An electron has no smaller pieces, but it has a definite nature (mass, charge, spin) distinct from the fact that it exists, and it could have failed to exist — so it is metaphysically composite. The universe is plainly composite. In chapter 7 Feser applies exactly this reasoning to the universe and the laws of physics.

## 7. Common misunderstandings

- **"This argument is about the Big Bang."** No. It concerns what holds things in existence right now. It works whether or not the universe had a beginning.
- **"The argument says everything has a cause."** No. It says everything *composite* has a cause of its composition. That is exactly why the simple cause needs none.
- **"'Simple' means primitive or easy to understand."** It means *without parts*. Feser stresses that the One is very hard to understand — as we should expect.
- **"Being immaterial is the same as being simple, so an angel or a soul could be the One."** No. An immaterial creature still has an essence distinct from its existence and so is metaphysically composite. Absolute simplicity rules out every kind of composition.
- **"You have to accept Aristotle's form and matter or Aquinas's essence and existence for the argument to work."** Feser says explicitly that nothing rides on those theories. The principle works with ordinary physical parts; the metaphysical parts just show how far it reaches.
- **"If God's attributes are spoken of analogically, talk about God is only metaphorical."** Analogical language is literal. Wine and George are both literally good, in related senses.
- **"The regress must end simply because infinite chains are impossible."** The point is that in a hierarchical series every member's power is borrowed; without an unborrowed source there would be no power at all, however long the chain.

## 8. How to explain it to others

### The simple version

Pick up anything — a phone, a pen, a cup. It exists only while its parts are held together; take it apart and it's gone. It can't hold itself together, because it only exists once the parts are already together. So something else is holding it together *right now* — molecular bonds, forces, and so on. But those things have parts too, so something must be holding *them* together, right now. Each level only has holding-power it gets from the level below. So the chain has to end in something that doesn't need holding together because it has no parts at all. And something with no parts has to be one of a kind (two would need some difference, and a difference is a part), changeless (change means gaining or losing something), eternal, immaterial, and — since it's not an abstraction or a mere thought — a mind. That is what people have always meant by God.

### The best analogy: the magnetic sculpture

Imagine a sculpture made of loose steel pieces that stays standing only because an electromagnet in its base is switched on. Nothing in the sculpture itself holds the sculpture together. Cut the power and it collapses instantly — even if it had been standing there forever. The magnet in turn works only because current flows from a generator, which runs only because something else drives it. Each level holds only because something deeper holds it.

This picture shows three things at once. First, the question is not who *built* the sculpture, but what is holding it up *right now*. Second, if someone says "that steel piece in the middle holds all the others," they have just added another piece that needs holding. Third, every level has only borrowed holding-power, so without an unborrowed source, nothing would stand. Where the analogy breaks down: the One isn't just one more power station at the bottom of a physical stack. It is the partless reality on which every level — including the laws of electromagnetism and the very existence of the steel — depends.

When presenting it, say "right now" often, state the principle exactly (*whatever has parts needs a cause of their union*, never "everything has a cause"), and start with ordinary objects before introducing metaphysical parts.

## 9. Key takeaways and review questions

### Key takeaways

- A composite exists only while its parts are united — moment to moment, not merely historically.
- No composite can unite its own parts; the causes that do so form a hierarchical series that must end in a first member.
- That first member must be absolutely simple, without physical or metaphysical parts: the One.
- Simplicity entails uniqueness, changelessness, eternity, uncausedness, and pure actuality; elimination shows the One is a mind.
- Being purely actual, the One is identical with chapter 1's Unmoved Mover.
- Many attributes don't mean many parts: sense differs from reference, and divine attributes are predicated analogically.
- A further part, a brute fact, or the laws of nature cannot explain composition.
- "What caused God?" misfires: only what has parts can have a cause.

### Ten review questions with brief answers

1. **In what two senses can a whole and its parts each be "more fundamental"?**
   The whole is prior for understanding (a part is defined by its role in the whole); the parts are prior for existence (the whole needs its parts to exist and be properly arranged). The argument uses the second sense.

2. **Why does Feser say the dependence of a composite on its parts is atemporal?**
   Because at every moment a composite exists, it needs its parts united at that moment — even if it was never assembled (like a body) or always existed.

3. **Why can't a chair be the cause of its own parts being united?**
   Its existence depends on that union, so if it produced the union, we'd have a vicious circle — the chair lifting itself by its bootstraps.

4. **What makes a causal series hierarchical?**
   Each member's causal power is derived from a prior member, as the stick moves the stone only because the hand moves the stick. Simultaneity is typical but not essential.

5. **How do form and matter count as parts needing a cause?**
   Form alone is an abstraction; matter alone is mere potential. Each depends on the other, so their union needs an outside cause.

6. **Why can there be only one absolutely simple cause?**
   Two would need a feature distinguishing them, but having such a feature would make each composite.

7. **How does Feser show that the One is a mind?**
   Everything is a mind, a mental content, a physical thing, or an abstract thing. The One isn't abstract (abstractions cause nothing), physical (physical things have parts), or a mental content (thoughts depend on minds). So it is a mind, or more like one than anything else.

8. **Why are the One and the Unmoved Mover identical?**
   The One is purely actual (a mix of act and potency would be a composition), and there can be only one purely actual being.

9. **How does Feser answer the objection that many divine attributes mean many parts?**
   With Frege's sense/reference distinction (like the morning star and evening star both being Venus) and Aquinas's analogical predication: the words apply to God in related but not identical senses, and in God they name one reality.

10. **Why doesn't appealing to the laws of nature explain composition?**
    Because the parts plus the law form a further composite (why this law rather than another?), and on the Aristotelian view laws presuppose the form–matter composition they would be invoked to explain.
