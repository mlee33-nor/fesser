# Introduction to *Five Proofs of the Existence of God*: What These Proofs Are Trying to Do

## 1. Orientation

This is the study guide for the Introduction (pages 9–16) of Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (Ignatius Press, 2017), and the first module of a course on the whole book. The introduction is short but does three jobs. It says what kind of book this is: not a commentary on Aquinas' Five Ways, but a direct defense of five arguments inspired by Aristotle, Plotinus, Augustine, Aquinas, and Leibniz. It explains how every proof chapter is built. And it gives a roadmap, ending with a bold claim: God's existence can be rationally demonstrated, so the serious debate is among theists, not between theists and atheists.

The introduction takes several ideas for granted that Feser spells out later: what a "demonstration" is, why the proofs are not design arguments, why they concern a cause operating now rather than a first event, and which conception of God they target. This guide draws on those later passages and says so where it does.

## 2. Why this introduction matters

Most people know two kinds of argument for God. The design argument says the eye or the fine-tuned universe looks designed, so there is probably a designer. The popular "first cause" argument says everything has a cause and the universe began with the Big Bang, so something caused that beginning. Critics have familiar replies: evolution or a multiverse might explain the appearance of design, the universe might have had no beginning, and if everything needs a cause, what caused God?

The introduction signals that Feser defends neither of these arguments. His proofs don't rely on biology or cosmology, don't claim that God is merely the most probable explanation, and don't depend on the universe having a beginning. The God they conclude to is not the most powerful being in reality but the God of "classical theism," who is not one being among others at all. Readers who miss this will misread nearly every later step, and many of the objections answered in chapter 7 turn out to target arguments Feser never makes.

## 3. Key concepts and vocabulary

**Natural theology.** Feser's definition: the study of what unaided human reason, apart from divine revelation, can know about God's existence and nature and about God's relationship to the world. The key word is *unaided*. Premises must be available to any rational inquirer: no Bible verses, no church authority, no private religious experience.

**Divine revelation.** Knowledge of God that depends on God's having specially disclosed it, through prophets or scripture. Whether any revelation has actually happened is, Feser says, beyond the book's scope.

**Demonstration.** Explained in the last section of chapter 7. To demonstrate that God exists is to show that the conclusion follows with deductive necessity from certain premises, whose certainty can be shown by metaphysical analysis. It is not a probability estimate or an "argument to the best explanation" where a rival explanation could in principle be correct.

**Probabilistic or abductive argument.** An argument that a hypothesis is the likeliest or simplest explanation of the evidence, judged by parsimony and fit with established theory. Paley's watchmaker argument and Intelligent Design are examples. None of the five proofs is of this type.

**Classical theism.** The mainstream Western conception of God, found in Aristotle, Plotinus, Augustine, Maimonides, Avicenna, Aquinas, and Leibniz. God is pure actuality, absolutely simple, subsistent existence itself, immutable, and eternal. He belongs to no genus, not even the kind "god," and is not "a being" alongside others but the source of whatever being anything else has.

**Divine simplicity.** The doctrine that God is in no way composite. The introduction calls it absolutely central to classical theism yet neglected by contemporary theists and atheists alike.

**Theistic personalism.** A modern view discussed in chapter 6 (the label is Brian Davies', who counts Plantinga, Swinburne, and Hartshorne as examples). It treats God as the unique member of the genus "person," like us but without our limits. The contrast with classical theism is *not* about whether God is personal, since most classical theists attribute intellect and will to God. It is that personalists place God within a genus and so treat him as composite.

**Two-stage structure and divine attributes.** Stage 1 of each proof shows that something fitting a key description exists; Stage 2 shows it must have the divine attributes: unity, simplicity, immutability, immateriality, eternity, necessity, omnipotence, omniscience, perfect goodness, will, love, and incomprehensibility.

**Actuality and potentiality.** Change is the actualization of a potential, and only something already actual can actualize a potential. Hot coffee is potentially cold; the cooler air actualizes that potential.

**Hierarchical versus linear causal series.** Introduced in chapter 1. In a *linear* series, causes follow one another in time, and later ones can act after earlier ones are gone: the air conditioner keeps cooling the room after you leave. In a *hierarchical* series, each member's causal power is on loan, here and now, from the one before it: the cup is held up by the desk, the desk by the floor, the floor by the foundation, all at this moment. Such a series must have a "first" member, first in the order of dependence rather than in time, with underived power.

**Divine conservation and concurrence.** Previewed in the introduction and developed in chapter 6. Conservation: nothing could continue to exist for even an instant unless God were sustaining it. Concurrence: created things have real causal power but cannot exercise it unless God imparts that power at each moment they act.

## 4. The introduction told as a story

### "This is not a book about the Five Ways"

Feser starts by correcting an expectation. He has defended Aquinas at length before, and the title invites comparison with Aquinas' Five Ways, the arguments from motion, efficient causality, contingency, degrees of perfection, and purpose in nature. But this is not another commentary on Aquinas.

None of the proofs is Feser's invention; all are old. What's new is that several are arguments he hasn't defended at length before, and that most have been largely ignored by contemporary philosophy. He finds this remarkable, since they were historically prominent and are, in his view, the most powerful arguments for God there are. His diagnosis is blunt: the neglect reveals nothing about the arguments and a great deal about the state of academic philosophy, including philosophy of religion.

### Why a new approach was needed

In *The Last Superstition* (2008) and *Aquinas* (2009), Feser approached natural theology by explaining and defending Aquinas, whom he considers the greatest natural theologian. That approach had two drawbacks. First, it was exegetical: it had to explain what Aquinas meant, lay out his background principles about change, causality, and contingency, and separate his philosophical insights from the outdated science he sometimes used to express them. In both books the reader faced about seventy pages of dense metaphysics before reaching God. Second, it limited the discussion to the arguments Aquinas himself thought most important.

So Feser saw a need for a defense of important arguments Aquinas doesn't discuss, and for a presentation of all the most important arguments that skips exegesis and long preambles, gets straight to the heart of each argument, and introduces background principles only as needed.

### What this book does

Two of the five proofs can be found in Aquinas: the Aristotelian proof corresponds to his First Way, and the Thomistic proof to the argument of *On Being and Essence*. The other three are not arguments he discusses, at least not at length or in this form. There is no interpretation of historical texts. The arguments are inspired by great thinkers and, Feser believes, capture what is essential in their reasoning, but he doesn't claim those thinkers said or would endorse everything in the book. It is an *Aristotelian* proof, not exactly Aristotle's own, and an *Augustinian* proof, not a reading of Augustine's writings.

### How each chapter is built

Each of the first five chapters defends one proof using the same plan. It begins with an **informal statement** in two stages. In Stage 1, Feser argues that something exists fitting a key description, for example "an uncaused cause of the existence of things." In Stage 2, he argues that whatever fits it must have the divine attributes: unity, eternity, immateriality, omnipotence, omniscience, and perfect goodness.

The informal statement is discursive rather than a list of numbered premises, for two reasons: Feser wants newcomers to philosophy to be able to follow, and he needs room to digress into broader metaphysics so that it is clear what the proofs claim and misunderstandings are headed off early. It gets technical at times, and he offers a memorable image: every chapter ends in the deepest part of the pool, but each begins in the shallowest part he can find. That is easier for some proofs than others.

Next comes a **more formal statement**, setting out the steps so the logical structure is plain. Feser warns that these are not meant to stand alone, since their key concepts are explained only in the informal sections. Finally, each chapter ends with a long section on **objections**, which sometimes contains the most technical material in the book.

### The roadmap

**Chapter 1: the Aristotelian proof.** It begins with the fact that real change occurs. It analyzes change as the actualization of potential and argues that no potential could be actualized at all unless something exists that actualizes without itself being actualized: a "purely actual actualizer," or what Aristotle called the Unmoved Mover. Aristotle, Maimonides, and Aquinas (in his First Way) all gave versions, expressed in old science such as the motion of heavenly spheres. But modern Aristotelians have shown that the essential kernel does not depend on that outdated husk, and chapter 1 states the argument as those thinkers might today.

**Chapter 2: the Neo-Platonic proof.** It begins with the fact that the things we experience are composite, made up of parts in various ways, and argues that their ultimate cause can only be something absolutely simple: what Plotinus called "the One." The idea is in Plotinus' *Enneads*, and Aquinas also expressed it. This is where divine simplicity, the heart of classical theism, comes to the fore.

**Chapter 3: the Augustinian proof.** It argues that universals (redness, humanness, triangularity), propositions, possibilities, and other abstract objects are real in some sense, but rejects Plato's view that they exist in a "third realm" apart from any mind and from particular things. Their only possible ultimate ground is a divine intellect. The idea has Neo-Platonic roots, was central to Augustine, and was defended by Leibniz; Feser's version is, he believes, the most detailed and systematic yet.

**Chapter 4: the Thomistic proof.** It argues that in every contingent thing there is a real distinction between its essence (what it is) and its existence (that it is). Nothing with such a distinction could exist even for an instant unless caused to exist by something whose very essence is existence, which can give existence without receiving it. Aquinas gave the argument in *On Being and Essence*, and many Thomists consider it the paradigmatically Thomistic argument.

**Chapter 5: the rationalist proof.** It defends the principle of sufficient reason and then argues that the existence of contingent things cannot be explained unless there is a necessary being whose existence is explained by its own nature. It is associated with Leibniz, but Feser's version departs from him and reads the key ideas in an Aristotelian-Thomistic way. It is "rationalist" in holding that everything is intelligible, not in accepting innate ideas or the epistemology of Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz.

**Chapter 6: the nature of God and his relationship to the world.** It opens with three background principles: proportionate causality (whatever is in an effect must pre-exist in some way in its total cause), *agere sequitur esse* (how a thing acts follows from what it is), and the Thomist account of analogical language (words like "wise" apply to God and creatures in related but not identical senses, which are still literal). With these it shows that all five proofs reach one and the same God, that there can be only one God, and that God is simple, immutable, immaterial, eternal, necessary, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, willing, loving, and incomprehensible. It then defends divine conservation and concurrence, ruling out pantheism, panentheism, occasionalism (only God really causes anything), and deism (God made the world and left it to run). It ends with what a miracle is, which bears on whether revelation is possible, though whether any has occurred lies beyond the book.

**Chapter 7: objections to natural theology.** It takes up objections not covered earlier and revisits others in more depth. Feser predicts that by the end it will be clear none of them succeeds, and that the most common ones are, in his words, "staggeringly feeble and overrated."

### A confident discipline

Feser admits the claim is confident, but natural theology was historically a confident discipline. Aristotelians, Neo-Platonists, Scholastics, early modern rationalists, and others, whether pagan, Jewish, Christian, Muslim, or purely philosophical theists, held that God's existence can be demonstrated by philosophy alone. The book aims to show they were right and that this long-mainstream view should be mainstream again.

On this view the real debate is among theists of different kinds (Jews, Christians, Muslims, Hindus, philosophical theists), and it begins where natural theology stops. The book doesn't enter that debate. Feser will be satisfied if it returns us to the point from which the deepest questions can be asked.

### Four clarifications from later in the book

**Demonstration, not probability.** The proofs must be judged as demonstrations. You refute one by showing a premise false, the reasoning invalid, or the question begged, not by suggesting some other explanation might be simpler.

**Not design arguments.** The proofs don't appeal to complexity or probability. For the Neo-Platonic proof, a pebble or a drop of water is as good a starting point as the human eye.

**Here and now, not back then.** Feser grants, for the sake of argument, that the universe or a multiverse might have existed forever. Even then it would need a cause keeping it in existence at every moment. As chapter 6 puts it, creation is not a one-time past event but happens at every moment.

**Philosophical, not scientific.** The starting points are facts any scientific investigation takes for granted. Every future observation will itself involve change, so no observation could show change unreal. That is why the proofs aren't "god of the gaps" arguments, and why the kernel of Aristotle's argument survived the collapse of his astronomy.

## 5. The introduction's line of reasoning, step by step

The introduction contains no formal proof, but its reasoning, filled out with the later clarifications, can be set out in nineteen steps.

1. Natural theology asks what reason alone, without revelation, can know about God. *(Feser's definition.)*
2. A long, diverse tradition held that God's existence can be demonstrated by philosophy alone. *(The introduction's appeal to history.)*
3. A demonstration is a deduction from certain premises whose certainty can be shown by metaphysical analysis. *(End of chapter 7.)*
4. Demonstrations differ in kind from probabilistic or design arguments, which leave rivals possible in principle. *(Chapter 7 on Paley and ID.)*
5. So the proofs must be judged by the truth of their premises and the validity of their logic, not by whether a simpler rival hypothesis can be imagined. *(Follows from 3 and 4.)*
6. A demonstration doesn't fail just because many people are unpersuaded; it succeeds if it can be defended against every objection. *(Follows from 3 and 5.)*
7. The proofs' starting points (change, composition, abstract objects, the essence–existence distinction, contingency) are presupposed by any possible empirical inquiry. *(Chapters 1 and 7.)*
8. So the proofs are philosophical, not scientific: no discovery can overturn them, they aren't god-of-the-gaps arguments, and their core survives outdated science. *(Follows from 4 and 7; the kernel-and-husk point.)*
9. The causal series they trace are hierarchical, operating here and now, not linear chains in time. *(Chapter 1's cup, desk, and floor.)*
10. So they don't need a beginning of the universe; they conclude to a cause that sustains everything at every moment, not a first event or a deist starter. *(Follows from 9; divine conservation.)*
11. Classical theism holds that God is pure actuality, absolutely simple, subsistent existence itself, and not a being among beings. *(The introduction on simplicity; chapters 6 and 7.)*
12. Theistic personalism treats God as the greatest member of the genus "person," and so as composite. *(Chapter 6.)*
13. Since every proof concludes to a cause with no composition, the proofs support classical theism and would not support a personalist "god." *(Follows from 11 and 12.)*
14. All five proofs converge on one and the same God, and there can be only one. *(Promised in the introduction, shown in chapter 6.)*
15. Each proof has two stages: something fitting a key description exists, and whatever fits it has the divine attributes. *(The introduction's description of chapters 1–5.)*
16. So the complaint that a first cause isn't God is answered by the structure of the book itself. *(Follows from 15.)*
17. Each proof is presented informally, then formally, then defended against objections, and the formal versions don't stand alone. *(The introduction's account of its method.)*
18. Natural theology doesn't establish revealed doctrines; whether revelation has occurred is beyond the book's scope. *(The introduction and chapter 7.)*
19. Therefore, if the proofs succeed, atheism is false, demonstrative natural theology deserves to be mainstream again, and the remaining debate is among theists about revelation. *(The introduction's conclusion.)*

## 6. Objections and replies

**"No one can claim a proof, since so many people reject it."** The book's last objection, which gets three replies. First, much criticism attacks caricatures (such as "everything has a cause"), and rejection by the ill-informed proves nothing. Second, there is a double standard: no one says an argument for materialism simply "fails" because someone objects to it, yet theistic arguments are treated as refuted by any objection, and an old remark of Hume's is treated as final. (Feser cites Hilary Putnam's point that no argument by the most celebrated analytic philosophers convinces every rational person either.) Third, critics misunderstand "demonstration." A deduction from certain premises need not win immediate or universal assent, and its premises may take real work to defend. What it requires is that, in the end, it can be defended against every objection.

**"These are just the old Five Ways, refuted long ago."** The book isn't about the Five Ways, and their neglect reflects academic fashion, not merit. Standard "refutations" usually target caricatures, and the book answers specific Humean and Kantian objections one by one.

**"These arguments rest on outdated science."** Science refuted particular claims of medieval Aristotelianism, like geocentrism, not the metaphysical claim that change is the actualization of potential, which science itself presupposes, since every experiment involves change.

**"Science is the only source of knowledge, and God is a gap-filler."** Gap arguments allow that a natural explanation might someday be found; the proofs claim no alternative is possible in principle. Insisting that all real explanation must be scientific assumes natural theology is wrong without showing it, and scientism refutes itself: "only science gives knowledge" is not a scientific finding.

**"Even if there is a first cause, it isn't God."** This is exactly what Stage 2 of every proof addresses, and chapter 6 develops the divine attributes at length. Historical defenders such as Aquinas and Samuel Clarke wrote hundreds of pages deriving these attributes, arguments the critics usually don't engage.

**"Why assume the universe had a beginning?"** The proofs don't assume it. They grant a possibly beginningless universe and ask what sustains things in existence right now.

**"What caused God?"** None of the proofs says "everything has a cause." They say that whatever has unactualized potential, or parts, or a distinction between essence and existence, or contingency, needs a cause. And each concludes to something that lacks exactly that feature, so it not only has no cause but could not have one.

**"The God of the philosophers isn't the God of religion."** The proofs don't establish the Trinity or the Bible's inspiration, but that is irrelevant: they target one belief central to many religions, and if they succeed, atheism is false. Ordinary believers affirm the same basic attributes with less technical understanding, as a layperson believes in the same atoms a physicist does.

**"Go one god further."** This is a category mistake. The God of classical theism is not one more member of a class of gods alongside Zeus and Thor, any more than triangularity is one more triangle, only better drawn. He is being itself, not a being.

**"A designer would be more complex than the universe."** The proofs aren't design arguments. They don't argue from complexity or probability, and they agree that anything complex would need a cause. That is precisely why they conclude to something absolutely simple.

**"Arguments for God are just rationalizations."** This is an ad hominem that poisons the well; an argument's soundness doesn't depend on the arguer's motives. And motives cut both ways: Feser quotes Thomas Nagel admitting he hopes there is no God.

**"Claiming certainty is arrogant."** Natural theology was historically confident, and the confidence comes from the kind of argument involved. If the premises are certain and the logic valid, the conclusion is certain. Whether that is so can only be settled by examining the arguments.

## 7. Common misunderstandings

- **"It's a book about Aquinas' Five Ways."** It isn't. There is no exegesis, and three of the five proofs are not Aquinas' arguments.
- **"The proofs are about the Big Bang."** They grant that the universe might have no beginning. They are about what keeps things in existence now.
- **"They are fancy design arguments."** They have nothing to do with complexity, probability, or Intelligent Design.
- **"A real proof would convince everyone."** A demonstration can be sound even if it takes hard work to defend and even if informed people resist it.
- **"They get you a first cause, not God."** Every proof has a Stage 2 deriving the divine attributes.
- **"God is the biggest, most powerful being."** On classical theism, God is not a being among beings but being itself, absolutely simple.
- **"Classical theists think God is impersonal."** Most attribute intellect, will, and love to God, understood analogically.
- **"If the proofs work, they prove my religion."** Revelation is a separate question.

## 8. How to explain it to others

**The simple version.** "Most arguments for God you've heard are guesses: the world looks designed, so probably someone designed it. This book defends an older kind. It starts from facts you can't deny, like that things change and have parts, and tries to prove that something must be keeping everything in existence right now, and that this something must be one, eternal, immaterial, all-powerful, all-knowing, and good. It isn't about who lit the fuse at the Big Bang. It's about what's holding everything up at this moment."

**The best analogy: the cup on the desk.** A cup sits three feet off the floor because the desk holds it up, the floor holds the desk, the foundation holds the floor, all right now. None holds anything up by its own power; each passes along borrowed power, and adding more desks, even infinitely many, explains nothing unless something has the power underived. Now replace "held up" with "kept in existence" and apply it to everything. The analogy makes three points at once: the causes operate now, intermediate causes explain nothing on their own, and "first cause" means underived rather than earliest.

**A second analogy: the detective and the geometer.** A detective concludes the butler probably did it, and new evidence could change that. A geometry proof is not open to revision by new evidence. Design arguments are detective reasoning; Feser's proofs aim to be geometry, with starting points that any inquiry presupposes.

**Order of presentation.** Follow Feser's method: homely examples first, then concepts, then formal steps. Always include Stage 2, introduce classical theism early, and be clear that the aim is to refute atheism, not to prove a particular religion.

## 9. Key takeaways and review questions

### Key takeaways

- Natural theology asks what reason alone, without revelation, can know about God.
- The five proofs, inspired by Aristotle, Plotinus, Augustine, Aquinas, and Leibniz, start from change, composition, abstract objects, the essence–existence distinction, and the principle of sufficient reason.
- Each has two stages (something fitting a description exists; it has the divine attributes) and is presented informally, then formally, then defended.
- Chapter 6 shows the proofs reach one God and derives the attributes; chapter 7 answers objections.
- The proofs aim at demonstration, not probability, and concern a cause sustaining things here and now.
- They are metaphysical, not scientific, and reach the absolutely simple God of classical theism.
- If they succeed, the serious debate is among theists about revelation, which the book leaves open.

### Ten review questions with brief answers

1. **How does Feser define natural theology?** As the study of what unaided reason, apart from divine revelation, can know about God's existence, nature, and relationship to the world.
2. **Why isn't this a book about the Five Ways?** Only two of its proofs come from Aquinas, three are not arguments he develops, and Feser defends the arguments directly rather than interpreting any text.
3. **What do Stage 1 and Stage 2 of each proof do?** Stage 1 shows that something fitting a key description exists; Stage 2 shows that it must have the divine attributes.
4. **Why does each chapter start informally?** To ease newcomers in, and to explain concepts and head off misunderstandings before the formal statement.
5. **What is the starting point of the Augustinian proof?** The reality of universals, propositions, possibilities, and other abstract objects, which cannot exist in a Platonic mind-independent realm and so must ultimately exist in a divine intellect.
6. **What is a demonstration, and how is it refuted?** A deduction from certain premises. You refute it by showing a premise false, the logic invalid, or the question begged, not by proposing a simpler rival hypothesis.
7. **Why don't the proofs depend on the universe having a beginning?** They concern hierarchical causes that sustain things in existence at every moment, so even an eternal universe would need such a cause.
8. **What separates classical theism from theistic personalism?** Personalists put God within the genus "person" and so treat him as composite; classical theists hold that God is absolutely simple and in no genus, though they still regard him as personal.
9. **What are Feser's three replies to "no one can claim a proof since many reject it"?** Many critics attack caricatures; skeptics hold theistic arguments to a double standard; and demonstration doesn't require universal or immediate assent.
10. **Where does Feser say the real debate lies?** Among theists of different kinds, over what, if anything, God has revealed. The book leaves that debate open.
