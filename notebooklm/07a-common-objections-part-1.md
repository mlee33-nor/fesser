# Common Objections I: "What Caused God?" and Other Misfires

## A study guide to Edward Feser, *Five Proofs of the Existence of God*, Chapter 7 (first half, pages 249–273)

### Orientation

Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (Ignatius Press, 2017) defends five classical arguments for God. The **Aristotelian proof** reasons from change to a purely actual Unmoved Mover. The **Neo-Platonic proof** reasons from composite things to an absolutely simple One. The **Augustinian proof** reasons from abstract truths (numbers, propositions, universals) to a divine intellect. The **Thomistic proof** reasons from the distinction between a thing's essence and its existence to a being whose essence just *is* existence. The **rationalist proof** reasons from contingent things, by the principle of sufficient reason, to a necessary being. Chapter 6 then argues at length that whatever these proofs arrive at must be one, simple, immutable, eternal, immaterial, all-powerful, all-knowing, and perfectly good.

Chapter 7 turns to the objections. This study guide covers its first half: the objections aimed chiefly at "First Cause" or "cosmological" reasoning. These include "If everything has a cause, what caused God?", "Maybe the universe itself is the uncaused cause", "Quantum events have no cause", "Why assume the universe had a beginning?", "fallacy of composition", "even a first cause wouldn't be God", "the cosmological argument depends on the ontological argument", "god of the gaps", and the physicists' claim that the universe came from nothing. The second half of the chapter, covered in a separate module, deals with scientism, laws of nature, "who designed the designer?", the Zeus-and-Thor comparison, evil, divine hiddenness, and similar challenges.

This part of the course is where understanding turns into conversation. You will meet these objections far more often than any technical criticism, so it pays to know why each one fails and to be able to say so calmly in a sentence or two.

## 1. Why this chapter matters

Most people who reject arguments for God have never met the arguments Feser defends. What they have met is a cartoon: "Everything has a cause, so the universe has a cause, and that cause is God." Next to the cartoon they learned a comeback: "Then what caused God?" The cartoon and the comeback have been repeated so often, in popular books, textbooks, and even professional philosophy, that many people assume the whole topic was settled long ago.

Feser's aim in this chapter is to show that the most common objections **misfire**. They aren't wrong because they are impolite or because the people who make them are unintelligent. They are wrong because they never make contact with the arguments actually defended by Aristotle, Aquinas, Leibniz, and their modern successors. Feser admits that some of this ground was covered in earlier chapters. He returns to it for two reasons. There are further objections to address, and some very weak objections are so widespread, and so heavily promoted by the "New Atheist" writers (Sam Harris, Richard Dawkins, Daniel Dennett, Christopher Hitchens, Victor Stenger, Lawrence Krauss, Alex Rosenberg, A. C. Grayling, and on this topic Stephen Hawking and Leonard Mlodinow), that they deserve a closer and deeper look.

The chapter matters for three reasons:

- **It clears the ground.** Until the cartoon is gone, nobody can hear the real arguments.
- **It teaches a method.** The same few diagnostic questions dispose of objection after objection. Once you learn them you can handle objections that are not in the book.
- **It shows where the real debate is.** Feser openly grants that a serious critic could argue the premises are false, or that notions like "pure actuality" are secretly incoherent. That is a real debate worth having. What a critic cannot do is make it go away with one clever-sounding question.

## 2. Key concepts and vocabulary

**Straw man.** A distorted version of someone's argument, knocked down in place of the real one. The "everything has a cause" argument is Feser's central example.

**The "basic cosmological argument."** Philosopher Robin Le Poidevin's label for a three-step argument: whatever exists has a cause; nothing causes itself; the universe exists; therefore the universe has a cause outside itself. Le Poidevin himself admits that no one has ever defended exactly this argument, but he uses it as a starting point anyway.

**Efficient cause.** In the traditional sense, something *other than* the effect that brings the effect about. The builder is the efficient cause of the house. Traditional theists deny that God has an efficient cause of any kind.

**Sufficient reason (explanation).** Whatever accounts for why something exists or is the way it is. This is a broader idea than efficient cause. Every cause is an explanation, but not every explanation is a cause. A necessary being would be explained by its own nature without being caused by anything.

**Principle of sufficient reason (PSR).** The principle, defended in chapter 5, that everything has an explanation. For contingent things the explanation lies outside them. For a necessary being it lies in the being itself.

**Pure actuality.** The conclusion of the Aristotelian proof. Pure actuality has no unrealized potential at all. Since causing something means actualizing a potential in it, what is purely actual could not be caused.

**Absolute simplicity.** The conclusion of the Neo-Platonic proof. Something absolutely simple has no parts of any kind. Composite things need something to account for their parts being combined, but the absolutely simple has nothing to combine.

**Essence and existence.** A thing's essence is *what* it is; its existence is the fact *that* it is. In ordinary things the two are distinct, so something must join existence to the essence. The Thomistic proof concludes to a being whose essence simply *is* existence.

**Contingent and necessary.** A contingent thing could have failed to exist or could have been otherwise, so it needs an explanation beyond itself. A necessary being could not have failed to exist.

**The principled criterion.** This is a course term, not Feser's. Each causal proof identifies a specific feature that makes a thing need a cause: potential that must be actualized, parts that must be combined, an essence that must have existence added, or contingency. It then argues for a first cause lacking that feature. This criterion is the key to answering "What caused God?" and "Why not stop at the universe?"

**Per se (hierarchical) causal series.** A series in which each intermediate member has its causal power only *derivatively*, here and now, from something higher up. The standard example: a hand moves a stick, which moves a stone. The stick does nothing unless the hand is acting. Such a series must have a first member that has its power in itself, and that holds however long the series is.

**Per accidens (linear, temporal) causal series.** A series in which earlier causes produce later effects and can then drop out of existence, like parents and children or a line of falling dominoes. The book's proofs do *not* claim such a series must have a beginning.

**Fallacy of composition.** Wrongly assuming that a whole has a property because each of its parts has it. Every brick being light doesn't make the wall light. Not all part-to-whole reasoning is fallacious, though. If every Lego brick in a model is red, the model is red.

**Chance.** An event that results from independent causal lines crossing without anyone planning it. Chance presupposes causes.

**Indeterminism.** The absence of a *deterministic* cause, meaning a cause that fixes exactly one outcome. Quantum mechanics may show that some events are indeterministic. That is not the same as showing they are uncaused.

**Special pleading.** Applying a standard everywhere except in the one case where it is inconvenient for you.

**Logical, conditional, and metaphysical necessity.**
- *Logical* necessity is truth by definition, as in "a bachelor is unmarried."
- *Conditional* necessity is being necessary only relative to something else, as oxygen is necessary for fire.
- *Metaphysical* necessity, as the proofs use it, is unconditional necessity grounded in a thing's nature. Something with no potential, no parts, and no gap between its essence and its existence has nothing in it that could fail.

**God of the gaps.** Appealing to God to fill a current hole in scientific knowledge. On this approach a natural explanation might in principle be correct, and God is claimed only as the more probable one.

**Metaphysical demonstration.** An argument claiming to show that its conclusion is the *only possible* account of its starting point. Such an argument is judged by whether its premises are true and its logic valid, not by parsimony or by how well it fits current theory.

**"Nothing."** Strictly, the absence of anything whatsoever. It is not empty space, not a quantum vacuum, not a set of laws. It is not a kind of stuff at all.

## 3. The story of the chapter, told informally

### Scene one: the most famous objection

Picture a dinner-party debate. Someone gives the First Cause argument, and someone else smiles and asks, "But if everything has a cause, what caused God?" The room nods as if the matter were settled.

Feser says the objection fails twice over.

First, **the argument never said everything has a cause.** No premise like that appears in the book's proofs or in the great historical versions. With no such premise, there is no contradiction for the theist to fall into and no "exception" to complain about.

Second, and more important, **the arguments explain why the First Cause couldn't have a cause.** Each one begins by asking what it is about ordinary things that makes them need a cause, then argues that the chain of such things must end in something lacking exactly that feature:

- Changing things have potentials being made actual, and no potential can actualize itself. So there must be something *purely actual*.
- Composite things need something to unite their parts. So there must be something with no parts.
- In ordinary things, *what* they are differs from the fact *that* they are, so something must give them existence. So there must be something whose nature is to exist.
- Contingent things might not have existed. So there must be something that could not fail to exist.

Now put the dinner-party question in the argument's own terms. What actualized the potentials of the thing with no potentials? What assembled the parts of the thing with no parts? These questions aren't hard. They are confused.

Feser adds a fair concession. A critic may argue that the proofs don't really establish such a being, or that notions like pure actuality are secretly incoherent. Those are real objections, and they require working through the details. What won't do is one smug question that assumes centuries of thinkers missed the most obvious point imaginable. (A footnote notes that the Augustinian proof isn't a causal argument, so the objection doesn't even seem to apply to it.)

### Scene two: the cartoon everyone attacks

Why is such a weak objection everywhere? Because critics nearly always target a cartoon of the argument. Feser finds it across the board. Daniel Dennett gives its "simplest form" as: everything must have a cause, so the universe does, namely God. Nigel Warburton, in a popular introduction, describes tracing a chain of prior causes back to a first one. The logician Graham Priest motivates it with everyday cases like illnesses and car breakdowns. The philosopher of religion Robin Le Poidevin lays out a three-step "basic cosmological argument." The follow-up is always the same: ask what caused God, say that if nothing did we may as well say nothing caused the universe, complain that the argument assumes a beginning, and treat the serious versions as desperate patches.

Feser's reply is blunt. **No major defender of the cosmological argument ever gave this argument.** He runs through some thirty names, from Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus, Avicenna, Maimonides, Aquinas, Scotus, and Leibniz down to modern defenders like Swinburne, Pruss, Oderberg, and William Lane Craig. Le Poidevin himself admits that "no-one has defended a cosmological argument of precisely this form," yet uses it as a stepping-stone anyway.

Feser's analogy shows why that is unfair. Imagine Intelligent Design advocates saying "the basic Darwinian thesis" is that a monkey once gave birth to a human baby. They cite no Darwinian who said it and admit no one holds it, but they call it a helpful stepping-stone. Darwinians would rightly be furious, because the framing makes everything they actually claim look like damage control for an absurd idea.

Nor are defenders secretly committed to the cartoon. None of them relies on "everything has a cause," and some explicitly deny it. None simply assumes a beginning; only the kalām version even tries to prove one. And most versions make no claim about the universe as a whole.

### Scene three: a family tree of a mistake

How did the cartoon become conventional wisdom? Critics tend to learn about the argument from other critics, and generations of copying did the rest. For the deeper history, Feser relies on the Thomist **W. Norris Clarke**, whose 1970 article diagnosed a "curious blind spot" in English-language atheist argument. Clarke found the cartoon in mid-century textbooks such as John Hospers' *An Introduction to Philosophical Analysis*. He traced it to Bertrand Russell's *Why I Am Not a Christian*, back through John Stuart Mill and his father James Mill, and ultimately to David Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*.

Clarke made a pointed observation. If anyone really did argue from "every being has a cause," the refutation would be crushing. But the refutation is so easy that it should have made critics suspicious, and none of them ever quotes a theistic philosopher who argued that way. Hospers had sneered that "many people" use the argument to reach God and then forget it. Feser asks who these people are, and turns the jibe around: it is the critics who, having found a way to dismiss Aristotle, Aquinas, and Leibniz, forget what those thinkers actually said.

### Scene four: two meanings of "cause"

Clarke's account of the origin is the most illuminating part. Hume, he argues, misread two rationalists, René Descartes and Baruch Spinoza.

Descartes' Third Meditation contains an unusual "preservation" argument asking what keeps *him* in existence from moment to moment. The argument implies that in some sense everything has a cause, but it is still not the cartoon. It doesn't ask about the universe. "Everything has a cause" isn't one of its explicit premises. And it makes no exception for God, whom Descartes calls *self-caused*.

What Descartes and Spinoza really meant was close to the **principle of sufficient reason**: everything has an *explanation*. Contingent things are explained by something else; a necessary being is explained by its own nature. Every cause is an explanation, but not every explanation is a cause, and PSR-based arguments involve no exception and no contradiction.

The problem was the word "cause," which normally suggests an efficient cause distinct from its effect, the one kind of cause God has never been thought to have. Hume merged the two senses. He read "everything has a cause, meaning an explanation" as "everything has an efficient cause distinct from itself," and pinned that claim on cosmological arguers in general. Since they deny God has any such cause, they seemed to contradict themselves. Clarke calls the result "a monster of their own fabrication," a hybrid that neither Scholastics nor Cartesians would recognize. Hume had some excuse given Descartes' muddled terminology, but his successors have much less. Feser treats willingness to admit this history as a test of a critic's competence and honesty. He also notes that another Thomist, Richard F. Clarke, lodged the same complaint in 1889, and he expects the cartoon to keep surviving because habits die hard.

### Scene five: "Then why not stop at the universe?"

Hume, and after him Dawkins, Dennett, Grayling, Krauss, Rosenberg, and Stenger, suggest that if God can be uncaused, so can the universe, or the Big Bang, a multiverse, quantum events, or the laws of physics.

The principled criterion answers this directly. By each proof's standard, every candidate on that list needs a cause. The universe mixes actuality and potentiality. The Big Bang, each stage of a multiverse, and each quantum event (deterministic or not) are potentials being actualized. The laws of physics could have been different, so they too are merely potential. All of these are composite or lack absolute simplicity. None has an essence identical to its existence. All are contingent.

Feser gives the laws of physics special attention because people invoke them so casually. What *is* a law of nature?

1. If it is just a *statement that a regularity exists*, it describes what needs explaining rather than explaining it.
2. If it is an *abstract, Platonic entity*, we must explain how the world comes to follow it, and why it follows these laws rather than others.
3. If it is *shorthand for how things behave given their natures* (the Aristotelian view, defended by David Oderberg), it depends on those things.

On none of these views is a law the final explanation. The skeptic may reject all of this, but merely *proposing* the universe as the stopping point ignores the proofs rather than answering them.

Then comes a twist. If the universe really were uncaused, self-explanatory, and necessary, it would have distinctively divine attributes. That would be **pantheism**, God identical with the world. Many Hindus might welcome it. Jews, Christians, Muslims, and atheists could not.

### Scene six: chance and quantum physics

Stenger appeals to *chance*, Dennett and Rosenberg to *quantum mechanics*, and Grayling to *Hume*. Even setting aside that the proofs don't assume "everything has a cause," none of this touches the principles they do use.

**Chance** depends on causes. A farmer plowing a field strikes buried treasure. That is chance, but it happens only because two ordinary causal lines crossed: his decision to plow there that day, and someone else's decision to bury treasure in that spot. When a boulder loosened by an earthquake happens to smash another boulder, that happens only against a background of plate tectonics, gravity, and the solidity of rock. No defender of the argument ever denied that chance events occur.

**Quantum mechanics** shows at most that some events lack a *deterministic* cause. Neither the principle of causality nor PSR requires causes to be deterministic. And quantum events, however indeterministic, happen only within contingent quantum laws, which are merely potential until a universe governed by them is actualized. So appealing to quantum physics either misses the point or begs the question, since quantum reality is part of what needs explaining.

### Scene seven: Hume's thought experiment

Grayling treats Hume's claim that we can conceive of an effect without a cause as decisive. Feser gives three reasons it isn't.

1. **Special pleading.** No working scientist would accept "maybe there's just no cause" for something under investigation, so why accept it here? And, as Lloyd Gerson notes, the cosmological argument *is* an explanation. Saying there might be no explanation is no answer to one that has actually been given.
2. **Conceiving isn't possibility.** You can think of a triangle without thinking of it as three-sided, but no triangle lacks three sides. You can think of a man without thinking of his height, but no man lacks a height.
3. **Anscombe's point.** In Hume's picture of something suddenly appearing, what makes it an *uncaused* coming-into-being rather than one with an unseen cause, or a teleportation? Settling that seems to require causation. The natural way to tell "came into being" from "was moved here" is by a *generating* versus a *transporting* cause.

### Scene eight: beginnings and endless laws

Rosenberg and Krauss suggest that a beginningless multiverse, with our Big Bang branching off from a prior universe, removes the need for God. Krauss, citing Richard Feynman, adds that there might be endlessly deeper layers of physical law.

Feser's answers:

- **The arguments aren't about a beginning.** Even a beginningless universe or multiverse would need a divine cause to *keep it in existence* and to explain *why it exists at all*.
- **Laws can't be ultimate explanations**, however many layers there are.
- **Stacked laws would form a hierarchical series.** If each level holds only as a special case of a deeper level, each borrows its force from below. That is the per se structure of chapter 1, which needs a first member with underived causal power no matter how long the series runs.

The skeptic imagines a line of dominoes stretching into the past. The argument is about a stack of floors, each held up here and now by the one beneath.

### Scene nine: the fallacy of composition

Grayling, following Hume, says that once each member of a series is explained by an earlier one, nothing is left to explain, and that asking about "the whole" is a fallacy of composition. Feser notes that his arguments need no premise about the universe as a whole, and that chapter 5 showed the rationalist's inference from contingent parts to a contingent whole is legitimate, since contingency behaves more like color than like weight. Then he turns the charge around:

- An infinite series of **sticks**, none able to move anything on its own, still needs a mover outside the series.
- An infinite row of **mirrors** reflecting a face still needs a real face outside the row.
- An infinite chain of **geometry books** (Leibniz's example), each copied from an earlier one, never explains why the series has *this* content.

So the fallacy belongs to the critics. Explaining each member by its neighbor doesn't explain why there is any motion, image, or content in the series at all.

### Scene ten: "Why call it God?" and "What about the Trinity?"

Grayling, Dawkins, and Krauss say a first cause needn't be all-powerful, all-knowing, or good. Feser calls this as inept as "What caused God?" Aquinas spends roughly a hundred double-column pages of the *Summa Theologiae*, right after the Five Ways, and about two hundred pages of the *Summa Contra Gentiles* arguing for the divine attributes. Samuel Clarke's *Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God* does the same, as do Aristotle, Plotinus, Avicenna, Scotus, and Leibniz. The New Atheists never engage these arguments, and Feser's own chapters 1–6 argue for the attributes in detail.

Others say that even an all-powerful, all-knowing, good First Cause wouldn't show that God inspired the Bible or is a Trinity. True, Feser says, but irrelevant. The arguments aim at one claim that many religions share. If they succeed, atheism is false, and the remaining question is *which* theism is right. Faulting them for not proving more is like rejecting relativity because it says nothing about evolution.

### Scene eleven: the ontological-argument charge

Kant, echoed by Grayling, claims the cosmological argument secretly relies on the ontological argument. On Descartes' version of that argument, existence is a perfection, so a supremely perfect being must exist, which would make God exist *by definition*. Since the cosmological argument ends in a *necessary* being, Kant assumed it needed this definitional necessity. And since, on his view, the ontological argument fails, the cosmological argument falls with it.

Feser answers in two steps. First, Aquinas and most Thomists reject the ontological argument while defending the cosmological one, and they are not inconsistent. The necessity they ascribe to God isn't necessity by definition (chapter 6).

Second, Grayling objects that "metaphysical" necessity would only make God a necessary *condition* for the world, not something that couldn't fail to exist. Feser calls this a **false dichotomy**. Metaphysical necessity can be unconditional without being logical. The purely actual has no potential for non-existence. The absolutely simple has no parts that could come apart. The being whose essence *is* existence needs nothing added to exist. Feser adds that one should say God's essence *is* existence, not that it "includes" existence, since "includes" implies parts.

### Scene twelve: "god of the gaps" and "nothing"

Feser calls the god-of-the-gaps charge the central conceit of the New Atheism: all real explanation is scientific, so God is at best a bad hypothesis. Hitchens invokes Ockham's razor. Dawkins prefers a Big Bang singularity or an undiscovered physical principle. Harris says nobody knows how or why the universe began. Hawking and Mlodinow say that because of a law like gravity, "the universe can and will create itself from nothing," and Krauss wrote a whole book on the theme.

Feser finds two problems.

**First**, the charge misses the point or begs the question. A god-of-the-gaps explanation admits that a natural explanation might in principle be correct and claims only greater probability. Feser's arguments are strict demonstrations claiming no alternative is possible, so parsimony isn't the standard for judging them. And their starting points aren't discoveries that science could overturn. They are features any scientific theory presupposes: potentials actualized, composite things, essences that must receive existence, contingency.

**Second**, the "universe from nothing" is a nonstarter, because a law like gravity isn't nothing. Krauss's book, harshly criticized even by philosophers with no theological stake (notably David Albert in the *New York Times*), spends most of its pages on a universe arising from empty space with energy and laws, then retreats to the laws of quantum gravity, then to layers of laws, each time admitting that the starting point isn't really nothing. His talk of "candidates for nothingness" treats nothing as an exotic stuff that is hard to measure. But nothing is simply the absence of anything, and what Krauss is left with, laws, can't be the ultimate explanation.

## 4. The formal line of reasoning, step by step

This part of chapter 7 isn't a new proof. It's an argument *about* objections. Here is its structure, paraphrased, with a short justification for each step.

1. **An objection counts only if it targets the argument as its defenders actually state it.** This is just the rule against straw men, and the Darwinism analogy makes it vivid.
2. **The real arguments don't assume "everything has a cause," don't assume a beginning, and mostly don't argue from the universe as a whole.** Feser's roll call of some thirty defenders supports this, and even Le Poidevin admits nobody gave the "basic" argument.
3. **Each proof identifies what makes things need a cause (potential, parts, an essence distinct from existence, contingency) and concludes to a first cause lacking that feature.** This is the principled criterion. It makes God's uncausedness a conclusion, not an exception.
4. **The regress the proofs rule out is a hierarchical, here-and-now series, not a temporal one.** This comes from chapter 1's hand, stick, and stone, and from the sticks and mirrors.
5. **So objections that attribute "everything has a cause" or a beginning to the arguments attack a straw man.** This follows from steps 1 and 2 and disposes of "What caused God?" and the "basic cosmological argument."
6. **The straw man arose from Hume's fusion of "cause as explanation" with "efficient cause distinct from its effect."** This is Clarke's genealogy through Descartes, Spinoza, the Mills, Russell, and the textbooks.
7. **Objections that offer the universe, Big Bang, multiverse, quantum events, or laws as the stopping point fail, because each candidate has the very feature that calls for a cause.** This follows from step 3. On any view, laws aren't ultimate, and success would yield pantheism.
8. **Objections that appeal to a beginningless past or endless layers of laws confuse the two kinds of series.** This follows from step 4, since stacked laws would themselves form a hierarchical series.
9. **Objections that deny causal principles rely on equivocation or bad inference.** Chance presupposes causes, indeterminism isn't the same as uncausedness, and conceiving things apart doesn't show they can exist apart.
10. **Objections that appeal to science or brute fact beg the question.** The proofs start from what any science presupposes, the physics invoked itself needs explaining, and "maybe there's no explanation" is special pleading.
11. **Objections that misread technical terms, or that are true but irrelevant, leave the conclusion untouched.** Necessity isn't the same as definition, nothing isn't a kind of stuff, the Trinity is a further question, and the divine attributes are argued for at length.
12. **So every common objection considered here misfires.** This follows from steps 5 through 11.
13. **A critic remains free to challenge the real premises or argue that the key concepts are incoherent.** Feser grants this explicitly.
14. **Therefore the burden returns to the critic, who must engage the actual premises. A one-line retort can't substitute for that.** This follows from steps 12 and 13.

## 5. Every objection and its reply, in brief

For quick review: each objection, the diagnostic failure behind it, and a calm one-sentence reply for conversation. (Full replies are in section 3.)

- **"If everything has a cause, what caused God?"** Straw man, plus it ignores the principled criterion. *Say:* "The argument doesn't say everything has a cause. It says things that *can* be caused need one, and concludes to something that *can't* be."
- **"The 'basic cosmological argument' is a fair simplification."** Straw man. *Say:* "A simplification keeps the premises. This one swaps out every one of them."
- **"Descartes and Spinoza said everything has a cause."** Equivocation on "cause." *Say:* "They meant everything has an explanation, and God's explanation is his own nature, so there's no exception to catch."
- **"Maybe the universe (Big Bang, multiverse, quantum events, laws) is the uncaused cause."** Ignores the criterion. *Say:* "The universe changes, has parts, and could have been different, which are exactly the features that call for a cause. And if it didn't have them, it would be divine."
- **"Chance shows not everything has a cause."** Equivocation. *Say:* "Chance is causal lines crossing. It needs causes; it doesn't replace them."
- **"Quantum events have no cause."** Equivocation and question-begging. *Say:* "Not deterministic isn't the same as not caused, and the quantum laws need explaining too."
- **"Maybe there's just no explanation."** Special pleading. *Say:* "You'd never accept 'maybe it has no cause' in a lab, and here an explanation has actually been offered."
- **"We can conceive of an effect without a cause."** Bad inference and underdescription. *Say:* "Imagining a thing without its cause is like imagining a triangle without thinking 'three-sided.'"
- **"Why assume a beginning?"** Straw man, and it confuses the two kinds of series. *Say:* "Grant an infinite past. The question is what keeps it all existing *now*."
- **"Maybe it's laws all the way down."** Per se/per accidens confusion. *Say:* "More borrowers never produce a lender."
- **"Fallacy of composition."** Misreads the argument, and the critic commits the fallacy. *Say:* "Explaining each mirror by the next never tells you where the face came from."
- **"A first cause needn't be God."** Ignores the arguments for the attributes. *Say:* "The attributes are argued for across hundreds of pages. The objection only works if you haven't read them."
- **"It doesn't prove the Trinity."** True but irrelevant. *Say:* "It settles whether God exists, not which religion is true."
- **"It presupposes the ontological argument."** Equivocation on "necessary." *Say:* "'Necessary' here means 'nothing in it could fail to exist,' not 'true by definition.'"
- **"Metaphysical necessity is merely conditional."** False dichotomy. *Say:* "God isn't necessary *relative to* the world. He's necessary because nothing in him could fail."
- **"God of the gaps."** Question-begging. *Say:* "Every mechanism science finds will involve change, parts, and contingency, and that's where the argument starts."
- **"Physics shows a universe from nothing."** Equivocation on "nothing." *Say:* "Empty space, energy, and quantum laws are something, so why do *they* exist?"

## 6. Common misunderstandings

- **"The argument starts from 'everything has a cause.'"** It doesn't. That premise belongs to a cartoon that goes back to Hume's confusion.
- **"Saying God is uncaused is special pleading."** It's a principled conclusion. Things need causes because of specific features, and God lacks those features.
- **"The argument needs the Big Bang, or a first moment of time."** The book's proofs work even for an eternal universe. They concern present dependence.
- **"If God can be uncaused, so can the universe."** The universe has the very features that require a cause. And if it didn't, it would be divine, which is pantheism.
- **"Quantum physics proved some events are uncaused."** At most it showed some causes aren't deterministic.
- **"Whatever I can imagine is possible."** You can imagine a triangle without thinking of its three sides, but no triangle lacks them.
- **"Explaining each link explains the chain."** The mirrors show otherwise.
- **"The argument is a scientific hypothesis that science can replace."** It is a metaphysical demonstration about what any science presupposes.
- **"'Necessary being' means 'exists by definition.'"** Not in these arguments. That's why Aquinas could reject the ontological argument.
- **"Physicists have explained how something comes from literally nothing."** Their "nothing" is always something.

## 7. How to explain it to others

### The simple version

"Most objections to the First Cause argument attack a version nobody ever defended: 'everything has a cause, so the universe has a cause.' The real argument is narrower. Things that change, that are made of parts, or that could have been otherwise depend on something else, here and now. Follow that dependence and you have to reach something with none of those features. That's why 'What caused God?' misses: it's asking what caused the one thing the argument shows *can't* be caused. And it's why the universe can't be the stopping point: the universe has all those features."

### The best analogy: the borrowed-money chain

Imagine everyone in a line pays you with money they borrowed from the next person. Make the line as long as you like, even infinite, and you still haven't explained where the money came from. Somewhere there has to be someone who *has* money without borrowing it.

This one picture does three jobs:

1. **It shows the structure of the arguments.** The question isn't how long the line is, or whether it had a beginning. The question is whether anyone has money that isn't borrowed.
2. **It shows why "What caused God?" misfires.** Asking "Who lent money to the person who didn't borrow?" misunderstands the whole point. The original owner is, by definition of the conclusion, the one who didn't borrow.
3. **It shows why the universe can't be the answer.** The universe, the multiverse, and an endless stack of laws are all just more borrowers.

(Add a caveat when you use it. The "money" stands for existence and causal power, and the true source isn't one more person in the line but the source of the whole line.)

### Tone

Feser writes with sharp, polemical energy. In conversation you'll usually do better with calm generosity. Acknowledge that the objection is the one almost everyone learned, give your one-sentence reply, and then hand the conversation back with a question that points to a real premise. For example: "What do you think a law of nature actually *is*?" or "Does the universe change? Does it have parts?"

## 8. Key takeaways

- The famous objection "What caused God?" targets a premise, "everything has a cause," that no major defender of the cosmological argument ever used.
- The real arguments explain *why* the First Cause can't be caused. It lacks the very feature (potential, parts, an essence distinct from existence, contingency) that makes other things need causes.
- The cartoon version spread through the Hume–Russell tradition. According to W. Norris Clarke, it began with Hume confusing "cause" in the sense of explanation with "cause" in the sense of an outside producer.
- The universe, the Big Bang, a multiverse, quantum events, and physical laws all have the features that call for a cause. If the universe didn't, it would be divine, which is pantheism rather than atheism.
- Chance presupposes causes. Indeterminism isn't uncausedness. Conceiving things apart doesn't show they can exist apart.
- The proofs don't need a beginning of time. They concern present, hierarchical dependence, and an endless stack of laws would itself be such a hierarchy.
- In the composition debate, it's the critic who commits the fallacy, as the sticks, mirrors, and geometry books show.
- Arguments for the divine attributes are abundant, and failing to prove the Trinity isn't a failure to prove God.
- "Necessary" in these arguments means grounded in God's nature, not true by definition, so they don't depend on the ontological argument.
- The proofs aren't god-of-the-gaps guesses, and the physicists' "nothing" is always something.
- The real debate is over the premises themselves, and that is the conversation a serious critic should be having.

## 9. Ten review questions with brief answers

1. **What premise do critics wrongly attribute to the cosmological argument?**
   "Everything has a cause." No major defender uses it, and some explicitly deny it.

2. **Even setting the straw man aside, why is "What caused God?" confused?**
   Because each argument concludes to something that lacks the feature that makes things need a cause, so the question asks what caused the uncausable.

3. **What was Feser's point with the Darwinian "monkey gave birth to a human" analogy?**
   That describing a view by a version nobody holds, even as a "stepping-stone," distorts the whole discussion and makes the real view look like a patch job.

4. **According to W. Norris Clarke, what confusion started the Hume–Russell tradition?**
   Hume merged "everything has a cause in the sense of a sufficient reason" (Descartes, Spinoza) with "everything has an efficient cause distinct from itself," then pinned the result on theists.

5. **Why can't the laws of physics be the ultimate explanation?**
   If they merely describe regularities, they explain nothing. If they are abstract entities, the world's participation in them needs explaining. If they summarize how things behave, they depend on those things.

6. **What does the farmer who finds buried treasure show?**
   That chance events arise from the crossing of ordinary causal lines, so chance presupposes causation rather than refuting it.

7. **What does Anscombe say is wrong with Hume's imagined uncaused event?**
   Nothing in it distinguishes an uncaused appearance from one with an unseen cause or from teleportation, and specifying the difference brings causation back.

8. **Why doesn't a beginningless multiverse remove the need for a first cause?**
   Because the arguments concern what sustains things in existence and explains why they exist at all, and that dependence holds with or without a beginning.

9. **How does Feser answer Kant's claim that the cosmological argument depends on the ontological argument?**
   Aquinas rejects the ontological argument yet accepts the cosmological one. The necessity at issue isn't definitional but metaphysical, grounded in pure actuality, simplicity, or essence being existence, and Grayling's "logical or merely conditional" choice is a false dichotomy.

10. **What is wrong with the claim that physics shows the universe came from nothing?**
    The starting points (a law like gravity, space with energy, the laws of quantum gravity, layers of laws) are all something, while nothing is simply the absence of anything.
