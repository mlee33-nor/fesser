# The Aristotelian Proof: From a Cooling Cup of Coffee to the Unmoved Mover

*A study guide to Chapter 1 of Edward Feser's* Five Proofs of the Existence of God *(Ignatius Press, 2017), pages 17–68*

## Orientation

Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* defends five classical arguments for God's existence, each drawn from a major tradition in Western philosophy, followed by chapters on the nature of God and on common objections. Chapter 1, "The Aristotelian Proof," comes first for a reason. It is the most foundational argument in the book, and it introduces the concepts every later chapter relies on: **actuality and potentiality**, the **principle of causality**, the difference between **linear** and **hierarchical** chains of causes, the idea of a **first cause** as a cause with underived power rather than an earliest cause, and the **principle of proportionate causality**. This guide is self-contained. It explains the chapter's key ideas, tells the argument as a story, restates Feser's 50-step formal version in plain language, goes through every objection the chapter answers, and closes with tips for explaining the argument to others, key takeaways, and review questions.

## Why This Chapter Matters

Most people's mental picture of a "first cause" argument goes like this: everything has a cause, the causes go back in time, they can't go back forever, so something started the universe, and that something is God. Skeptics have well-known replies. What caused God? Why couldn't the universe be eternal? Maybe the Big Bang had a natural cause.

Feser's Aristotelian proof is a different argument altogether, and seeing why is half the battle.

- **It doesn't say everything has a cause.** It says only that whatever goes from being *potentially* some way to being *actually* that way needs something already actual to bring that about.
- **It doesn't need the universe to have had a beginning.** Feser grants, for the sake of argument, that the universe (or a multiverse) might have existed forever. Aristotle himself believed the world was eternal.
- **It isn't about the past at all.** It is about what keeps things in existence *right now*, at this very moment and every moment they exist.
- **It doesn't stop at a vague first cause.** Its second stage argues that whatever is at the bottom of reality must be one, unchanging, eternal, immaterial, perfect, good, all-powerful, intelligent and all-knowing, which is just what classical theism means by God.

The chapter also matters because it takes modern science seriously. Feser answers challenges drawn from David Hume, Immanuel Kant, Bertrand Russell, Newton's law of inertia, Einstein's relativity, quantum mechanics, radioactive decay, and the physicist Arthur Eddington. Its central lesson here is powerful and widely applicable: physics, because of its mathematical method, leaves certain features of reality out of its descriptions by design, and **the absence of something from a representation is not evidence of its absence from reality.**

Finally, this chapter is a toolkit. The Neo-Platonic, Augustinian, Thomistic and Rationalist proofs all share its basic shape: a chain of dependence happening *now*, which must end in something that is not itself dependent. Learn this chapter well and the rest of the book becomes much easier.

## Key Concepts and Vocabulary

**Change.** For Aristotle, change is the *actualization of a potential*: something that could be a certain way comes to be that way. There are four kinds. *Qualitative* change is a change in some quality, like coffee cooling. *Local* change is change of place, like a leaf falling. *Quantitative* change is change of size or amount, like a puddle growing. *Substantial* change is one kind of thing becoming another, like a fly dying when swatted, so that a living thing becomes dead matter.

**Actuality (act).** The way a thing actually is here and now. Only what is actual can do anything or cause anything. The coffee's actual heat and the actual coolness of the room's air are actualities.

**Potentiality (potency).** A thing's real but unrealized capacity to be or become some way. Potentiality is not nothing: it is specific. Hot coffee has the potential to become cold, to stain the carpet, or to perk you up. It does not have the potential to become chicken soup, fuel a car, or turn into a live chicken. That difference shows that a potential is something real.

**The principle of causality.** No potential can be actualized except by something already actual. In other words, whatever goes from potential to actual is caused. This is not the claim that "everything has a cause."

**Linear causal series (per accidens, or "accidentally ordered").** A series of causes spread out through time, in which each member has its own causal power relative to the others. Example: you press a button, which turns on the air conditioner, which cools the air, which cools your coffee. Once you've turned it on, the air conditioner keeps running even if you leave the house. Another example: Al fathers Bob, who fathers Chuck. Bob can father Chuck whether or not Al is still alive. Feser grants that such a series could, in principle, go back forever.

**Hierarchical causal series (per se, or "essentially ordered").** A series in which every member except the first has its causal power only in a borrowed, derivative way, and exercises it only while it is itself being acted on. Example: your coffee cup is held three feet off the ground by the desk, which is held up by the floor, which is held up by the foundation, which rests on the earth. All of this happens at once. If the floor gives way, the desk and cup fall immediately. Such a series *must* have a first member.

**Instrumental cause.** A cause that passes along power it has received from something else, like a paintbrush in a painter's hand or a stick used to push a stone. The painter paints the picture; the brush is just the instrument.

**First cause (primary cause).** In a hierarchical series, the "first" cause is the one whose causal power is built in rather than borrowed: it can actualize without having to be actualized. "First" here means first in the order of dependence, not first in time and not "number one" in a finite list.

**Pure actuality.** Something with no potentiality whatsoever: nothing about it could be other than it is, and nothing in it needs to be actualized. It doesn't merely *have* actuality; it *is* actuality itself.

**Unactualized actualizer / Unmoved Mover.** Aristotle called the ultimate cause the "Unmoved Mover." Feser's more precise term is "unactualized actualizer": something that actualizes other things' potentials without being actualized itself.

**Sustaining (conserving) cause.** A cause that keeps something in existence here and now, as opposed to the cause that originally produced it. The brewing process made your coffee; the Aristotelian argument asks what keeps it existing at this instant.

**Privation.** The absence of something a thing's nature requires for it to be complete, such as a squirrel crippled by a car or a tree with damaged roots. Imperfection and badness are privations: failures to realize some potential proper to a thing.

**Principle of proportionate causality.** Whatever is in an effect must in some way be in its cause, because a cause can't give what it doesn't have. A cause can "have" what is in its effect *formally* (having the very same feature), *virtually* (having the power to get or produce it), or *eminently* (having it in a higher way, such as the power to bring such things into existence in the first place).

**Intellect.** The capacity to grasp universal concepts, to combine them into thoughts, and to reason from one thought to another. Its essence is *having the form of a thing without being that kind of thing*: you can grasp what a triangle is without becoming triangular.

**Form.** The pattern or nature that makes a thing the kind of thing it is. It can exist concretely in individual things (this triangle) or abstractly in a mind (the concept of triangle).

**Formal cause and efficient cause.** A thing's formal cause is its nature, which grounds how it behaves. An efficient cause is what produces or sustains something.

**Hylemorphism.** Aristotle's view that a material substance is a composite of *substantial form* (what makes it the kind of thing it is) and *prime matter* (pure potential to take on form).

**Fallacy of accident.** Treating a feature that the cases you've observed happen to share as though it were essential to the kind. Example: concluding that no one over seven feet tall could ever be president because no president so far has been that tall.

## The Argument as a Story

### Part One: Change Is Real

Start with a cup of coffee on your desk. Leave it for a while and it gets cold. Outside the window a leaf falls. Rain keeps falling and a puddle grows. You swat a fly and it dies. Change is everywhere, in all four of its kinds.

Could the senses be fooling us? Suppose, as the philosopher René Descartes imagined, or as in the movie *The Matrix*, that your whole life has been a dream or a simulation. Change would still be undeniable, because your experiences change. You have one thought and then another. You wonder whether you're dreaming, dismiss the idea, and then find yourself worrying about it again. That sequence of experiences is itself change.

Could even that be an illusion? The ancient Greek philosopher Parmenides argued that change is impossible. His reasoning went like this. When hot coffee becomes cold, its coldness, which didn't exist before, comes into existence. But if the coldness didn't exist, it was nothing, and something can't come from nothing. So the coffee can't really become cold, and the same reasoning applies to any change whatsoever.

Feser gives two replies. The first is that nobody can coherently accept Parmenides' conclusion. To persuade yourself or anyone else that change is unreal, your mind must move through the argument step by step, from one premise to the next and on to the conclusion, and that movement is itself a change. Doubting that change occurs already assumes that it does.

### Part Two: Aristotle's Solution — Actuality and Potentiality

The second reply is Aristotle's, and it is the key to the whole chapter. Parmenides was wrong to think change requires something to come from nothing. While the coffee is hot, its coldness isn't *actual*, but it isn't simply *nothing* either. It is there *potentially*. The coffee has a real capacity to become cold, and that capacity is quite specific. The coffee can cool down, stain the floor, or make you more alert. It cannot power a gasoline engine, turn itself into chicken soup, or sprout feathers and start squawking. Because the coffee has some potentials and lacks others, its potential coldness is a genuine feature of it, somewhere between full actuality and sheer nothingness.

So change is the *actualization of a potential*. When the coffee cools, a potential it already had becomes actual. Nothing comes from nothing, because a potential was never nothing. With one distinction, Parmenides' puzzle dissolves and everyday experience is vindicated.

### Part Three: Every Change Needs a Changer

Every change has something that brings it about. The cool air in the room lowers the coffee's temperature, and a flick of your wrist brings the swatter down on the fly. But Feser insists that this principle is not merely a generalization from lots of examples. It follows from what change *is*.

Think about it. The coffee's coldness, while the coffee is still hot, is merely potential. If it were actual, the coffee would already be cold. And something merely potential can't *do* anything. Only what is actual can act. So the coffee's potential coldness cannot turn itself into actual coldness. Something that is already actual has to do it: the actual coolness of the surrounding air, or ice cubes you drop in. In general, **a potential can be actualized only by something already actual.** Feser calls this the **principle of causality**. Notice exactly what it says. It concerns things that go from potential to actual. It does not say that absolutely everything has a cause, and that difference will matter a great deal later.

### Part Four: Two Kinds of Causal Series

Often the thing causing a change is itself being changed. The cool air chills the coffee, but the air's coolness was once merely potential, until the air conditioner actualized it. Your wrist moves the swatter, but your wrist's motion was actualized by nerve signals. Feser is careful here. He does not say that every cause of change must itself be changing. That doesn't follow, and the chapter will argue it isn't true. The point is only that *if* a cause is itself changing, then its change needs a cause too.

So we get *series* of causes. The coffee was cooled by the air, the air by the air conditioner, and the air conditioner came on because you pressed the button. The fly was killed by the swatter, the swatter moved by your wrist, the wrist by your neurons, and the neurons fired because the buzzing annoyed you. These series stretch backward in time in what Feser calls a **linear** fashion.

Now comes Feser's big concession. Suppose this kind of series goes back *forever*, with no first moment, so that the material world has always existed and always been changing. Aristotle believed exactly that. Today many people think the Big Bang shows that the universe had a beginning, while some scientists speculate that our universe came from an earlier one, or branched off from another in an endless series. Feser thinks this speculation is doubtful, but he says it **doesn't matter**. Grant an infinite past, and the argument still goes through, because it rests on a different kind of series.

That second kind is the **hierarchical** series. The easiest way to understand it is to picture a single moment. Your coffee cup sits three feet above the floor. Why? Because the desk is holding it up, right now. What holds the desk up? The floor, right now. The floor is held up by the foundation and the foundation by the earth, all at the same moment. Or picture a lamp hanging from a chain attached to a fixture screwed into the ceiling, which is held up by the walls, which rest on the foundation.

These series involve actualizing potentials even though no time passes. The desk actualizes the cup's potential to be three feet up, and the floor actualizes the desk's potential to hold the cup there. But what makes the series *hierarchical* is not that everything happens at once. It is a special kind of dependence. The cup has no power of its own to stay three feet up. The desk has no power of its own to hold it there, because without the floor the desk would fall too. The floor holds the desk only because it is itself being held up. So in a sense it is really the earth doing all the holding, working *through* the floor, the walls, the desk and the chain as its instruments, the way a painter works through a brush. The brush doesn't paint the picture; the painter does.

Compare the linear series. Once you switch on the air conditioner, it keeps cooling the room even if you leave the house or drop dead, because its power, once set going, is its own. Even the cooled air keeps its power to chill the coffee for a while after the machine shuts off. In the hierarchical case, if the floor collapses, the desk and cup fall *immediately*. Each later member acts only as long as, and only because, the earlier members are acting on it. In a footnote Feser gives the traditional names: a linear series is ordered **per accidens** ("accidentally"), and a hierarchical series is ordered **per se** ("essentially").

### Part Five: What "First" Really Means

A hierarchical series must have a first member, but we have to be careful about what "first" means. It doesn't mean first in time, as if we had to trace back to the Big Bang. It means this: since the desk, the floor and the foundation have no power of their own to hold anything up, the series couldn't exist at all unless *something* had the power to hold them up, and the cup through them, *without itself needing to be held up*. If the intermediate members are instruments, something must be using them. If their power is borrowed, something must have that power unborrowed, "built in." A first cause in this sense is one whose causal power is **nonderivative**.

Nor does "first" depend on the series being finite. A paintbrush cannot move itself, and giving it an infinitely long handle wouldn't change that. If the brush moves, something outside it must have the built-in power to move it. An infinite stack of desks would be as powerless to hold up your cup as a single desk. So "first" means *primary*, having causal power inherently, as opposed to *secondary*, having it only on loan. It does not mean "the one that comes before the second, third and fourth."

Feser also hints that the earth isn't really first in the strict sense. The earth has potentials of its own. That hint pays off in the next step.

### Part Six: From Change to Existence

Here is the pivotal move of the whole argument. The coffee can cool, or be held up by the desk, only if it *exists*. So what makes it exist? Not in the sense of who brewed it. The question is what keeps it in existence *here and now*, at every moment that it exists.

To simplify, think about the water that makes up most of the coffee. The matter that makes up that water could also exist as separate quantities of hydrogen and oxygen gas. Right now, though, its potential to exist as *water* is the one that is actualized. Why?

Two tempting answers miss the question. "Some chemical process in the past combined the hydrogen and oxygen" tells us *how the water got here*. "Nothing has come along to split them apart" tells us *how the water might someday stop existing*. Neither tells us what keeps it existing at *this* instant.

"Chemical bonding" doesn't answer the question either; it just restates it. The atoms could be bonded in other ways, so why are they bonded in the water-making way right now? Pointing to the structure of atoms only pushes the question down a level. Why are the subatomic particles arranged in just this way, here and now, rather than some other way?

What we have is exactly like the cup, the desk and the floor, except that now what is being held up is the thing's very *existence* rather than its location. The coffee's existence depends, right now, on the water's existence. The water's existence depends, right now, on an actualized potential of its atoms, and the atoms' existence depends on an actualized potential of their subatomic particles. This is a hierarchical series of actualizers of existence, and so it must have a first member.

### Part Seven: The Unactualized Actualizer

What would the first member of this series have to be like? It would have to actualize the existence of other things *without its own existence needing to be actualized* by anything. That means it has no potential for existence waiting to be actualized. It simply *is* actual, always already actual. It doesn't just *have* actuality the way the things it sustains do; it *is* pure actuality. It isn't something that merely happens to lack a cause. It couldn't have needed a cause even in principle, because there is nothing in it that could be actualized. This is an uncaused cause, Aristotle's Unmoved Mover, or more precisely an **unactualized actualizer**.

Notice where the argument started. It didn't begin by asking where the universe as a whole came from. It began with a cup of coffee. But the conclusion has universal implications, because what is true of the water is true of every material thing: the leaf, the fly, you, the stars. Anything that could exist or not exist can exist at a given moment only if certain potentials are actualized at that moment, and ultimately only because an unactualized actualizer is keeping it in being.

A technical note: Feser's example talks as if water were just a collection of atoms. In Aristotle's fuller theory, called hylemorphism, water is a unified substance made of *substantial form* and *prime matter*, and the atoms exist in it only "virtually." Feser avoids that framing because it is controversial and the argument doesn't need it. But the argument can be restated in those terms. Prime matter depends on form to exist concretely, and the form depends on matter to be realized. So the form–matter combination would be an explanatory circle unless something outside it were actualizing it and keeping it in existence.

### Part Eight: From Pure Actuality to God

Why call this unactualized actualizer "God"? There are two reasons. First, it is the ultimate cause of things in the most intimate way possible: it is what keeps everything in existence from moment to moment. Whatever else God is supposed to be, God is the ultimate cause of things. Second, the traditional attributes of God follow from pure actuality. Feser gives a sketch here and a full treatment in a later chapter.

**Immutable (unchangeable).** Change is the actualization of potential, and pure actuality has no potential. So it cannot change.

**Eternal.** Anything existing in time is changeable, since it passes from moment to moment. An unchangeable being must therefore exist outside time altogether, without beginning, end or succession. It simply is.

**Immaterial and incorporeal.** Being material means being changeable and existing in time. So something unchangeable and timeless can't be material and can't have a body of any kind.

**Perfect.** Think about what makes something imperfect. A squirrel hit by a car can no longer run from predators as fast as it needs to, and a tree with damaged roots may be unstable or unable to take in enough water. Such a defect is a *privation*, the lack of something a thing's nature calls for, which amounts to a failure to realize a potential. Something purely actual has no unrealized potential, so it has no privation and is maximally perfect.

**Fully good.** In a general sense, a thing is good insofar as it realizes the potentials of its kind and bad insofar as it falls short. A good painter has developed his capacity for craftsmanship and composition, and a bad painter hasn't. Pure actuality falls short in no way, so it is fully good.

**One.** Could there be two purely actual beings? There could be two things of a kind only if something distinguished them, some feature one has and the other lacks. We usually tell things apart by material or temporal features, like size, position or age, but pure actuality is immaterial and eternal. More generally, things of a kind differ by some perfection one has and another lacks, as when one tree has sturdier roots or one squirrel is missing its tail. But pure actuality lacks nothing. So nothing could distinguish one purely actual being from another, and there can be only one. The same single cause sustains everything.

**Omnipotent.** Power just is the ability to actualize potential. Pure actuality is not one actual thing among many; it is the source of all the actualizing power that anything else has. So it has all power.

**Intelligent.** This needs two more ideas. The first is what intelligence is. Intelligence involves grasping universal concepts (like the concept *man*, which applies to every possible man), combining them into thoughts ("all men are mortal"), and reasoning ("Socrates is a man, so Socrates is mortal"). The most basic of these is grasping concepts, and to grasp a concept is to have in your mind the same form or pattern that exists in things, in a universal way, *without being that kind of thing*. A triangle drawn on paper is triangular. When you think about triangularity, you have that form in your mind without becoming a triangle.

The second idea is the **principle of proportionate causality**: whatever is in an effect must somehow be in its cause, because a cause can't give what it doesn't have. Feser's illustration is a gift of $20. I can give you $20 only if I have it in some way. I might have a $20 bill in my wallet, which is having it *formally*, the same way it will be in your hands. I might have no cash but $20 in the bank that I can write a check against, which is having it *virtually*, as the power to obtain it. Or, far-fetched as it sounds, Congress might pass a law letting me print my own twenties, which is having it *eminently*, in the higher form of a power to bring such things into existence at all. If I have it in none of these ways, I can't give it to you.

Now put the two ideas together. The purely actual cause is the cause of everything that exists or could exist. To cause a thing is always to cause a thing of a particular kind, a stone rather than a tree or a tree rather than a cat, which means a thing with a particular form. So the forms of all things must be in the cause somehow. They can't be there as they are in physical things, because the cause isn't a cat or a tree and couldn't be, being immaterial. And they must be there universally, since this cause is responsible for *every possible* cat and tree and stone. But having forms in a universal, abstract way is exactly what an intellect does. The cause also accounts for how things are related, not just for cats but for this cat's being on this mat, so these relations must exist in it the way thoughts exist in a mind. Things pre-exist in their cause much as the things we make pre-exist in our minds as plans.

**Omniscient.** Since there is nothing that exists or could exist that falls outside the range of this intellect's thoughts, it knows everything.

Put it all together and the cause that sustains the coffee in existence right now is one, unchanging, eternal, immaterial, incorporeal, perfect, fully good, all-powerful, intelligent and all-knowing. That, Feser says, is what it is for God to exist.

## The Formal Argument, Step by Step

Feser restates the argument as fifty numbered steps. Here they are in paraphrase, each with a one-line justification.

**Stage 1: from change to a purely actual actualizer**

1. Change really happens. *Justification: the senses show it, and even denying it requires a change of mind.*
2. Change is a potential becoming actual. *Aristotle's answer to Parmenides: what a thing becomes was already in it potentially.*
3. So potentials really are actualized in the world. *Follows from 1 and 2.*
4. A potential can be actualized only by something already actual (the principle of causality). *What is merely potential can't do anything, so it can't actualize itself.*
5. So every change is caused by something already actual. *Follows from 2 and 4.*
6. Every change is a change in some thing, a substance, that changes. *There's no cooling without something that cools.*
7. For that substance to exist at any moment, its potential for existence must be actualized at that very moment. *Its matter could be otherwise (water could be separate gases), so its being what it is now is an actualized potential.*
8. So at each moment the substance has something actualizing its existence. *Follows from 4 and 7.*
9. That actualizer is either itself being actualized at that moment, or it is purely actual. *These are the only two options.*
10. If it is being actualized, there is a chain of simultaneous actualizers that either goes on forever or ends in something purely actual. *Each further actualizer faces the same two options.*
11. Such a chain is a hierarchical series, and a hierarchical series can't go on forever. *Each member has only borrowed power, which needs an unborrowed source.*
12. So either the actualizer is purely actual or the chain ends in something purely actual. *Follows from 9–11.*
13. So any change, and the existence of any changing thing at any moment, requires a purely actual actualizer. *Follows from 6, 8 and 12.*
14. **So a purely actual actualizer exists.** *Change is real (step 3), so what it requires exists.*

**Stage 2: the divine attributes**

15. Two purely actual actualizers would need some feature that distinguishes them. *That is how things of a kind are told apart.*
16. Any such feature would mean one of them had an unrealized potential, which pure actuality lacks. *Differences involve something one has and the other lacks.*
17. So nothing could distinguish two of them. *Follows from 15 and 16.*
18. So there is only one. *Unity.*
19. To be capable of change, it would need potentials. *Change is the actualization of potential.*
20. Being purely actual, it has none. *By definition.*
21. So it is immutable. *Follows from 19 and 20.*
22. Anything in time is capable of change. *Temporal existence means passing from moment to moment.*
23. So it is eternal, outside time. *Follows from 21 and 22.*
24. Anything material would be changeable and in time. *Matter is always changeable.*
25. So it is immaterial. *Follows from 21, 23 and 24.*
26. Having a body would mean being material. *By definition.*
27. So it is incorporeal. *Follows from 25 and 26.*
28. Any imperfection would be an unrealized potential. *Imperfection is a privation.*
29. So it is perfect. *Follows from 20 and 28.*
30. Being less than fully good means lacking something proper to oneself, which is a privation. *Goodness is the realization of a thing's potentials.*
31. Pure actuality can have no privation. *It has no unrealized potential.*
32. So it is fully good. *Follows from 30 and 31.*
33. Power is the ability to actualize potentials. *That is what it means to make something happen.*
34. Every actualization comes from the purely actual actualizer, directly or through a series ending in it. *This is the result of Stage 1.*
35. So all power derives from it. *Follows from 33 and 34.*
36. Being the source of all power is being omnipotent. *By definition.*
37. So it is omnipotent. *Follows from 35 and 36.*
38. Whatever is in an effect is in its cause formally, virtually or eminently (proportionate causality). *A cause can't give what it lacks.*
39. The purely actual actualizer causes all things. *This is the result of Stage 1.*
40. So the forms of all things must somehow be in it. *Follows from 38 and 39.*
41. Forms exist either concretely, in individual things, or abstractly, in an intellect. *For example, a drawn triangle versus the concept of triangle.*
42. The forms can't be in it the way they are in individual physical things. *It's immaterial, and it causes every possible instance of each form.*
43. So they're in it abstractly, as in an intellect. *By elimination, from 41 and 42.*
44. So it has intellect. *Having forms abstractly is what intellect is.*
45. Since the forms of all things are in its thoughts, nothing lies outside them. *It causes everything that exists or could exist.*
46. For nothing to lie outside something's thoughts is for it to be omniscient. *By definition.*
47. So it is omniscient. *Follows from 45 and 46.*
48. So there is a purely actual cause of things that is one, immutable, eternal, immaterial, incorporeal, perfect, fully good, omnipotent, intelligent and omniscient. *This gathers the results above.*
49. For such a cause to exist is what it is for God to exist. *These are the defining attributes of God in classical theism.*
50. **So God exists.**

## Objections and Replies

Feser answers a long series of objections. Here is each one with his reply.

**"Even a first cause wouldn't be God."** A common complaint is that first-cause arguments can't tell us anything about the first cause. Stage 2 answers it: pure actuality entails unity, immutability, eternity, immateriality, perfection, goodness, omnipotence, intelligence and omniscience. Feser grants that these derivations need more defense, which he supplies in a later chapter, but the argument clearly goes far beyond a bare cause.

**"The universe might not have had a beginning."** This misreads the argument. Feser explicitly allows that the universe or a multiverse may be infinitely old. The argument is about what sustains things in existence now, not about what started them.

**"Just because each part of the universe has a cause doesn't mean the whole does."** No premise is about the universe as a whole. The argument starts from any single object, such as a stone or a cup of coffee. That the conclusion applies to everything is a consequence, not an assumption.

**"If everything has a cause, what caused God?"** The argument never says that everything has a cause. It says that whatever goes from potential to actual needs a cause. Things need causes because they have potentials, and what is purely actual has none, so there is nothing in it to be caused. Saying the two principles are the same is like saying "all triangles have three sides" means the same as "all shapes have three sides."

**"That formulation is just a dodge."** First, a person's motives don't make their claim false; judging the claim by the motive is the ad hominem fallacy. Second, the dodge charge is historically false, because from Aristotle through Aquinas to today the principle has always been stated this way. Third, the principle follows naturally from the analysis of change, and you don't need to believe in God to doubt that a mere potential could actualize itself.

**Hume: "We can imagine a thing popping into existence uncaused."** Imagining a stone suddenly appearing is imagining it *without also imagining* its cause, not imagining it *with no* cause. You can think of a three-sided closed figure without thinking "triangle," but no such figure fails to be a triangle. Also, as Elizabeth Anscombe pointed out, what makes the imagined scene a case of *coming into existence* rather than the stone being *moved* from elsewhere? Only the type of cause involved, so causation sneaks back in. Finally, experience overwhelmingly supports the principle. If it were false, as W. Norris Clarke noted, elephants or hotels could appear on your lawn out of nowhere, and they would be doing so all the time.

**Kant: "Causality only applies within experience."** We conclude that things need causes not because we experience them but because they are potential. That reason applies wherever there is potential, observable or not. We learn geometry from diagrams in black ink, but geometry applies to figures of any color, or none.

**Russell: "Physics has equations, not causes."** Feser gives four replies. First, the objection proves too much, since "law," "event" and "explanation" aren't in the equations either. As Jonathan Schaffer quips, it's like claiming mathematics has abolished the variable because the word "variable" never appears in its equations. Second, physics does describe particles as having dispositions, or tendencies to produce effects. Third, other sciences use causation, and naturalists themselves rely on causal theories of knowledge and perception. Fourth, and most important, physics isn't a complete description of reality. It captures only mathematical structure, as the later Russell himself admitted. Inferring otherwise is like concluding there's no wood in the ground because the metal detector didn't beep, or like a drunk deciding his keys must be under the streetlamp because that's the only place he can see.

**Newton's law of inertia.** Inertia describes motion over time, while the argument concerns what keeps things in existence at a moment. Laws of nature are shorthand for how things with certain natures behave, so they presuppose, and can't explain, the existence of such things. And following the historian James Weisheipl, mathematical physics doesn't prove inertia but assumes it as a baseline for its equations, abstracting away from causes entirely. So physics can neither confirm nor refute the principle of causality.

**Einstein's block universe.** Some read relativity as saying that past, present and future are equally real and change is an illusion. But science itself depends on changing experiences, such as making a prediction and then observing the result, so denying all change undercuts the evidence for the theory. At most change is only in the mind, and change in the mind is still actualization of potential. And even a changeless block universe would need something to actualize its existence as this universe rather than another.

**Quantum indeterminism, Bell correlations, and virtual particles.** Causation doesn't require determinism, only that the effect be intelligible, and even on the standard Copenhagen interpretation every transition has causal antecedents. Various causal accounts of Bell correlations and vacuum fluctuations have been proposed. The deeper point is that mathematical physics leaves causation out by method: the absence of something from a representation isn't a representation of its absence, just as a black-and-white sketch doesn't show the world is colorless. Quantum theory may even point toward Aristotle, since Heisenberg compared quantum probabilities to Aristotle's idea of *potentia*. Quantum theory pictures potential without an actualizer, and the block universe pictures actuality without potential. Both leave out causation because of how they represent things, not because causation is missing from reality.

**Radioactive decay.** A lead-210 atom has a certain probability of decaying because of what lead-210 is: its nature, or formal cause. Something also generated it, and something keeps it existing now. As the philosopher Phil Dowe notes, if I brought a bucket of lead-210 into your room and you got radiation sickness, I would be responsible, and I couldn't be responsible for what I didn't cause. Scientists even describe decay as the *production* of mercury.

**"Aristotle's science is outdated."** Some of Aristotle's scientific claims, such as the earth-centered cosmos, the four elements and natural places, were refuted. The metaphysical analysis of change as actualization of potential was not, and science itself presupposes it.

**Eddington's "two tables."** The claim that desks are really clouds of particles doesn't show the desk isn't holding the cup. It describes the mechanism by which it does. No one faults engineers or doctors for ignoring particle physics, and the examples were only meant to introduce concepts that apply at any level of description.

**"Causes aren't truly simultaneous with their effects."** Simultaneous doesn't mean instantaneous. A cause acting and its effect being produced are one event, however long it takes, just as pushing a chair across a room takes time, but the pushing and the being-pushed happen together. Immediate causes are always simultaneous with their effects: it's the billiard ball's impact, not its earlier roll, that moves the other ball. Relativity's point about simultaneity concerns distant, separate events, not this. In any case, what makes a series hierarchical is borrowed power, not timing.

**"Infinite series exist in mathematics."** Mathematical series involve no instrumental causes at all. Aristotelians accept infinite mathematical series and even beginningless linear series. What they deny is an infinite chain of borrowed power with no lender. Picture an endless row of mirrors reflecting a face: somewhere there has to be an actual face.

**"Calling causes 'instrumental' begs the question."** It doesn't. Calling something an instrument means only that its power is derived. Whether a chain of derived powers must end is argued separately. Even a skeptic can see that a stick can't push a stone by its own power.

**"Unmoved isn't unmovable."** Couldn't the first cause have some unused potentials? If the first actualizer of existence had a potential needing actualization, then either something else actualizes it, in which case it isn't first; or a purely actual part of it does, in which case that part is the real first cause; or a mixed part does, which starts a vicious regress. So a first actualizer must be purely actual.

**"A cause that doesn't change is impossible."** The argument claims to prove that such a cause exists, so the critic must find a false premise, not simply insist the conclusion is impossible. The objection also commits a fallacy of accident: every U.S. president so far has been under seven feet tall, but that doesn't mean a seven-footer could never be president. Our causes change as they act because they are limited, as an arm must move to move a stick. Pure actuality has no such limits, and "something that actualizes without being actualized" is no contradiction.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **"It's a Big Bang argument."** It isn't. It works even if the universe is infinitely old.
- **"It says everything has a cause."** It says that what passes from potential to actual has a cause.
- **"First cause means earliest cause."** It means the cause with underived power, the one the others depend on right now.
- **"An infinite chain removes the need for a first cause."** That is true of linear chains but not of hierarchical ones, just as an infinitely long paintbrush still can't move itself.
- **"Physics has disproved causation."** Physics leaves causation out of its descriptions by method. It hasn't found that causation is absent from reality.
- **"Indeterministic means uncaused."** Probabilistic causes are still causes.
- **"The first cause could be some impersonal force."** Any force or field has potentials and so can't be purely actual. Pure actuality has the divine attributes.

## How to Explain It to Others

**The simple version:** "Things change, and change is a potential becoming actual, which only something already actual can bring about. Forget the past and ask what keeps your coffee existing right now. It depends on its water, the water on its molecules, the molecules on atoms, and the atoms on particles, all at this moment. Each layer has its existence on loan from the one beneath it. A chain of borrowers needs a lender who borrowed nothing: something purely actual. And anything purely actual has to be one, unchanging, timeless, immaterial, perfect, all-powerful and all-knowing. That's God."

**The best analogy** is Feser's own: the cup held up by the desk, held up by the floor, held up by the foundation. Knock out the floor and the cup falls *now*, not in a billion years, which shows the series is about the present, not the past. Every member passes along support it receives, so adding more members, even infinitely many, never produces a source. Then make the key swap and replace "being held up" with "being kept in existence." A second helpful image is the picture on a movie screen, which exists only while the projector is shining. The question isn't what started the film but what is keeping the picture there *right now*.

**Tips:**
- Never open with "everything has a cause."
- Concede the infinite past up front, and draw a horizontal chain (linear) and a vertical stack (hierarchical) side by side.
- Move quickly from the cup example to the existence example, where timing doesn't matter.
- Derive a few attributes before you use the word "God."
- Invite the "What caused God?" question and answer it calmly. It's a good objection to a bad version of the argument.

## Key Takeaways

- Change is undeniable, and it is the actualization of potential.
- No potential can actualize itself, so whatever goes from potential to actual needs an already-actual actualizer.
- Linear causal series, stretched out in time, might have no beginning. Hierarchical series, made of borrowed power, must have a first member.
- "First" means underived, not earliest, and even an infinite hierarchical series would need such a cause.
- The existence of anything at any moment depends on a hierarchical series of actualizers that must end in pure actuality.
- Pure actuality must be one, immutable, eternal, immaterial, perfect, good, omnipotent, intelligent and omniscient: God.
- "What caused God?" attacks a principle the argument never uses.
- Physics' silence about causation reflects its mathematical method, not the absence of causation from nature.
- The first mover must be not just unmoved but unmovable, and the claim that every cause must change commits a fallacy of accident.

## Ten Review Questions (with Brief Answers)

1. **How does Aristotle answer Parmenides?** The coffee's coldness wasn't nothing; it existed potentially. Change is the actualization of a potential, not something coming from nothing.
2. **Why can't a potential actualize itself?** Because what is merely potential isn't actual, and only the actual can act.
3. **What's the difference between linear and hierarchical series?** In a linear series, members have their own power and can act after earlier ones are gone, like the air conditioner running after you leave. In a hierarchical series, members have only borrowed power and act only while being acted on, like the desk held up by the floor.
4. **Why must a hierarchical series have a first member?** Borrowed power needs a source that has it unborrowed. Adding more borrowers, even infinitely many, never supplies it.
5. **What does "first" mean in "first cause"?** It means having underived, built-in causal power. It doesn't mean earliest in time.
6. **Why does the argument shift from change to existence?** Because change requires an existing thing, and a thing's existence right now is itself an actualized potential that needs a present actualizer. This also makes the argument independent of timing and of any beginning of the universe.
7. **Why can there be only one purely actual being?** Distinguishing two would require one to lack something the other has, which would be an unrealized potential.
8. **How does proportionate causality help show God is intelligent?** The cause of all things must contain their forms. Since it isn't a physical thing, it holds them universally, as ideas, and having forms that way is what intellect is.
9. **What's wrong with "What caused God?"** The principle applies only to what goes from potential to actual. Pure actuality has no potential, so it neither needs nor could have a cause.
10. **Why don't quantum mechanics or inertia refute the principle of causality?** Physics represents only mathematical structure and leaves causation out by method. Absence from the representation isn't absence from reality, and causation doesn't require determinism.
