# The Augustinian Proof: Why Eternal Truths Point to an Eternal Mind

## A study guide to Chapter 3 of Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God*

**Orientation.** Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (Ignatius Press, 2017) defends five classical arguments for God, each inspired by a great thinker of the past: Aristotle, Plotinus, Augustine, Aquinas, and Leibniz. Feser is not interpreting their texts; he is building his own rigorous versions of the arguments they made famous. Chapter 3 (pages 87–116) presents the **Augustinian proof**, sometimes called the *argument from eternal truths*. It begins not with motion, or parts, or the existence of things, but with something we use every day without noticing: numbers, general concepts, true statements, and possibilities. The argument claims that when we think carefully about what these things are and how they exist, we are led to an infinite, eternal, necessarily existing divine intellect. This guide is part of a course on the whole book. It comes after the Aristotelian proof (Chapter 1), which argued from change to a purely actual Unmoved Mover, and the Neo-Platonic proof (Chapter 2), which argued from composition to an absolutely simple One. The Augustinian proof borrows one key result from Chapter 1, and its conclusions are developed further in Chapter 6, where Feser discusses God's omniscience.

---

## 1. Why this chapter matters

There are three reasons this chapter deserves special attention.

**First, it is the one non-causal proof in the book.** The other four proofs reason from some feature of concrete things (their change, their composition, the distinction between what they are and the fact that they exist, their need for explanation) back to a cause. The Augustinian proof does not say that "something must have caused the laws of math." It asks a different question: *what kind of reality do necessary truths have?* Feser points out later in the book that this is why the familiar objection "But what caused God?" is not even relevant to this argument.

**Second, it meets skeptics on their own ground.** Many people who doubt God have enormous confidence in mathematics, logic, and science. They believe that two plus two equaled four long before any human being existed, that the laws of physics are discovered rather than invented, and that logic is not a matter of opinion. The Augustinian proof starts right there. If those convictions are correct, the argument claims, they have implications that reach all the way to God.

**Third, it forces a clear stand on one of philosophy's oldest questions**, the "problem of universals," which is over two thousand three hundred years old. Working through this chapter teaches you what realism, nominalism, and conceptualism are, what Plato and Aristotle disagreed about, and why the answer matters far beyond the philosophy classroom.

---

## 2. Key concepts and vocabulary

**Abstract object.** Something we can think about and know truths about, but cannot see, touch, or physically interact with. Feser discusses four kinds: universals, propositions, numbers (and other mathematical objects), and possible worlds.

**Universal.** A general pattern that many individual things can share. Imagine a pool hall. Every cue, ball, and rack is an individual thing. But all the cues share the pattern of *being a pool cue*. The red balls share *redness* with stop signs, fire engines, and strawberries. The balls share *roundness* with basketballs, globes, and the moon. The triangular rack shares *triangularity* with pyramids, dinner bells, and dunce caps. Redness, roundness, and triangularity are universals.

**Abstraction.** The mental act of setting aside the individual features of things to focus on what they share. To think about triangularity as such, we ignore that one triangle is made of wood and another of stone, that one is green and another orange.

**Proposition.** What a sentence expresses: a claim that is either true or false. "Snow is white" (English) and "Schnee ist weiss" (German) are different sentences, but they express one and the same proposition. So do "John is a bachelor" and "John is an unmarried man." This is why an ancient Greek philosopher and a modern American president, speaking different languages twenty-five centuries apart, can think exactly the same thought.

**Number versus numeral.** A numeral is a mark or sound we use to refer to a number. The numerals "2" and "II" are different, but they name the same number, just as the name "Barack Obama" is not the man himself. Erasing every numeral ever written would not make two plus two stop being four.

**Possible world.** A complete way things could have been. In the actual world, Mars has two moons; it could have had three. Unicorns do not exist, but they could have. What is possible in this sense is limited by logic rather than by physics: there is no possible world where two plus two equals five or where there are round squares.

**Pure possibles.** Things that are possible but never actually exist, such as unicorns, centaurs, and mermaids.

**Necessary truth.** A truth that could not have been false, such as the truths of mathematics and logic.

**Realism.** The view that abstract objects are real, cannot be reduced to anything material, and are not mere inventions of the human mind.

**Nominalism.** The view that abstract objects are not real. There are only individual things and the general words (names) we apply to them. There is the word "red," but no such thing as redness.

**Conceptualism.** The view that abstract objects are real, but only as constructions of the human mind.

**Psychologism.** The view that the laws of logic and mathematics are merely psychological laws describing how human minds happen to work. The logician Gottlob Frege argued against it.

**Platonic realism.** The view, first formulated by Plato, that abstract objects exist in a "third realm" that is neither physical nor mental: a realm of eternal Forms outside space and time, which physical things imperfectly copy or "participate in."

**Aristotelian realism.** The view that universals really exist, but only *in* individual things (always bound up with their individual features), and exist *as abstract* only in minds that abstract them from things.

**Scholastic realism.** Aristotelian realism plus the thesis associated with Saint Augustine: abstract objects exist ultimately in an infinite, eternal, divine intellect.

**Pure actuality.** A central idea from Chapter 1. Something is purely actual if it has no unrealized potential at all. Chapter 1 argued that a purely actual being must be unique, all-powerful, perfectly good, unchanging, immaterial, and eternal.

**Conceptual omniscience.** The philosopher Robert Adams's term for knowing all essences, possibilities, and necessary truths.

**Divine simplicity.** The classical doctrine that God has no parts of any kind, so that his intellect, will, power, goodness, and nature are really one and the same.

## 3. The argument told as a story

### Act One: We are surrounded by things we cannot touch

Begin in the pool hall. You are handling particular objects. But whenever you describe them, you use general patterns: "billiard ball," "red," "round," "triangular." These patterns are not themselves any of the objects on the table. You can pick up the rack, but you cannot pick up triangularity. Yet you know things about triangularity for certain: anything triangular has three sides, and the angles of a Euclidean triangle add up to two right angles.

The same is true of propositions. Write "Snow is white" on a whiteboard. You can see the sentence; you cannot see the proposition, because the German sentence "Schnee ist weiss" expresses the very same proposition while looking completely different. Now imagine erasing every copy of that sentence in every language. It would still be true that snow is white.

The same is true of numbers: destroy every "2" and every "II," and arithmetic is untouched. And the same is true of possibilities: we can reason carefully about what would have happened if a different candidate had won an election, or if Mars had three moons, but we can never travel to those possibilities or observe them.

So, in *some* sense, there are universals, propositions, numbers, and possible worlds. The question is: *in what sense?* Are they merely human inventions? Useful fictions? Could they be physical things of some exotic kind, such as patterns in our brains? Feser argues that none of these answers works.

### Act Two: The case for realism

Feser offers six direct arguments that abstract objects are real and independent of both matter and human minds.

1. **One over many.** Triangularity is not the same as any particular triangle, or even all of them together. Every triangle in existence could be destroyed and triangularity could be instantiated again in a new one. And triangles exist whether or not anyone notices them.

2. **Geometry.** Geometry studies perfect lines, perfect angles, and perfect circles, and it discovers objective, necessary truths about them. We discovered the angle sum of a triangle rather than inventing it; we could not change it if we tried; it was true before any human existed. And nothing in the physical world has geometrical perfection.

3. **Mathematics in general.** Mathematical truths are necessary and unchangeable, while the physical world and human minds are contingent and changing. Two plus two was four before anyone realized it and would remain four if everyone forgot it or the universe disappeared. Also, the series of numbers is infinite, but there are only finitely many physical things and finitely many ideas in all human minds combined.

4. **Propositions.** Some propositions are necessarily true and would stay true with no world and no minds. Even some contingent truths would survive: that Julius Caesar was assassinated on the Ides of March would remain true if everything physical and every human mind vanished tomorrow. And if nothing physical and no human mind had ever existed, the proposition "there is no physical world and no human mind" would have been true.

5. **Science.** Scientific laws and classifications are general, so they refer to universals. Science is in the business of discovering objective facts, and it is written in mathematics. To accept science is therefore to accept mind-independent universals and mathematical objects.

6. **Possible worlds.** Much of what is possible has nothing to do with the actual world or with us. There are possible worlds with radically different physical laws, including worlds where humans could not exist. And before the universe and human minds existed, it was already possible for them to exist.

### Act Three: The rivals refute themselves

Feser then argues that the alternatives to realism collapse.

**Against nominalism.** The nominalist says there is no redness, only the word "red." But why do we apply "red" to exactly these things? The obvious answer, "because they share redness," brings universals right back. The nominalist might say instead that red things simply resemble one another. But surely they resemble each other *in their redness*. And there are two deeper problems:

- **The vicious regress (from Bertrand Russell).** Resemblance itself is a universal. A stop sign resembles a fire truck; grass resembles the Incredible Hulk's skin. These are many instances of one thing: resemblance. If the nominalist says the resemblances merely resemble each other, he has introduced a higher-level resemblance, which is another universal. And so on forever.

- **Words are universals too.** The word "red" is itself a universal. When you say "red," and I say "red," and Socrates said "red," those are three instances (tokens) of one word (a type). That is exactly the "one thing shared by many" that nominalism denies. If the nominalist says our utterances are really different words that merely resemble each other, then no one ever uses the same word twice, communication becomes impossible, and the nominalist cannot even explain his view to us.

**Against conceptualism.** The conceptualist says universals exist, but only in human minds. But when you and I think of redness, or of the Pythagorean theorem, we are thinking of *the same* concept and *the same* truth, not two private versions. We share these with people long dead and with people not yet born. If humanity died out and new intelligent beings arose, they could grasp the very same truths. And communication proves the point: if every concept were a private construction, your idea of snow and mine would never match, and we could never mean the same thing by anything we said.

**Against psychologism.** Some people go further and say that logic and mathematics merely describe how our minds are wired, and that our wiring was shaped by evolution, culture, and history. Feser argues that this view undermines itself. To explain how evolution or culture shaped our minds, you must talk about selection pressures, genetic mutations, social trends, and class interests (all universals), rely on scientific and mathematical laws, and argue logically. But those are the very things the view says have no objective validity.

A milder version, inspired by Kant, says our minds *necessarily* think this way, and nothing could change it. But this version faces a dilemma. If our concepts and logic tell us nothing about objective reality, the Kantian cannot defend his own theory. If they *do* tell him the objective truth about how our minds work, then he has claimed exactly the kind of objective knowledge his theory said was impossible.

So some form of realism must be true.

### Act Four: Plato's third realm fails

There are three versions of realism: Platonic, Aristotelian, and Scholastic.

**Platonic realism** says abstract objects exist in a third realm, neither physical nor mental. Individual triangles are triangular because they imperfectly copy the eternal Form of Triangle. Feser raises four problems.

- **The Forms can't do anything.** A wooden rack pushes billiard balls into a triangle. A dinner bell makes a sound. An architect's *idea* of triangularity shapes the pyramid he designs. In every case where triangularity actually does something, it is either in a physical thing or in a mind. But triangularity sitting alone in a third realm would have no causal powers. Then how could it affect us so that we know about it? And how could it explain why physical triangles have the shape they do?

- **The Third Man.** A Form seems to be both a universal and an individual thing. But if the Form of Man is an individual, then it and individual men must share some further Form, a "Super-Form of Man," and then a Super-Super-Form, without end. Plato himself raised this objection, and people have argued about its force ever since.

- **The animality problem**, which Feser regards as more telling. Every animal is either rational (like humans) or non-rational (like dogs). Animality itself must cover both, so it cannot include rationality or non-rationality, which contradict each other. In itself, animality is neither. But nothing that exists as an individual thing can be neither rational nor non-rational. So animality cannot exist as an individual thing, which is what a Platonic Form would have to be.

- **Things lose their natures.** If what makes a tree a tree is found in the Form of Tree rather than in the tree, then the oak in your yard is no more truly a tree than a statue or mirror image of a tree. That is absurd.

### Act Five: Aristotle's alternative, and where it runs out

**Aristotelian realism** says universals exist really, but *in* things. Animality exists in Socrates, bound up with his rationality and humanity; it exists in Fido, bound up with his non-rationality and "dogness." Animality *considered by itself* exists only in a mind, which abstracts it from the individuals it perceives. This is not nominalism, because universals are real. It is not conceptualism, because the mind draws universals out of things rather than inventing them.

The central Aristotelian thesis is that abstraction is something only a mind does. So universals exist independently of minds, but not *as* abstract; and they exist as abstract, but not independently of minds. There is no third option, being abstract and mind-independent at the same time. Feser cites the philosopher David Oderberg's way of making the point: we encounter squareness constantly, in square things, but we never encounter squareness in the abstract, because squareness in the abstract is precisely what we abstract from those things.

Aristotelians can also explain many facts about possibility and necessity by pointing to the natures of real things. Human nature makes it possible for an ordinary man to hold his breath for ten seconds but impossible for ten hours. Two men plus two more men make four men, never twenty.

But some cases defeat an Aristotelian realism that appeals only to things and human (or other finite) minds.

- **Possibility with no world.** It is possible that no physical world and no human minds had ever existed. Even in that case, it would still have been possible for a world and minds to come into existence. What would ground that possibility? Not physical things, and not human minds; there would be none.

- **Pure possibles.** Unicorns, centaurs, and mermaids have never existed, yet they are possible. Their possibility cannot rest on real unicorns, and it cannot rest on human imagination, since they were possible before anyone imagined them.

- **Truths without minds.** The proposition that no world or minds exist would be true if everything vanished. Truths about history, like Caesar's assassination, would remain true.

- **Necessary truths.** The truths of mathematics and logic would hold whether or not any world or human mind ever existed.

### Act Six: Augustine's answer

This brings us to **Scholastic realism**. It is Aristotelian in spirit: universals exist only in things or in intellects, and there is no mindless third realm. But it grants Plato one point: there must be *some* reality beyond the physical world and beyond all human and other finite minds. Following Augustine, it identifies this reality as an **infinite, eternal, divine intellect**, in which all universals, propositions, mathematical and logical truths, and possibilities exist as ideas. If realism is true, and the Platonic and Aristotelian versions fail, then Scholastic realism is true, and there is a divine intellect. This is the core of the argument from eternal truths, defended by Augustine and later by Leibniz.

### Act Seven (Stage 2): From a necessary mind to God

Stage 2 makes the reasoning tighter and follows it all the way to God.

**Why a mind, and why not ours?** Abstract objects cannot depend on the physical world: physical things are always particular, while universals are general; some truths are necessary, while the world is contingent. So abstract objects exist either in intellects or in a third realm, and the third realm has failed. They exist in intellects. But not ultimately in human intellects, which come and go. Once there were none; someday there may be none again. The same reasoning rules out any finite minds, including embodied aliens and even angels. Angels never die, but they still exist only because God creates them, so they exist contingently. The only intellect that could ground necessary truths is one that exists **with absolute necessity**, one that could not possibly have failed to exist.

**Why only one mind?** Could there be several necessary minds, each holding some truths? No, because abstract objects form an **interlocking system**, a point Leibniz emphasized. To understand humanity (if a human is a rational animal), you must understand animality and rationality, and then whatever defines them, and so on through a whole network. Propositions are built out of universals. And every proposition is logically related to every other one. "All men are mortal" and "Socrates is a man" together imply "Socrates is mortal." "All men are mortal" and "cats have four legs" do *not* imply "It is raining in Cleveland." Any two propositions are either consistent or inconsistent, and they can be combined into compound propositions according to the truth tables of logic. These relationships would hold whether or not any world or finite mind existed. So a necessary intellect must grasp *all* of them. And the system is infinite: for every number, there is a true proposition and a false one about whether it is odd or even, and each of those is logically related to every other proposition. So this one intellect is **conceptually omniscient**: it understands all essences, possibilities, and necessary truths.

**Why this mind is God.** A critic might say: perhaps there are still several such minds; perhaps this mind knows necessary truths but not everyday facts; perhaps it has no power or goodness. Feser answers with Chapter 1's idea of pure actuality. An intellect that exists necessarily must be purely actual. If its existence depended on some potential being actualized, it would exist only on that condition, and so would be contingent. And Chapter 1 showed that anything purely actual must be unique (there is nothing that could distinguish two of them), all-powerful, perfectly good, unchanging, immaterial, and eternal. It must also know *all* truths, including contingent ones, because not knowing something would be an unrealized potential to know it. (Feser adds a second point: what is actual is a subset of what is possible, so a mind that knows all possible truths already knows the actual ones.) Therefore: there is exactly one necessarily existing intellect, which is purely actual, all-knowing, all-powerful, perfectly good, unchanging, immaterial, and eternal. That is God.

### An ally: Greg Welty's six conditions

Feser notes that the philosopher Greg Welty has defended a nearly identical view, which he calls "Theistic Conceptual Realism." Welty proposes six tests for any theory of abstract objects:

1. **Objectivity**: they are real independently of human minds.
2. **Necessity**: they exist necessarily.
3. **Intentionality**: they *represent* things, just as thoughts do. Triangularity represents triangles; a proposition represents a state of affairs; a possible world represents a way things might have been.
4. **Relevance**: the theory should explain why the necessary and possible truths are as they are.
5. **Plenitude**: there must be enough abstract objects to do all the work required.
6. **Simplicity**: the theory should not multiply kinds of things unnecessarily.

Objectivity rules out nominalism and conceptualism. Intentionality favors minds over a third realm, because representing is what minds do. Simplicity favors two realms (matter and minds) over three. Necessity and plenitude rule out finite minds alone, because they are contingent and have never entertained most truths. And relevance favors the divine mind. Welty's example: a drawing of Socrates hammering nails represents the possibility that Socrates might have been a carpenter. But nobody thinks that possibility depends on the drawing existing. A Platonic "possible world" floating in a third realm is no better. But if the possibility is an idea in the mind of an all-knowing, all-powerful being who *could have created* such a world, then the idea is genuinely connected to what can happen.

## 4. The formal argument, step by step (paraphrased)

1. There are only three basic accounts of abstract objects: realism, nominalism, and conceptualism. *These exhaust the options: not real, real but human-made, or real and not human-made.*
2. Realism is supported by decisive arguments. *The six direct arguments: one over many, geometry, mathematics, propositions, science, possible worlds.*
3. Nominalism faces insuperable objections. *Resemblance regress; words are universals too.*
4. Conceptualism faces insuperable objections. *Shared concepts, communication, and the self-refutation of psychologism.*
5. So some version of realism is true. *From 1 through 4.*
6. Realism has three versions: Platonic, Aristotelian, and Scholastic. *A third realm, only finite minds, or also a necessary mind.*
7. On Platonic realism, abstract objects exist in a third realm apart from matter and all minds. *Definition.*
8. On Aristotelian realism, abstract objects exist only in human or other contingent minds. *Definition (Aristotelian realism without God).*
9. On Scholastic realism, abstract objects exist in contingent minds and also in at least one necessary mind. *Definition.*
10. The third-realm view faces insuperable objections. *Inertness, Third Man, animality, natures exiled from things.*
11. So Platonic realism is false. *From 7 and 10.*
12. The contingent-minds-only view faces insuperable objections. *Pure possibles, possibility without a world, truths without minds, necessary truths.*
13. So Aristotelian realism is false. *From 8 and 12.*
14. So Scholastic realism is true. *From 5, 6, 11, and 13.*
15. So abstract objects exist in at least one necessarily existing intellect. *From 9 and 14.*
16. Abstract objects are logically related so as to form an interlocking system. *Universals define one another; propositions are built of universals and logically related.*
17. The reasons that put some abstract objects in a necessary intellect put the whole system there. *The logical relations too hold independently of any world or finite mind.*
18. So the whole interlocking system exists in at least one necessary intellect. *From 15 through 17.*
19. A necessarily existing intellect would be purely actual. *Otherwise its existence would depend on some potential being actualized.*
20. There cannot be more than one purely actual being. *From Chapter 1: nothing could distinguish two of them.*
21. So there cannot be more than one necessarily existing intellect. *From 19 and 20.*
22. An intellect containing the whole system would be conceptually omniscient. *It grasps all essences, possibilities, and necessary truths.*
23. So the one necessary intellect is conceptually omniscient. *From 18, 21, and 22.*
24. If it did not also know every contingent truth, it would have an unrealized potential and not be purely actual. *Ignorance is an unactualized potential to know.*
25. So it is omniscient in the full sense. *From 19 and 24.*
26. Whatever is purely actual is also all-powerful, fully good, unchanging, immaterial, bodiless, and eternal. *From Chapter 1.*
27. So there is exactly one necessary intellect that is purely actual, omniscient, omnipotent, perfectly good, immutable, immaterial, incorporeal, and eternal. *Combining the previous results.*
28. For such a being to exist is what it is for God to exist. *These are the defining attributes of God in classical theism.*
29. So God exists. *From 27 and 28.*

## 5. Every objection discussed, with replies

### Objection 1: "Ideas in God's mind would be concrete, not abstract."

Some philosophers, including Keith Yandell and William Lane Craig, argue that if universals and propositions are God's ideas, then they are really concrete mental items, not abstract objects at all.

**Reply.** First, following Welty, Feser notes that contemporary philosophers have no agreed definition of "abstract object," so the objection cannot lean on a settled consensus. Second, the objection begs the question. For Aristotelians and Scholastics, an abstract object just *is* what an intellect produces when it abstracts from particular things. The critic says abstract objects can't be in a mind; the Scholastic says they can *only* be in a mind. Simply asserting the critic's definition doesn't prove the Scholastic wrong. Feser also notes a deeper difference: modern philosophers usually picture thinking as having a "mental state," a concrete event in the brain or soul, and they define "abstract" as "outside space and time." Aristotelians picture thinking as taking on the form of a thing without its matter, and they define "abstract" by reference to the act of abstraction, so that the very same nature, such as humanness, can exist concretely in things and abstractly in a mind.

### Objection 2: Leftow's water objection

Brian Leftow criticizes "deity theories," which ground necessary truths in God's nature. (Scholastic realism is one, because in a simple God, his intellect and his nature are the same thing.) Suppose it is a necessary truth that water is H2O. On a deity theory, this follows from God's nature. But then, Leftow argues, if water were not necessarily H2O, God would not exist. So God's existence seems to depend on facts about water, which is strange.

**Reply.** Feser offers a parallel. Because two plus two necessarily equals four, it is necessary that two rocks plus two rocks make four rocks. By Leftow's reasoning, if that weren't necessary, two plus two wouldn't necessarily be four, so arithmetic "depends" on rocks. Obviously it doesn't. A necessary truth has necessary consequences about everything, but logical implication is not dependence. Rock science is never going to overturn arithmetic, and water science is never going to overturn God. Leftow doesn't claim it could, but then it's unclear what the troubling "dependence" is supposed to be. Further, the "if" clause describes an impossible scenario, just as "suppose two plus two did not necessarily equal four" does; there is nothing there to consider. Finally, Leftow holds that such truths are necessary because God *wills* them (a view called voluntarism), so God could have made them otherwise. But deity theorists reject voluntarism, so assuming it simply begs the question.

### Objection 3: Grim's argument from Cantor's theorem

Patrick Grim argues that omniscience would require knowing the *set* of all truths, but there can be no such set. By Cantor's theorem, the set of all subsets of any set (its "power set") is always larger than the set itself, and each subset generates a further truth, so no set can contain all truths. Hence, Grim says, omniscience is impossible.

**Replies.** Feser reports three. (1) Alvin Plantinga: the argument proves too much. It would equally show there is no set of all propositions, yet we can obviously say true things about all propositions, such as "every proposition is either true or false." So why can't we say an omniscient being knows all true ones? (2) Keith Simmons: omniscience doesn't need a set. Even if the truths don't form a set, it doesn't follow that there is any particular truth God fails to know, and that is all omniscience requires. (3) William Wainwright: Grim assumes God's knowledge is a collection of separate ideas forming a set. But given divine simplicity, what we describe as God's many separate pieces of knowledge are, in God, one single reality described in different ways.

### Objection 4: Coffey's charge of circularity

Surprisingly, some critics are Scholastic realists themselves. Peter Coffey agreed that eternal truths exist in God's mind but denied that this could be used to *prove* God. He objected that the argument assumes possibilities are eternally and necessarily possible, which we could know only if we already knew there was a divine mind. He also suggested that the idea of an order of truths independent of our minds is a Platonic illusion.

**Reply.** First, even an atheist can agree that possibilities are eternal and necessary; the atheist will simply interpret them in Platonic terms. So accepting the premise does not require already believing in God. Second, Coffey himself believes there *is* such an order, in God, so it can't be wholly an illusion; only the Platonic interpretation could be. Third, Coffey's implied dilemma (either you mean a Platonic realm, which is false, or you mean God's mind, which assumes the conclusion) is a false choice. You can affirm that there is an order of abstract truths existing in *some* way independent of all finite minds while leaving open *which* way. That is exactly the common ground Platonists and Scholastics share before they debate. So the Augustinian argument neither assumes anything false nor begs the question.

### Objections Feser answers within the argument

- **"Why not a committee of necessary minds?"** Because the truths form one interlocking system that must be grasped as a whole, and because a necessary mind is purely actual, and there can be only one purely actual being.
- **"This is only a cosmic mathematician, not God."** A necessary mind must be purely actual, and pure actuality brings every divine attribute, including full knowledge of contingent facts.

### Common modern objections the chapter equips you to answer

- **"Numbers just exist on their own, no mind needed" (modern Platonism).** Answer with inertness, the animality problem, and above all intentionality: propositions are *about* things, and aboutness is at home in minds. Ask for one example of something that is intrinsically about something else without getting its meaning from a mind.
- **"Math is a useful fiction or a game of symbols."** Fictions are invented and could have been written otherwise; mathematical truths are discovered, necessary, and were true before any storyteller. And if the mathematics in physics is fiction, so is the physics.
- **"Evolution explains our logic."** Telling that story requires objective logic, objective mathematics, and objective universals like "selection pressure." If evolution produced *reliable* reasoning, reliable means tracking something objective, and we are back to asking what that is.
- **"Possibility is just logical consistency; it needs no ground."** Consistency is a relation among propositions, which are exactly the abstract objects in question. Real, necessary truths that exist in no mind are Platonism under another name, and they inherit its problems.

## 6. Common misunderstandings

- **"It's another first-cause argument."** No. It does not argue that something caused the truths of math; it argues that such truths can exist only as thoughts in a necessary mind.
- **"It's a Platonist argument."** No. It explicitly rejects Plato's mindless third realm.
- **"It says God made up logic and could have made two plus two equal five."** No. That is voluntarism, which Scholastic realists reject. Necessary truths are grounded in God's intellect and nature, not in arbitrary choices.
- **"Aristotle's view is just conceptualism."** No. For Aristotle, universals really exist *in things*; the mind discovers them by abstraction rather than inventing them.
- **"You have to believe in God to know math."** No. The argument is about what grounds the existence of truths, not about how we learn them. Atheists know math perfectly well.
- **"Possible worlds are other universes."** No. They are ways things could have been, not places.
- **"God's mind is a library of infinitely many separate files."** No. Given divine simplicity, God's knowledge is one single act described in many ways.
- **"All of humanity together could ground eternal truths."** No. Any collection of minds that began to exist, or could have failed to exist, cannot ground truths that held before and without them.

## 7. How to explain it to others

**The simple version.** Two plus two equaled four before any human existed, and it would still equal four if every human vanished. So it is not something we invented, and it's not ink or brain chemistry. Truths are the kind of thing that are *thought*: they are *about* things, the way thoughts are. A truth that no mind anywhere has ever thought is very hard to make sense of. So eternal, necessary truths must exist in an eternal, necessary mind. And a mind that exists necessarily can have no unfulfilled potential, which means it knows everything, can do anything, is perfectly good, and is one. That is God.

**The best analogy: the architect's blueprints.** Before a building exists, where is it? In the architect's mind, as a plan. The plan is *about* the building. It is real before a single brick is laid. And it matters for what can be built, because it belongs to someone who has the power to build it. The Augustinian proof says that all the eternal truths and possibilities are like the plans in the mind of an architect who cannot fail to exist, never began, never forgets, and could bring any possibility into being. The analogy captures three things at once: truths need a mind (because they represent), necessary truths need a necessary mind, and possibilities are real possibilities because the mind that holds them has power. Its limit is that a human architect's plans are many, learned, and changeable, while God's ideas are one simple, unchanging act of understanding.

**A second image: the crossword.** Try dividing a finished crossword among several people, each keeping only some words. It can't be done, because every word crosses other words. The realm of truth is like that, infinitely so. That is why one mind, not a committee, must hold it.

**Tips for presenting.** Start by separating sentences from propositions and numerals from numbers with the "erase everything" thought experiment. Say clearly that this is not a causal argument. Expect many skeptics to become Platonists once you refute nominalism, and be ready for that turn. And always carry the argument through Stage 2 to pure actuality, so no one can say "that's just a cosmic calculator."

## 8. Key takeaways

- Universals, propositions, numbers, and possible worlds are abstract objects: real objects of thought that are not physical.
- Realism is supported by six direct arguments, and its rivals refute themselves: nominalism through the resemblance regress and the fact that words are universals, conceptualism through shared concepts, communication, and the self-defeat of psychologism.
- Plato's mindless third realm fails: its Forms are inert, generate the Third Man regress, cannot be neither-rational-nor-nonrational individuals, and would empty things of their natures.
- Nothing abstract exists without abstraction, and abstraction is the work of a mind.
- Finite minds (human, alien, angelic) are contingent and cannot ground pure possibles, possibilities without a world, truths without minds, or necessary truths.
- The ground must be one necessarily existing intellect that grasps the whole infinite, interlocking system of truths.
- A necessary intellect is purely actual, so it is unique, fully omniscient, omnipotent, perfectly good, immutable, immaterial, and eternal. That is God.
- The main objections (divine ideas are concrete; Leftow's water; Grim's Cantor; Coffey's circularity) all fail.
- This is the only non-causal proof in the book, and it links directly to Chapter 1 (pure actuality) and Chapter 6 (omniscience).

## 9. Ten review questions with brief answers

1. **What are the four kinds of abstract object Feser discusses?** Universals, propositions, numbers and other mathematical objects, and possible worlds.

2. **Why isn't a proposition the same as a sentence?** Different sentences in different languages can express one proposition, and erasing every copy of a sentence would not make the proposition false.

3. **What is Russell's regress argument against nominalism?** Resemblance is itself a universal; explaining it by further resemblances creates higher-level universals forever.

4. **Why does conceptualism make communication impossible?** If concepts were private constructions, no two people would ever mean the same thing by any word.

5. **What is the animality problem for Platonism?** Animality must be neither rational nor non-rational in itself, but no individual thing can be neither, so animality cannot exist as an individual Form.

6. **What is the key thesis of Aristotelian realism?** Universals exist in things but not as abstract; they exist as abstract only in minds. Nothing is both abstract and mind-independent.

7. **Name two cases Aristotelian realism (finite minds only) cannot handle.** Pure possibles like unicorns; the possibility of a world when none exists (also: truths without minds, necessary truths).

8. **Why can't angels ground necessary truths?** Although they don't die, they exist only because God creates them, so they are contingent.

9. **How does Feser get from a necessary mind to full omniscience?** A necessary mind is purely actual; not knowing a truth would be an unrealized potential; so it knows all truths.

10. **How does Feser answer Grim's Cantorian objection?** It proves too much (Plantinga); omniscience needs no set, only that no truth is unknown (Simmons); and divine simplicity means God's knowledge isn't a set of separate items (Wainwright).
