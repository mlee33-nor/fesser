# Five Roads, One Summit: A Capstone Synthesis of Edward Feser’s *Five Proofs of the Existence of God*

## 1. Orientation

This document is the capstone of a self-study course on Edward Feser’s book *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (Ignatius Press, 2017). The book has seven chapters and an introduction. Chapters 1 through 5 each defend one argument for God’s existence: the Aristotelian proof, the Neo-Platonic proof, the Augustinian proof, the Thomistic proof and the rationalist proof. Chapter 6 examines what God must be like and how he relates to the world. Chapter 7 answers the most common objections to arguments of this kind. Earlier modules of the course took each chapter apart in detail. This capstone puts the whole book back together. It explains the method all five proofs share, compresses each proof into a form you can carry in your head, shows why all five arrive at one and the same God, lists what the proofs do and do not claim, surveys how chapters 6 and 7 complete the case, and gives a twenty-item handbook of objections with replies. It ends with advice on explaining the case to different kinds of listeners, key takeaways and review questions.

## 2. Why this synthesis matters

It is possible to understand each of Feser’s five arguments separately and still misunderstand the book. The book is not five clever puzzles; it is one cumulative case. Three ideas bind it together.

First, **all five proofs have the same shape.** Each begins from something hard to deny, reasons to the existence of something fitting a precise description, and then shows that anything fitting that description must have the traditional attributes of God. That shape answers the single most common complaint about arguments for a “first cause” — that they might get you to some cause or other, but never to God.

Second, **the four causal proofs are about dependence here and now, not about the beginning of time.** They grant, for the sake of argument, that the universe might have existed forever. Their question is what keeps things in existence at this very moment. Almost every popular objection — “maybe the universe had no beginning,” “what caused God?”, “science will fill the gap” — misses this.

Third, **the five conclusions pick out one being.** Something that is purely actual must also be simple, must be existence itself, must exist necessarily, and there can be only one such thing. So the five proofs are five paths to the same summit.

If you can explain those three ideas, state each proof accurately, and answer the objections you are likely to meet without distorting Feser’s view or overstating it, you have mastered the book.

## 3. Key concepts and vocabulary

**Natural theology.** The study of what human reason, without appeal to divine revelation, can know about God’s existence and nature. The five proofs and the account of God’s attributes are natural theology; doctrines like the Trinity are not.

**Classical theism.** The mainstream conception of God in Western philosophy and religion, held by pagan, Jewish, Muslim and Christian thinkers alike: God as absolutely simple, unchanging, eternal, purely actual, and being itself rather than a being among beings.

**Actuality and potentiality.** Actuality is how a thing really is; potentiality is a real capacity to be otherwise. A hot cup of coffee is actually hot and potentially cold. It is not potentially chicken soup. Change is the actualization of a potential.

**The principle of causality.** A potential that becomes actual must be made actual by something already actual. The coffee cannot cool itself; the cool air does it. Notice what it does not say: it does not say that everything has a cause.

**Linear and hierarchical causal series.** A linear series runs through time and each member acts on its own once it exists: your grandfather fathered your father, who fathered you, and your birth did not require your grandfather still to be alive. A hierarchical series operates here and now, and every member except the first acts only with borrowed power: a cup is held up by a desk only because the floor holds up the desk, and the floor only because the foundation holds up the floor. A hand moves a stick that moves a stone.

**First cause.** In a hierarchical series, the first cause is first because its power is underived, not because it came earliest. An infinitely long paintbrush handle still cannot move itself.

**The principle of proportionate causality.** Whatever is in an effect must be in its total cause in some way: formally (I hand you a $20 bill from my wallet), virtually (I transfer $20 from my bank account), or eminently (I have the power to have genuine $20 bills printed).

**Agere sequitur esse.** Latin for “action follows being.” What a thing does reflects what it is. Stones do not grow because they are not alive.

**The principle of sufficient reason.** Everything is intelligible: whatever exists has an explanation, either in its own nature or in something else. Every cause is an explanation, but not every explanation is a cause, so something can be self-explanatory without being self-caused.

**Composition and simplicity.** A composite thing has parts that must be put together. Something absolutely simple has no parts of any kind — no physical parts, no form and matter, no essence distinct from existence.

**Abstract objects.** Universals such as redness or triangularity, propositions such as the one expressed by both “Snow is white” and “Schnee ist weiss,” numbers, and possible worlds.

**Realism, nominalism and conceptualism.** Realism says abstract objects are real and not merely human inventions. Nominalism says there are only general words. Conceptualism says universals are constructions of the human mind. Realism comes in three versions: Platonic (abstract objects exist in a third realm outside all minds and matter), Aristotelian (universals exist in things and, as abstract, only in minds), and Scholastic (Aristotelian realism plus a divine intellect).

**Essence and existence.** A thing’s essence is what it is; its existence is the fact that it is. You can know what a unicorn is without knowing whether any exist.

**Subsistent existence itself.** A being whose essence just is existence, which therefore does not receive existence from anything.

**Contingent and necessary being.** A contingent thing depends on something else and could fail to exist. A necessary being, in the strict sense, exists by its own nature and depends on nothing.

**Analogical language.** Using a word in related but not identical senses while still speaking literally, as “good” applies differently to good wine and a good person. It is the middle ground between univocal language (exactly the same sense) and equivocal language (unrelated senses, like a baseball bat and a flying bat).

**Cambridge change.** A new description becomes true of something only because something else changed. Socrates becomes shorter than Plato when Plato grows. God becomes Creator when the world comes to exist, with no change in God.

**Privation.** The absence of something a thing should have given its nature. Blindness is a privation in an eye; lacking wings is not a privation in a tree.

**Conservation and concurrence.** Conservation: nothing could remain in existence for an instant unless God were sustaining it. Concurrence: creatures are real causes, but they act only with God’s cooperation at every moment.

**Demonstration.** A deduction whose conclusion follows necessarily from premises that are certain and that no further empirical discovery could overturn. It is not a probabilistic “best explanation.”

## 4. The case told as a story

### The shared method

Every one of the first five chapters has the same architecture. First comes an informal statement of the argument in two stages. In Stage 1, Feser reasons from an everyday starting point to the existence of something fitting a key description. In Stage 2, he argues that anything fitting that description must be one, unchanging, eternal, immaterial, perfect, fully good, all-powerful, intelligent and all-knowing. Then comes a more formal statement in numbered steps, which summarizes the informal discussion rather than replacing it. Finally there is a long section answering objections. Feser deliberately avoids historical exegesis. The proofs are inspired by Aristotle, Plotinus, Augustine, Aquinas and Leibniz, but they are presented in Feser’s own way, with background metaphysics introduced only when needed. He describes the method as starting at the shallow end of the pool and working toward the deep end.

### The Aristotelian proof: from change to pure actuality

Start with your coffee cooling on your desk. Change is obvious. Even if your whole life were a dream, your experiences would still succeed one another, and even reasoning your way to the conclusion that change is an illusion would be a series of changes in your mind. The ancient philosopher Parmenides argued that change is impossible because it would mean something coming from nothing: the coldness is nothing while the coffee is hot. Aristotle’s reply was that the coldness is not nothing. It is there potentially, in a way that becoming chicken soup is not. Change is the actualization of a real potential.

A potential cannot actualize itself, because what is merely potential cannot do anything. Only something already actual can actualize it. The coffee is cooled by the air, the air by the air conditioner, the air conditioner by your finger on the button. Such chains stretch back through time, and Feser happily grants that they might stretch back forever. But now ask a different question. The coffee can cool only if it exists. What makes it exist right now? Its water exists because its matter is actually water rather than separate hydrogen and oxygen. That depends on the atoms being bonded in a certain way right now, which depends on their subatomic constituents being arranged in a certain way right now, and so on. This is not a chain stretching into the past; it is a stack operating at this moment, like the cup held by the desk held by the floor. Every member of such a stack acts only with borrowed power. A stack of borrowers, however tall, needs a lender. So there must be something that can actualize the existence of other things without itself being actualized — something with no potential at all. That is pure actuality, Aristotle’s Unmoved Mover. And it must be unmovable, not merely unmoved: a so-called first actualizer with unused potentials would need something else to actualize them, so it would not be first.

Stage 2 draws out the attributes. What has no potential cannot change, so it is immutable; what cannot change is outside time; material things change, so it is immaterial. Imperfection and badness are failures to realize potentials, so what is purely actual is perfect and fully good. Two purely actual beings could not differ in any way, since any difference would be a potential or a lack, so there can be only one. All power to actualize ultimately comes from it, so it is omnipotent. Finally, by the principle of proportionate causality, the patterns of everything it can cause must exist in it — not concretely, the way catness exists in a cat, but abstractly, the way ideas exist in a mind. So it has intellect, and since nothing lies outside the range of its thought, it is omniscient.

### The Neo-Platonic proof: from parts to the One

Look at the chair you are sitting on. It exists only if its legs, screws and seat exist and are held together in the right way right now. Your body is the same. This dependence is not merely a matter of assembly in the past; it holds at every moment, and it would hold even for a chair that had always existed. Can a chair be the cause of its own parts being combined? No, because then the chair would be both the effect of the combination and its cause, lifting itself by its own bootstraps. So at each moment a composite depends on other things: friction holding the screws, molecular bonds holding the wood, a room that is not so hot the wood burns. Those factors are composite too. The principle is general and applies to metaphysical parts as well — form and matter, essence and existence. So we get another here-and-now stack, which must end in something that has no parts at all, something absolutely simple. Following Plotinus, Feser calls it the One.

Stage 2 shows the One must be unique, since anything distinguishing two simple causes would be a part. It must be changeless, since change means gaining or losing a feature. It must be eternal, since beginning or ending would require parts to be assembled or dissolved. Borrowing a division from William Vallicella — everything is a mind, a mental content, a physical thing or an abstract object — Feser argues that the One is not an abstract object, because abstract objects cause nothing; not physical, because physical things have parts; and not a mental content, because contents depend on minds. So it is a mind, or more like a mind than anything else. And having no potential part, it must be purely actual — which means the One is the same being as the Unmoved Mover. This chapter also introduces Frege’s idea that “the morning star” and “the evening star” differ in sense but name the same planet, and it introduces analogical language. Both explain how God’s many attributes can be one simple reality.

### The Augustinian proof: from abstract truths to a divine mind

Think of the triangular rack on a pool table. It is one triangle; triangularity is shared by every triangle that is, was or could be. The truth that the angles of a Euclidean triangle add up to two right angles was discovered, not invented, and it would remain true if every human died. Numbers, propositions and possible worlds are similar. Feser gives several arguments that such objects are real: the problem of the one shared by many, the objective truths of geometry and mathematics, the nature of propositions, the reliance of science on universal laws and mathematics, and the reality of possibilities that no one has considered. Nominalism, which says there are only general words, fails because resemblance is itself a universal (Russell’s regress) and because words themselves are universals — the one word “red” spoken many times. Conceptualism, which says universals are mind-made, fails because we share concepts and communicate, and because theories that reduce logic to psychology undermine themselves.

So some realism is true. Platonic realism puts abstract objects in a third realm, but objects there would be causally inert, so it is hard to see how we could know them or how they could make anything what it is. Worse, a Form would have to be both universal and a particular thing. Animality itself would have to be neither rational nor nonrational, yet nothing individual can be neither. Aristotelian realism, which places universals in things and in the minds that abstract them, is better, but it cannot ground possibilities and necessary truths that would hold even if no material world and no human minds had ever existed — the possibility of unicorns, or the truth that two plus two equals four. What remains is Scholastic realism: abstract objects exist in an infinite, eternal, divine intellect.

Stage 2 argues that this intellect must exist necessarily, since human, alien and even angelic minds are all contingent. It must be one, because abstract objects form an interlocking system: grasping one concept requires grasping others, and every proposition stands in logical relations to every other. A mind that grasps them all is, in Robert Adams’ phrase, conceptually omniscient. A necessarily existing mind must be purely actual, since otherwise its existence would depend on something actualizing it, and so it has all the attributes of chapter 1. Greg Welty’s conditions for an adequate theory of abstract objects point the same way.

### The Thomistic proof: from essence and existence to existence itself

Describe a lion, a pterodactyl and a unicorn to someone who has never heard of them, and ask which exists, which is extinct and which never existed. Your description of what each is will not tell him. So a thing’s existence is not contained in what it is. Feser offers three arguments that the distinction between essence and existence is real, not merely verbal. The first is the one just given. The second is that things are contingent; if their existence were their essence, they could not fail to exist. The third is that something whose essence just is existence could only be one, since anything distinguishing two of them would make each “existence itself plus something,” and so not existence itself — yet there are, or could be, many stones, dogs and people. A real distinction does not require that the two items could exist separately; the radius and circumference of a circle are really distinct but inseparable.

A thing whose existence is distinct from its essence cannot give itself existence, because it would have to exist before it existed. So something else must impart existence to it, and not just when it began but at every moment, or it would simply blink out. Fido the dog needs a cause of his existence right now. If that cause also has an essence distinct from its existence, it too needs a cause right now, and we have another hierarchical stack. It can end only in something whose essence is existence — what Aquinas called subsistent existence itself. Stage 2 shows it is unique, necessary, uncaused, the cause of everything else, and purely actual, since essence and existence relate as potential to actual. So it is the same being again.

### The rationalist proof: from explanation to a necessary being

We always assume things have explanations. Sunglasses on your sofa were left by a friend; a rash came from poison oak; even an unsolved murder has an explanation of why it is unsolved. The principle of sufficient reason says everything has an explanation. It is extremely well confirmed by experience. More importantly, denying it undermines itself: if things can happen for no reason, we have no reason to trust that our perceptions connect to the world, or that our beliefs — including the belief that the principle is false — are held because of good reasons. Explanations that bottom out in a brute fact are illusions; a book resting on a shelf that floats for no reason at all has not been explained. And the philosopher Michael Della Rocca points out that people use explanation-based arguments everywhere else and have no principled reason to exempt the existence of things.

Anything is explained either by something else (so it is contingent) or by its own nature (so it is necessary). Contingent things exist. Suppose every contingent thing were caused by an earlier one, forever. The existence of the whole series would still need explaining, because a collection of contingent things is contingent — the way a structure of red Lego blocks is red, not the way a pile of light stones can be heavy. And specific features would remain unexplained: Leibniz imagined a geometry book copied from an earlier copy, forever; we would still not know why the series contains geometry rather than Shakespeare. Appealing to a further contingent cause only starts a higher series with the same problem. Or ask simply why any one contingent thing persists at this moment; a present stack of contingent causes cannot answer. Only a necessary being can end the regress. Stage 2 shows a necessary being must be purely actual, simple and existence itself, since potential, parts or received existence would make it depend on something. Feser adds that the deep reason things are contingent is exactly that they are mixtures of act and potency, composed of parts, and have existence distinct from essence.

### Why the five meet

Chapter 6 makes the convergence explicit. What is purely actual has no parts, because parts only potentially compose a whole until combined. What is simple has no potential, because a potential would be a second component. What is purely actual has no essence distinct from its existence, because essence relates to existence as potential to actual. What is existence itself depends on no actualization. What is necessary can have no potential, parts or received existence, and anything lacking these depends on nothing and so is necessary. A necessary mind must be purely actual. And there can be only one such being, because any two would need something to tell them apart, which would be a part or a potential. So the five conclusions describe one reality, as the morning star and the evening star are one planet.

### How chapter 6 completes the case

Chapter 6 defends three background principles. The principle of proportionate causality is defended against the charge that it is refuted by sponge cakes or by evolution; those examples concern only material ingredients, whereas the principle concerns total causes, and it never required causes to hand over their own features. Agere sequitur esse says action reflects being. Analogical language is shown to be necessary even to talk about potentiality and actuality. Then come the attributes: unity; simplicity, defended against Alvin Plantinga’s claim that it makes God a property; immutability, where acting without being changed is shown to be coherent and becoming Creator is a Cambridge change; immateriality; eternity as strict timelessness, illustrated by an author who conceives a whole story at once; necessity, defended against claims that necessity is only verbal; omnipotence, where the stone paradox describes no genuine power; omniscience, where God knows the world by knowing himself as its cause and his knowledge no more removes our freedom than an author removes a character’s free choice; perfect goodness, with evil understood as privation; free will, including the point that there is no best possible world; love, understood as willing another’s good; and incomprehensibility, since we cannot understand a partless being the way we understand things by their parts.

The chapter then shows that God conserves everything in existence at every moment, which rules out the idea that things could have existential inertia, and rules out pantheism and panentheism. Combined with the principle that action follows being, it yields concurrence: creatures are real causes, but secondary ones, like chalk that makes a square blue while the hand makes there be a square at all. This excludes occasionalism, which denies creatures any causal power, and mere conservationism, which drifts toward deism. Finally, a miracle is not a violation of natural law but an elevation of nature beyond its powers, or a suspension in which God refrains from sustaining some cause. Miracles would be the bridge from natural theology to revealed religion.

### How chapter 7 completes the case

Chapter 7 argues that the most common objections attack a caricature: “everything has a cause, so the universe has a cause, God.” No major defender of cosmological arguments ever used that premise; even the critic Robin Le Poidevin admits it. Following W. Norris Clarke, Feser traces the caricature back through Russell and the Mills to Hume, who confused the rationalist idea that everything has a sufficient reason with the idea that everything has an efficient cause distinct from itself. The chapter then answers a long list of objections, from “maybe the universe is the necessary being” to scientism, the problem of evil, divine hiddenness, and the claim that it cannot be a proof if so many people reject it. Its closing point is about what a demonstration is: a strict deduction from premises that any empirical inquiry presupposes, which can be controversial without failing.

## 5. The convergence argument, step by step

This summary argument is the course’s synthesis of the book, built from the formal statements of chapters 1 to 5 and the unity section of chapter 6.

1. Change is real, and every changing thing exists at each moment only through the concurrent actualization of its potential to exist. *Justification: denying change is itself a change; the coffee’s water depends right now on atoms and particles.*
2. So there is a purely actual actualizer. *Justification: a hierarchical series of actualizers needs an underived first member with no potency.*
3. The things of experience are composite, and a composite exists at any moment only if something combines its parts. *Justification: nothing can compose itself.*
4. So there is an absolutely simple cause, the One. *Justification: the regress of composers is hierarchical, and a composite first member would need a cause.*
5. Abstract objects are real, do not depend on matter or contingent minds, and cannot exist in a Platonic third realm. *Justification: the case for realism and against nominalism, conceptualism and Platonism.*
6. So they exist in a necessarily existing intellect that grasps their whole interlocking system. *Justification: only minds can house what is abstract, and only a necessary mind can ground necessary truths.*
7. In each thing we experience, essence is really distinct from existence, so each must receive existence from another at every moment. *Justification: knowledge of essence without existence, contingency, and the uniqueness of existence itself.*
8. So there is subsistent existence itself. *Justification: the hierarchical regress of existence-givers must end in something that is existence.*
9. The principle of sufficient reason is true, and no contingent thing or series of them can explain why contingent things exist or persist. *Justification: denying PSR undermines reasoning; contingency carries over to collections.*
10. So there is an absolutely necessary being. *Justification: explanation by another or by one’s own nature; nothing is explained by nothing.*
11. Whatever has parts is a mixture of actuality and potentiality. *Justification: parts only potentially compose a whole.*
12. Whatever has potentiality is composite. *Justification: potency would be a second constituent alongside act.*
13. So something is purely actual if and only if it is simple; steps 2 and 4 describe the same kind of being. *Justification: from 11 and 12.*
14. Essence and existence are related as potency to act. *Justification: Fido’s essence by itself is only a possible dog.*
15. So something is purely actual if and only if it is subsistent existence itself; steps 2, 4 and 8 describe the same kind of being. *Justification: from 14, together with 13.*
16. To be contingent is to depend on actualized potency, on combined parts, or on received existence. *Justification: these are the ways something can fail to exist of itself.*
17. So something is necessary if and only if it is purely actual, simple and existence itself; step 10 describes the same kind of being. *Justification: from 16 in both directions.*
18. A necessarily existing intellect must be purely actual. *Justification: otherwise its existence would depend on an actualizer.*
19. There cannot be more than one purely actual being. *Justification: a differentiating feature would be a part or a potency.*
20. So the purely actual actualizer, the One, the necessary intellect, existence itself and the necessary being are one and the same. *Justification: from 13, 15, 17, 18 and 19.*
21. Whatever is in an effect is in its total cause formally, virtually or eminently. *Justification: a cause cannot give what it in no way has.*
22. So this being has intellect and omniscience. *Justification: the forms of all possible things exist in it as ideas in a mind; step 6 reaches the same result directly.*
23. It is also immutable, eternal, immaterial, perfect, fully good, omnipotent, uncaused and the cause of everything else. *Justification: Stage 2 of every chapter.*
24. Nothing else could exist or act for an instant unless it conserved and concurred with it. *Justification: dependence here and now plus “action follows being.”*
25. For such a being to exist is what it is for God to exist. *Justification: this is the traditional concept of God.*
26. So God exists — one God, reached by five paths. *Justification: from the preceding steps.*
27. So the case is robust: a critic must refute each proof independently, and the interlocking conclusions show the divine attributes are facets of a single idea. *Justification: the independence of the routes and the necessity of their convergence.*

## 6. The objection handbook

**1. “If everything has a cause, what caused God?”** None of the proofs says everything has a cause. Each says that things with a particular feature need a cause — potential being actualized, parts being combined, existence being received, contingency — and shows there must be something lacking that very feature. The rationalist proof makes no exception at all: God has an explanation, in his own nature. The Augustinian proof is not causal. (Chapters 1, 2, 4, 5, 7.)

**2. “The argument assumes the universe had a beginning.”** Every causal proof grants a beginningless universe or multiverse for argument’s sake. The concern is what sustains things now. (Chapters 1, 2, 4, 7.)

**3. “An infinite regress could explain everything.”** For chains through time, Feser agrees a regress is possible. But in a here-and-now stack each member only passes on borrowed power; infinite mirrors still need a face and infinite IOUs never become money. (Chapters 1, 4, 5, 7.)

**4. “Fallacy of composition.”** The proofs start from single things, and the claim that God causes everything is a conclusion. Where a proof does reason from parts to whole, contingency behaves like color, not weight. The critic who thinks explaining each member explains the series commits the fallacy himself. (Chapters 4, 5, 7.)

**5. “Hume showed something could begin without a cause.”** Imagining something appear without imagining its cause is not imagining it without a cause, just as thinking of a man without thinking of his height does not show he could lack one. And Elizabeth Anscombe asked what would make the imagined appearance a beginning rather than teleportation — only a reference to causes. (Chapters 1, 7.)

**6. “Quantum mechanics shows events without causes.”** Indeterministic does not mean unintelligible. A decaying atom behaves according to its nature, was produced by something, and is kept in existence now. Physics’ mathematical method leaves causes out of its description, as a line drawing leaves out color. (Chapters 1, 5, 7.)

**7. “Physics has eliminated causation and change.”** Terms like “law” and “explanation” are not in the equations either; physics describes structure, not the intrinsic nature of what changes; the evidence for any theory comes through changing experiences; and even an unchanging block universe would need a cause of its being actual. (Chapter 1.)

**8. “The universe or its laws could be the necessary being.”** The universe changes, has parts and could have been otherwise. Laws either describe regularities, presuppose the things that follow them, or need their own explanation. And a necessary universe would be pantheism, not atheism. (Chapters 5, 6, 7.)

**9. “Brute facts are acceptable.”** Denying the principle of sufficient reason undermines reasoning, makes all explanations resting on the brute fact illusory, and is adopted only to avoid theism. (Chapters 5, 7.)

**10. “A first cause needn’t be God.”** Stage 2 of every chapter and all of chapter 6 derive the divine attributes. Historically, defenders always argued for them. (Chapters 1 to 7.)

**11. “A thing’s parts might unify themselves.”** A unifying part is one more thing needing to be unified; a brute unity is not an explanation; laws plus what they govern are another composite. (Chapter 2.)

**12. “The essence–existence distinction is merely conceptual.”** A complete grasp of aspirin reveals it is acetylsalicylic acid, but no complete grasp of a lion’s essence reveals its existence; Anthony Kenny assumes a real distinction requires separability and that the logical quantifier captures all there is to existence. (Chapter 4.)

**13. “Abstract objects don’t need a divine mind.”** Platonic objects would be inert and unknowable; ideas in a mind are exactly what abstraction produces; and Patrick Grim’s argument against a set of all truths proves too much and ignores divine simplicity. (Chapter 3.)

**14. “Divine simplicity is incoherent.”** Plantinga imports a broad notion of property, a Platonist view of properties and univocal language; classical theists reject all three and treat contingent truths about God as Cambridge properties. (Chapters 2, 6.)

**15. “An unchanging, timeless God couldn’t act, love or choose.”** Being changed is not essential to acting, loving or choosing; assuming it is commits a fallacy of accident. God causes a whole temporal world in one act, loves by willing good, and is free because nothing compels him. (Chapter 6.)

**16. “If God knows the future, we aren’t free.”** Knowledge does not cause what it knows; and God relates to the world as an author to a story in which characters act freely, not as a hypnotist inside it. (Chapter 6.)

**17. “It’s a god of the gaps; physics explains a universe from nothing.”** The proofs are demonstrations from what any empirical theory presupposes. A quantum vacuum and physical laws are not nothing. (Chapter 7.)

**18. “Only science gives knowledge.”** That claim is not scientific, so it refutes itself — or, if “science” includes philosophy, it becomes trivial. Physics’ success no more shows it captures everything than a metal detector’s success shows only metal exists. (Chapters 1, 7.)

**19. “Evil and hiddenness disprove a good God.”** Some goods, like forgiveness, courage and compassion, logically require evils; evil is privation; and anyone with independent proof of a good God has reason to believe there is a greater good even when it is unseen. Most people have in fact believed in the divine. (Chapters 6, 7.)

**20. “Go one god further; the God of philosophers isn’t the God of religion.”** Classical theism’s God is not one member of a genus “gods” but being itself, so the comparison is a category mistake, like treating triangularity as one more drawn triangle. And ordinary believers hold the same God in a less sophisticated way, as laymen believe in atoms. (Chapter 7.)

## 7. Common misunderstandings

- **“The proofs argue for a Big Bang creator.”** They grant a beginningless universe and argue for a present sustainer.
- **“They rely on everything having a cause.”** They rely on narrower causal principles and on the principle that everything has an explanation.
- **“They reach five gods, or just a vague first cause.”** The five conclusions are provably one being with all the divine attributes.
- **“They are scientific hypotheses.”** They are metaphysical demonstrations and do not compete with physics.
- **“God is the biggest being in the universe.”** God is not a being among beings but subsistent being itself.
- **“Simplicity means God has only one property, or is a property.”** It means God has no parts; his attributes are one reality under different descriptions.
- **“Analogical means metaphorical.”** Analogical language is literal.
- **“An unchanging God is inert and unloving.”** He acts without being changed and loves by willing good.
- **“Miracles violate natural law.”** They elevate or suspend nature; God cannot literally intervene in his own ongoing work.
- **“A real proof would convince everyone.”** Controversy does not show that a demonstration fails.

## 8. How to explain it to others

**The simple version.** Everything around us depends, right now, on other things: the cup on the desk, the desk on the floor, your body on its cells, the cells on their molecules. Each is borrowing its existence and power. A chain of borrowers can be as long as you like, but if everything in it is only borrowing, nothing is actually lending. So there has to be something that borrows nothing — no potential waiting to be realized, no parts to hold together, no existence to receive. Feser finds that same something by five different roads, and shows it must be one, unchanging, eternal, immaterial, all-powerful, all-knowing and good. That is what people have always meant by God.

**The best analogy.** A paintbrush moves paint only because a hand moves it. Make the handle a mile long or infinitely long; it still cannot move itself. Length adds nothing. What the series needs is something with power of its own. Add a second image for the convergence: five trails climbing one mountain from five valleys. The hikers describe the summit differently, but they end up standing on the same rock.

**Choosing a proof for the listener.** Scientists and engineers usually respond to the Aristotelian or Neo-Platonic proof, with the question “what keeps this existing right now?” Mathematicians and logicians, who already believe in objective truths, are natural audiences for the Augustinian proof. People who ask why anything exists, or who prize explanation, suit the rationalist proof. Philosophy students often relish the Thomistic proof. Believers who picture God as a very powerful person benefit from simplicity and analogy. Casual skeptics first need the caricatures removed. Whatever you choose, give an everyday example, state the key principle exactly, stress the here-and-now point, include the Stage 2 attributes, and answer the one objection that listener is most likely to raise. Keep Feser’s content and leave behind his combative tone.

## 9. Key takeaways and review questions

### Key takeaways

- Each of the five proofs has two stages: reach a precisely described cause, then derive the divine attributes from that description.
- The four causal proofs concern hierarchical dependence here and now; they grant that the universe may have no beginning.
- “First cause” means a cause with underived power, not the earliest event.
- The principles used are narrow: potential actualized, parts combined, existence received, contingency explained. None says everything has a cause.
- Pure actuality, simplicity, subsistent existence and necessity entail one another, a necessary intellect is purely actual, and there can be only one such being. So the proofs converge on one God.
- Chapter 6 grounds the attributes in proportionate causality, action following being and analogy, and explains conservation, concurrence and miracles.
- Chapter 7 shows that the famous objections target a caricature and that scientism, brute facts, evil and hiddenness do not defeat the proofs.
- The proofs are offered as demonstrations; controversy is not refutation, and critics must identify a false premise or an invalid step.
- Natural theology establishes classical theism, not any particular revealed religion; miracles would be the bridge.

### Ten review questions with brief answers

1. **What does Stage 2 of each proof do?** It shows that whatever fits the Stage 1 description must be one, immutable, eternal, immaterial, perfect, good, omnipotent, intelligent and omniscient.
2. **What is the difference between a linear and a hierarchical causal series?** A linear series runs through time and its members have their own power; a hierarchical series operates now and its members have only borrowed power, so it needs an underived first member.
3. **Why is “What caused God?” a bad objection?** Because the proofs say only things with potential, parts, received existence or contingency need causes, and they show God lacks exactly those features.
4. **What is the starting point of the Augustinian proof, and why does it end in a necessary mind?** The reality of abstract objects; they can exist only in things or minds, and necessary, eternal truths need a necessary, eternal mind.
5. **Give one argument for the real distinction between essence and existence.** We can know what a lion or unicorn is without knowing whether it exists.
6. **Why can’t an infinite series of contingent things explain itself?** The series is itself contingent, its features remain unexplained, and further contingent causes only start a new series with the same problem.
7. **Why must the purely actual actualizer and the One be the same being?** Anything with parts has potential, and anything with potential has parts, so the purely actual and the simple coincide, and there can be only one of either.
8. **How can an unchanging God create the world?** Acting does not essentially involve being changed, and becoming Creator is a Cambridge change — a change in the world, not in God.
9. **What is Feser’s reply to scientism?** The claim that only science gives knowledge is not itself scientific, so it refutes itself; science’s success at what it studies does not show nothing else exists.
10. **What do the proofs not establish?** They do not establish the doctrines of any particular religion; that further question depends on revelation, for which miracles would be the rational sign.
