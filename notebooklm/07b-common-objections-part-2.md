# Common Objections to Natural Theology, Part II: Scientism, Other Gods, Evil, and Hiddenness

*A study guide to the second half of Chapter 7 of Edward Feser's* Five Proofs of the Existence of God *(Ignatius Press, 2017), pages 273–308*

## 1. Orientation

Edward Feser's *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* defends five classic arguments for God's existence. Each starts from a very general feature of the world and argues that it could not exist without a cause that is purely actual, absolutely simple, and necessary:

- the **Aristotelian proof**, from change;
- the **Neo-Platonic proof**, from things being made of parts;
- the **Augustinian proof**, from abstract truths such as those of logic and mathematics;
- the **Thomistic proof**, from the distinction between what a thing is and the fact that it exists;
- the **rationalist proof**, from the principle of sufficient reason.

Chapter 6 works out God's attributes. Chapter 7, the last chapter, answers objections. Its first half handled technical objections to the cosmological argument: "What caused God?", challenges to causation from Hume and quantum mechanics, and Lawrence Krauss's universe from "nothing." This second half, which ends the book, turns to the broad objections that dominate popular culture:

1. Science is the only real source of knowledge (**scientism**).
2. The fundamental laws of nature are a **brute fact**.
3. A designer would be **more complex** than the universe.
4. Go **"one god further."**
5. The **God of the philosophers** isn't the God of ordinary believers.
6. The **problem of evil**, logical and evidential.
7. **Divine hiddenness**.
8. Arguments for God are just **rationalizations**.
9. It can't be a **proof** if so many people remain unconvinced.

## 2. Why This Chapter Matters

Most people who dismiss arguments for God have never studied them. They are working from a general conviction that rules the arguments out in advance, and the chief one is **scientism**: the belief that science is the only way to know anything. If scientism were true, Feser's arguments would be "unscientific" and could be ignored. That is why he spends so long on it. Once scientism falls, the critic has to engage the proofs premise by premise.

The rest of the section teaches a skill worth having: **diagnosing an objection**. Popular objections almost always make one of three moves:

1. **They refute themselves.** Scientism and Hume's Fork fail their own tests.
2. **They attack a caricature.** They picture God as one more complicated being inside reality, which is exactly what the proofs deny.
3. **They use the wrong standard.** They judge demonstrations by probability, by whether everyone agrees, or by the motives of the person arguing.

Learn to spot these moves and you can answer most objections briefly and calmly. The section also faces the most emotionally charged objection, suffering, and Feser's answer there is careful and deliberately modest.

## 3. Key Concepts and Vocabulary

**Scientism.** The view that science is the only genuine source of knowledge. It is not science itself but a philosophical claim about the limits of all knowledge.

**Self-refuting claim.** A claim that undercuts the grounds for believing it. "Only scientifically established claims are knowledge" is not itself scientifically established.

**Presuppositions of science.** Things science must assume and can't prove without circularity: a real world independent of our minds, regularities in that world, and minds able to discover them.

**Methodological abstraction.** Attending only to some features of a thing for a purpose. Engineers treat passengers as average weights; physics treats nature as mathematical structure.

**Structure versus intrinsic nature.** Physics gives the mathematical relations among events but not what the related things are in themselves (Bertrand Russell's point).

**Laws of nature.** On the *Aristotelian* view they describe how things act given their natures. On the *Cartesian-Newtonian* view they are God's decrees. On the *Humean* view they summarize observed regularities. On the *Platonic* view they are abstract objects that things "participate" in.

**Brute fact.** A fact with no explanation of any kind.

**Principle of sufficient reason (PSR).** Everything has an explanation, either in its own nature or in something else (defended in chapter 5).

**Hume's Fork.** Hume's claim that everything knowable is either a relation of ideas (a conceptual truth) or a matter of fact (an empirical truth). It is neither, so it refutes itself.

**Conceptual analysis.** Philosophy seen as mapping how our concepts relate, which critics say shows how we think rather than how the world is.

**Design argument.** An argument like Paley's or Intelligent Design's: from complex, precisely arranged structures (the eye), by analogy with artifacts, to a probable designer.

**Strict demonstration.** A deductively valid argument from premises that are certain, their certainty established by metaphysical analysis. It is not a probability estimate.

**Category mistake.** Treating something as a member of a category it doesn't belong to, such as treating triangularity as one more triangle.

**Classical theism.** The conception of God found in Aristotle, Plotinus, Augustine, Maimonides, Avicenna, Aquinas, and Leibniz: absolutely simple, immutable, eternal, purely actual, and existence itself.

**Subsistent being itself.** God is not a thing that has existence and receives it from elsewhere. He *is* existence, the source of every other thing's existence.

**Defense versus theodicy.** A *defense* shows that evil is compatible with God. A *theodicy* explains why God actually permits evil.

**Logical versus evidential problem of evil.** The first says God and evil contradict each other. The second says evil makes God improbable.

**Epistemic distance.** John Hick's idea that God keeps his existence less than obvious so that we are free to choose whether to relate to him.

**Divine hiddenness.** J. L. Schellenberg's argument that a loving God would make his existence obvious.

**Poisoning the well.** Dismissing an argument because of the arguer's supposed motives.

## 4. The Story of the Argument

### Part One: The Case Against Scientism

Feser calls scientism a key ingredient of the New Atheism and makes a strong claim about it: there are no good arguments for it and decisive arguments against it. He raises four problems.

#### Problem one: self-refuting or trivial

Consider the claim, in Alex Rosenberg's version, that science's methods are the only reliable route to knowledge. What experiment established that? None did, and none could. It is a philosophical claim about science.

Science can't even prove that it is *a* rational form of inquiry. Every experiment assumes a mind-independent world, regularities that laws can capture, and minds able to discover them. Science can't prove these assumptions without arguing in a circle. Justifying them means stepping outside science, and the existence of that outside standpoint shows science isn't the only rational way of knowing.

Science also needs philosophy to interpret its results. Is the world made of things or of events? What is a cause? Are there universals, like "electron," over and above individual electrons? Do theories describe reality or just predict? Science bears on these questions but can't settle them. So science depends on philosophy at both ends, and Feser agrees with John Kekes that philosophy has the better claim to be the model of rationality.

The defender of scientism might answer, "Then philosophy is part of science." But now "science" covers anything that could be said against scientism, and the thesis becomes empty. It also lets in what it was meant to keep out. Aristotle and Aquinas considered natural theology a science. It isn't mathematical or experimental like physics, but they held it to be *more* certain than physics, because it demonstrates from premises every physical science takes for granted. Stretch "science" that far and natural theology can't be excluded. Scientism was supposed to justify dismissing natural theology unheard. On either horn it can't.

#### Problem two: science can't describe everything

The method that makes physics successful also makes it incomplete. Following Galileo's image of nature as a book written in mathematics, physics describes the world in purely quantitative terms. So it naturally finds the aspects of reality that can be predicted and controlled, because those are the only aspects it looks at. Everything else falls through its net.

Experience is full of qualities: colors, sounds, tastes, warmth, pain, thoughts, purposes, meanings. Physics sets them aside, which is fine as long as we remember we're doing it. Feser's illustration: engineers working out an airplane's capacity treat each passenger as an average weight, a figure no actual passenger may have. That is exactly right for their purpose. But suppose the catering staff concluded that since the engineers' data say nothing about food preferences, passengers have none. The data are silent because the method guaranteed it. When physics leaves out a familiar feature, it hasn't discovered that the feature is absent. It decided in advance not to look.

Feser adds a point from Bertrand Russell, no friend of theism. Russell stressed that physics gives only the abstract structure of events and is silent about the intrinsic nature of whatever has that structure. But a structure can't exist on its own; something has to have it. So physics itself implies there is more to reality than physics describes.

#### Problem three: laws can't be the ultimate explanation

Science explains by laws, but laws raise their own questions. What is a law? How can it make anything happen? Where does it come from? Science presupposes laws, so it can't answer these questions. Krauss's "layers" of laws just multiply them. Feser shows that none of the main theories of laws helps scientism:

- **Aristotelian:** laws describe how things act given their natures. So they presuppose the things and can't explain why the things, or the universe, exist.
- **Descartes and Newton:** laws are God's ordering of the world. Then God does the real explaining.
- **Humean:** laws summarize regularities. They tell us *that* a pattern holds, not *why*. They re-describe; they don't explain.
- **Platonic:** laws are abstract objects things participate in. Then we still need to explain why a physical world participates in them at all, and in these laws rather than others.

Someone might reply that ultimate explanation simply isn't available. But that is a philosophical claim, not a scientific one, and it is false, because it violates the principle of sufficient reason.

#### Problem four: the argument from success

Why do smart people accept scientism? Borrowing from Wittgenstein, Feser says a picture holds them captive: the picture of science's predictive and technological triumphs. Rosenberg argues that physics' success shows its methods are the right ones for all knowledge. Feser reduces the argument to two steps. Science's predictive power and technology are unmatched; therefore, what science reveals is probably all that is real.

That is as good as arguing that because metal detectors find metal better than any other method, metal is probably all that exists. A metal detector responds to one aspect of the world, and even a perfect record wouldn't make it likely that nothing else exists. Physics brilliantly captures what can be modeled mathematically. It doesn't follow that nothing else is real.

Rosenberg adds that critics of scientism are inconsistent: they trust their cardiologists and mechanics but cry "scientism" when their morals or metaphysics are examined. Feser replies that you can trust metal detectors without thinking they alone detect physical objects. Trusting a tool for its job doesn't mean thinking it's the only tool. And if someone says science, unlike a metal detector, covers the whole of reality, he is assuming the very point in dispute.

The same fallacy shows up in the demand, "What has metaphysics ever predicted?" That's like asking for the metal-detecting record of gardening, cooking, and painting and then throwing out spades, spatulas, and brushes. It's also like the drunk who looks for his keys only under the streetlamp because that's where he can see, and concludes they can't be anywhere else.

#### The fallback positions

**"Science covers everything worth knowing."** Some retreat to this: philosophy's questions never get settled and never produce technology, so science covers all that matters. Feser's first reply is that the success is rigged. It's like a student who takes only classes he knows he'll ace and then cites his GPA as proof of his brilliance. If only predictable, consensus-producing questions count as "science," its success is guaranteed and proves nothing about the questions it leaves out. His second reply is that the claim that only such questions matter is itself philosophy.

**"Anything else is mere conceptual analysis."** James Ladyman and Don Ross argue that unless philosophy is an extension of science, all it can be is conceptual analysis, which relies on intuitions that may not track reality. Feser calls this a false choice and a version of **Hume's Fork**. The claim that every legitimate inquiry is either science or conceptual analysis is neither a conceptual truth nor something scientific evidence could support, so it refutes itself. The person making it takes a third kind of stance toward reality in the very act of denying one exists. In a footnote Feser adds that appealing to neuroscience would beg the question, since whether such sciences are our only source of knowledge about the mind is the point at issue.

**Logic and mathematics.** Their truths fit neither prong of the fork. They are *necessary*, unlike empirical findings, and *objective*, which mere conceptual analysis can't guarantee. Chapter 3 showed the serious difficulties facing attempts to deny this, and the only motive for such attempts is to save scientism. Natural theology's conclusions belong in the same group: necessary and objective, but neither scientific nor merely conceptual. If the scientism advocate can't see how such knowledge is possible, that's his problem. He already relies on logic, mathematics, and his own fork without being able to account for them. Feser compares him to a thief caught with the loot who demands that the arresting officers prove they own their squad car.

### Part Two: The Remaining Objections

#### The laws are a brute fact

Suppose the atheist grants all of that and still says the deepest laws simply have no explanation. They explain everything else and are themselves just there. Feser says Mackie and Russell essentially held this. He sees three problems.

1. It contradicts the principle of sufficient reason, so it is a nonstarter.
2. It is ad hoc. Its only motive is to avoid arguments like Feser's, which is ironic given how often atheists accuse theists of rigging premises. The Humean and quantum challenges to PSR, which might have motivated it, fail. And, as Lloyd Gerson observed, when an argument offers an explanation, you can't answer it by saying there might be none. You have to show what's wrong with the explanation offered.
3. It drains every law of explanatory power. Explaining an event by law A, A by law B, and then calling B a brute fact is like resting a book on a shelf, the shelf on brackets, and then letting go of the brackets in midair. A regress of laws works like the hierarchical series of causes in chapter 1. It needs a first member that explains itself and passes explanatory power along without borrowing it. Only something purely actual, without parts, whose essence is existence, and so absolutely necessary, can be that first member.

#### A designer would be more complex than the universe

Hume and Dawkins argue that a designer of the universe would have to be more complex than the universe and so would need a cause of its own. This objection targets **design arguments** like Paley's, which reason from complex, precisely arranged structures such as the eye, by analogy with artifacts, to a probable designer. The five proofs are different in three ways.

- **They don't appeal to complexity.** The Neo-Platonic proof starts from things having parts, but it doesn't care how many or how precisely arranged. A pebble or a drop of water would work, and Paley would never use either.
- **They don't appeal to probability.** They concern hierarchical causes acting here and now, not a chain back to the origin of species. And they are strict demonstrations, so asking whether some rival explanation is "more parsimonious" misses the point. Refuting them means showing a false premise, an invalid step, or a begged question.
- **Most importantly, they agree with the objection.** A complex cause would need a cause. That is exactly why the proofs conclude that the first cause is *absolutely simple*. That is true of the God they reach and of nothing the atheist proposes as the final explanation.

#### Go "one god further"

Dawkins and Colin McGinn argue that monotheists already reject Zeus, Venus, Thor, and Quetzalcoatl, and atheists simply reject one more god. Feser's first reply is that there are at least five demonstrations of the God of classical monotheism and no one offers arguments for Zeus, so there's no inconsistency.

His deeper reply is that the objection is a **category mistake**. It pictures God as one member of a class of supernatural beings, like werewolves or ghosts. The God of classical theism belongs to no genus, since that would make him composite and he is simple. He shares no essence with other gods, since that would separate his essence from his existence and he *is* existence. And he isn't one cause among many but the purely actual source of all causal power, including that of any gods that might exist. The pagan gods of the stories have bodies, moods, parents, beginnings, failures, ignorance, and vices. Each is *a being*. God is being itself.

Feser's analogies make the point vivid. Imagine a critic of Euclidean geometry who says, "You agree that drawn triangles have imperfect sides. I just go one step further and say triangularity itself does." Or a critic of Plato who says the Form of the Good is imperfect too. Both confuse the standard with one more thing measured by it. The point holds whether or not Euclid or Plato is right. If space were non-Euclidean, Euclidean triangles would simply have no instances; they wouldn't have crooked sides. If Plato were wrong, there would be no Form of the Good, not an imperfect one. Likewise, even if atheism were true, it wouldn't be because God is one more nonexistent god. That idea doesn't even make sense.

#### The philosophers' God is not the believers' God

First, this would be irrelevant even if true. If the proofs work, atheism is false, and what remains is a debate among theists about revelation. Second, it is false. Standard Catholic teaching holds that the God of the Bible is the God reachable by reason. Some truths about him, like his omnipotence, can be known both ways, and others, like the Trinity, only through revelation. Other religions make similar claims. And ordinary believers really do believe in this God, just less technically. Someone who pictures molecules as balls on sticks still believes in atoms. The ordinary believer who holds that God is the unique, uncaused, all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good cause of the world believes in the same God the philosopher does.

#### The problem of evil

Feser separates two questions. Is evil consistent with God? If so, why does God permit it? Answering the first is a **defense**, answering the second a **theodicy**. A defense is enough to rebut the objection, and it is what Feser offers.

**Mackie's logical argument.** J. L. Mackie argued as follows. God is supposed to be all-powerful and perfectly good. An all-powerful being has no limits, and a good being eliminates evil as far as it can. So God would eliminate all evil, and yet evil exists. Feser, drawing on chapter 6, says the second premise fails twice.

- Even omnipotence can't do contradictions, like making a round square. If some goods logically require permitting certain evils, God can't have the goods without the evils.
- A good being doesn't necessarily remove every evil it can, because some goods depend on evils. A parent could end a child's homework misery by cancelling the homework, but a good parent won't, because the child would lose the learning.

Mackie anticipated the free-will reply: God could have made free creatures who always freely choose good. Alvin Plantinga and others deny that God could guarantee this, but Feser says the theist needn't go that route. Grant that such a world is possible, even one with serious moral choices. It still couldn't contain *all the goods of ours*. Forgiveness and mercy require actual wrongdoing. Courage requires danger, compassion requires suffering, and sacrifice requires giving up something good (John Hick's "soul-making" theme). Mackie himself later conceded that evil doesn't make theism inconsistent. Feser adds that he is not claiming, as Leibniz did, that this is the best possible world. God could have created a better world in various respects, or none at all. Having created this one, God permits its evils in order to draw from them goods that would otherwise be impossible.

**Rowe's evidential argument.** William Rowe argues that a perfect being would prevent any suffering it could prevent without losing a greater good, and that probably some suffering is like that. His example is a fawn trapped in a lightning-sparked forest fire, burned, suffering for days before it dies. Rowe admits we can't prove that no greater good comes of it. He says only that it is rational to believe none does.

Feser's reply is that this belief is rational only if we have no independent reason to think a good God exists. If we have such a reason, we have reason to think there *must* be a greater good even when we can't see it. The five proofs and the arguments for God's attributes supply that reason. Since Rowe grants that a hidden good is possible, he must grant that independent proof of God makes it likely. So Rowe's argument can't challenge the proofs without first refuting them, and if it could refute them it wouldn't be needed.

Feser adds that our ignorance of God's reasons is just what we should expect. If there is an afterlife (he argues for the soul's immortality elsewhere), then an endless good could outweigh even the worst sufferings of this life, which all come to an end. And we can no more picture that life than a small child can picture adulthood. Children often can't see the point of homework, spinach, or discipline until they grow up, and the gap between this life and the next is far greater. For a defense, the mere *possibility* of such an afterlife is enough.

#### Divine hiddenness

Schellenberg's argument depends on two assumptions: God would make his existence obvious, and he hasn't.

On the first: not all theists think God seeks a relationship with us. Aristotle's Unmoved Mover contemplates itself and ignores us. So the argument fails as a general case for atheism. Even for Jews and Christians, who believe, rightly in Feser's view, that God does seek relationship, it leaves untouched most of what the proofs show: a cause that is one, simple, purely actual, eternal, all-powerful, and all-knowing. That already refutes atheism. On God's love, the reply is the same as with evil. Given proof of a loving God, his not being more obvious must serve a greater good.

Hick's idea of epistemic distance, Feser says, is overstated. The universe isn't religiously ambiguous, and free choice about God doesn't depend on doubt, since plenty of believers have done evil without doubting God. But it contains a kernel of truth: God gives us a long leash in what we think as well as in what we do. Philosophers have denied change, time, causation, free will, the self, even consciousness. Finite minds err, and free wills can look away from evidence.

On the second assumption: most humans throughout history have believed in some divine reality, and most still do, often confidently. If God is hidden from most people, they haven't noticed. That their reasons are unsophisticated doesn't make God hidden, any more than a layman's weak grasp of science makes science hidden from him. Most experts in Western history, from Plato and Aristotle through Augustine, Aquinas, Descartes, Leibniz, and Newton, held that God can be known by argument, and saying their arguments have since been refuted begs the question. Intellectuals who reject God on the basis of incompetent objections like "What caused God?" reveal more about their grasp of natural theology than about God's hiddenness.

#### Arguments for God are just rationalizations

This is **poisoning the well**, an ad hominem fallacy: an argument's quality doesn't depend on the arguer's motives. The charge also cuts both ways. Thomas Nagel admitted he hopes there is no God, and he suspected that this "cosmic authority problem" drives much modern scientism and reductionism. Feser adds that not all atheists are dishonest, and neither are all theists. The only way to judge an argument is to examine it.

#### It can't be a proof if so many people are unconvinced

Feser's three replies:

1. **Much of the rejection is uninformed.** Many criticisms attack caricatures.
2. **There's a double standard.** Arguments for materialism or against free will stay "on the table" despite their critics, while theistic arguments are declared failures because someone objected, sometimes Hume two centuries ago. As Hilary Putnam noted, by the "must convince everyone" standard, the philosophical arguments of Frege, Russell, and Quine would fail too.
3. **"Demonstration" is misunderstood.** It means a valid deduction from premises that are certain. That makes it more secure than a scientific inference: it isn't a probability estimate, and future observation can't overturn its premises. The Aristotelian proof's premise that change occurs is empirical in a sense, but any future experience would itself be a change. We can doubt that a particular change is real, never that change as such is. The other premises are defended metaphysically, for instance by deriving the principle of causality from PSR, which in turn is defended by showing that denying it undermines itself.

Nothing about a demonstration requires that everyone, even a fair and informed reader, will see its force immediately, or that its premises need no hard work to defend. Controversy isn't failure. What a demonstration needs is to survive every objection, and Feser holds that the five proofs do. He closes the book with *quod erat demonstrandum*, "which was to be demonstrated."

## 5. The Main Line of Reasoning, Step by Step

1. Scientism says science is the only genuine source of knowledge. *(Feser's definition.)*
2. That thesis isn't established by science. *(It's a philosophical claim.)*
3. Science rests on presuppositions it can't prove without circularity. *(A real, orderly, knowable world.)*
4. So justifying science requires a standpoint outside it, which refutes scientism. *(From 2 and 3.)*
5. Science also needs philosophy to interpret its results. *(Causation, substance, universals.)*
6. Stretching "science" to include philosophy lets natural theology in. *(Aristotle and Aquinas called it a science.)*
7. So scientism is self-refuting or empty. *(From 4 and 6.)*
8. Physics deliberately attends only to what can be described mathematically. *(The source of its success.)*
9. What a method leaves out by design isn't shown to be absent. *(The airplane engineers.)*
10. Physics gives structure, not intrinsic nature. *(Russell.)*
11. So physics can't describe all of reality, and it implies there is more. *(Structure must be the structure of something.)*
12. Scientific explanation presupposes laws. *(It can't explain what they are or why they hold.)*
13. On every theory of laws, laws can't be the ultimate explanation. *(All four views fail.)*
14. So ultimate explanation lies beyond science, and denying it is philosophy and false. *(PSR.)*
15. The main argument for scientism is science's success. *(Rosenberg.)*
16. That argument is invalid. *(The metal detector.)*
17. "Everything worth knowing" is rigged, and it is itself philosophy. *(The easy-A student.)*
18. "Science or mere conceptual analysis" refutes itself, and logic and mathematics fit neither. *(Hume's Fork.)*
19. So scientism has no good support and faces decisive objections. *(From the above.)*
20. So the proofs must be met on their own terms. *(A false premise, an invalid step, or a begged question.)*
21. Some objections refute themselves or are arbitrary. *(Scientism, Hume's Fork, brute-fact laws.)*
22. Others attack a caricature or use the wrong standard. *(The designer, "one god further," Rowe, Schellenberg, motives, disagreement.)*
23. **Conclusion:** No popular objection overturns the five proofs.

## 6. Every Objection and Its Reply: One-Liners

- **Scientism:** "Only science gives knowledge" isn't something science discovered.
- **The argument from success:** A metal detector's perfect record doesn't prove only metal exists.
- **"You trust your cardiologist":** Trusting a tool for its job isn't believing it's the only tool.
- **"Everything worth knowing":** Grading science only on questions it can answer guarantees a perfect score.
- **"Mere conceptual analysis":** The dichotomy falls into the very gap it says doesn't exist.
- **Laws as ultimate explanation:** Laws describe how things behave. They can't make there be anything to behave.
- **Brute-fact laws:** If the bottom law explains nothing, nothing resting on it explains anything.
- **The complex designer:** Agreed, a complex cause needs a cause, which is why the proofs end in something with no parts.
- **One god further:** Zeus is a character in the world. God is the reason there's a world.
- **The philosophers' God:** Atheism is refuted either way, and it's one God known in two ways.
- **Logical problem of evil:** A world with nothing to forgive, fear, or pity has no forgiveness, courage, or compassion.
- **Evidential problem of evil:** Not seeing God's reasons isn't evidence there are none when you have independent proof of his goodness.
- **Hiddenness:** If God is hidden from most people, most people haven't noticed.
- **Rationalization:** Why someone argues tells you nothing about whether the argument is sound.
- **Disagreement:** Every serious argument has critics. The question is whether their objections work.

### Answering suffering humanely

The intellectual problem of evil and the experience of suffering are different things. Feser offers a *defense*: he does not claim to know why any particular evil happens. With someone who is grieving, start there. Listen. Acknowledge that the evil is real; Feser holds that evil is a genuine lack of what ought to be, not an illusion. Say honestly, "I don't know why this happened." Only if they want to go further, share gently that belief in God doesn't depend on explaining this event, that mercy, courage, and compassion exist only where there is suffering, and that if God is good, this isn't the last word. Never say "everything happens for a reason."

## 7. Common Misunderstandings

- **"Rejecting scientism means rejecting science."** Feser praises physics. He rejects only the claim that science is the only source of knowledge.
- **"Feser claims to know why God allows each evil."** He offers a defense, not a theodicy.
- **"Feser says this is the best possible world."** He explicitly denies that.
- **"The proofs are design arguments."** They don't rely on complexity or probability.
- **"If physics doesn't mention something, it isn't real."** Physics leaves things out by design.
- **"God is hidden because he doesn't care about us."** Feser uses Aristotle's aloof God only to show the argument isn't fully general. He holds that God does seek relationship with us.
- **"Quoting Nagel proves atheists are dishonest."** Feser denies that. Motives cut both ways.
- **"A real proof convinces everyone."** No philosophical argument meets that standard.

## 8. How to Explain It to Others

**Simple version:** "Most objections to God rest on the assumption that only science gives knowledge. But science could never prove that, so the assumption undercuts itself. Science is brilliant at the measurable and leaves the rest out on purpose. Without that assumption, the popular objections either picture God as one more complex being, like Zeus, instead of the source of all being, or they judge careful arguments by the wrong standards."

**Best analogy: the metal detector.** It finds metal better than anything else, but if it doesn't beep at your sandwich, your sandwich is still real. Science is a superb instrument aimed at the measurable. Its success shows its power there, not that there's nothing else.

**Second analogy: triangle and triangularity.** Rejecting Zeus and rejecting God differ the way calling a chalk triangle imperfect differs from calling triangularity itself imperfect.

**Tips:** Praise science. Sort each objection: does it refute itself, attack a caricature, or use the wrong standard? Grant what's true in it. On suffering, listen first. Always invite the other person to name the premise they think fails.

## 9. Key Takeaways and Review Questions

### Key takeaways

- Scientism is either self-refuting or empty.
- Physics captures structure by design, so its silence isn't denial.
- Laws can't be the ultimate explanation, and brute-fact laws explain nothing.
- The proofs conclude to an absolutely simple cause, so "Who designed the designer?" misses.
- "One god further" is a category mistake.
- Evil is compatible with God, and unseen reasons are still reasons given independent proof.
- Hiddenness rests on two doubtful assumptions.
- Motives and disagreement don't refute arguments. A demonstration must survive every objection, not convince everyone.

### Ten review questions with brief answers

1. **What is the dilemma for scientism?** It is either self-refuting (not scientific) or empty (it lets natural theology in).
2. **What do the airplane engineers show?** A method's silence can come from its design rather than from reality.
3. **What did Feser take from Russell?** Physics gives only structure, which implies there is more to reality.
4. **Why don't Humean laws explain?** They say *that* a pattern holds, not *why*.
5. **What's wrong with the argument from success?** It has the same form as the metal-detector argument.
6. **Why does Hume's Fork refute itself?** It is neither conceptual nor empirical.
7. **What is the key reply to the complex designer?** The proofs conclude to an absolutely simple cause.
8. **How does Feser answer Mackie's free-will point?** He grants it but notes that forgiveness, mercy, courage, and compassion require evil.
9. **Why doesn't Rowe's fawn refute the proofs?** Independent proof of a good God gives reason to believe in unseen goods.
10. **What is a demonstration?** A valid deduction from certain premises. Disagreement doesn't defeat one; only a successful objection does.
